### Screen readers × streaming chat
FINDINGS:
  - Live-region change events are queued one-per-mutation, and the only coalescing behaviour ever specified is for aria-atomic="true" regions. A streaming chat message (role="log" or aria-live="polite" with the default aria-atomic="false") therefore generates an unbounded, un-deduplicated queue of announcement events — one per token flush — with no spec-defined mechanism to collapse them.
    (ARIA Screen Reader Implementors Guide — MDN Web Docs, 2025 (living doc) — https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/Accessibility/ARIA/Guides/Screen_Reader_Implementors; confidence high)
  - role="log" — the WCAG-sanctioned chat pattern — was designed for discrete messages appended to a history, not for a single message whose text mutates continuously. Its implicit values are aria-live="polite" and aria-atomic="false", i.e. exactly the configuration that gets no coalescing.
    (ARIA23: Using role=log to identify sequential information updates (WCAG 2.1 Techniques), 2023 — https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Techniques/aria/ARIA23; confidence high)
  - You cannot engineer your way out of the flood with aria-busy / aria-atomic / aria-relevant: support for all three is inconsistent, and aria-busy in particular is ignored by most screen readers other than JAWS, which read the "busy" region's contents anyway.
    (Accessible notifications with ARIA Live Regions (Part 1) — Sara Soueidan, 2024 — https://www.sarasoueidan.com/blog/accessible-notifications-with-aria-live-regions-part-1/; confidence high)
  - Live regions only work if the container is in the accessibility tree and EMPTY before content is injected. Mounting a live region together with its content — the natural React/Svelte pattern for "render the assistant message with aria-live" — silently announces nothing.
    (Why are my live regions not working? — TetraLogical, 2024 — https://tetralogical.com/blog/2024/05/01/why-are-my-live-regions-not-working/; confidence high)
  - The best-evidenced production pattern is to DECOUPLE announcement from content: a separate visually-hidden live region, outside the visible transcript, that receives a short summarised string, is transiently populated (~100ms) and then cleared — while the visible transcript stays a plain navigable region with no live semantics.
    ([Accessibility] Put aria-live region outside of transcript · Issue #3236 · microsoft/BotFramework-WebChat, 2020 — https://github.com/microsoft/BotFramework-WebChat/issues/3236; confidence high)
  - Even in 2026, a polite live region is not a reliable delivery channel: VoiceOver on macOS does not announce polite regions during read-all, yet treats them assertively in Braille where it destroys the braille line the user was reading. Live-region announcement is therefore a best-effort channel, never the only path to the content.
    (Live Region Support — Adrian Roselli, 2026 — https://adrianroselli.com/2026/01/live-region-support.html; confidence high)
  - Shipping two live regions (e.g. one for "generating…" status and one for the message text) is unsafe: the ARIA spec is silent on simultaneous updates to multiple live regions, and in practice only one gets announced.
    (Clarify support or non-support for multiple aria-live regions on the same page · Issue #1689 · w3c/aria, 2021 (open) — https://github.com/w3c/aria/issues/1689; confidence medium)
  - ariaNotify() is now a real, shipping alternative to live regions for imperative announcements (Chrome 141+, Firefox 150+, NVDA and TalkBack confirmed working), but WebKit has not implemented it — iOS VoiceOver announces nothing — so it can only be a progressive enhancement layered on top of a live-region fallback.
    (How good is Browser Support for the ARIA Notify API? — oidaisdes.org, 2026 — https://oidaisdes.org/blog/aria-notify-browser-support/; confidence high)
  - ariaNotify solves reliability but not usability: it is speech-only, ephemeral, has exactly two priority levels, and gives the user no control or opt-out — accessibility practitioners are actively warning it will be abused to narrate things users already understand.
    (The Siren Song of ariaNotify() — CSS-Tricks, 2026 — https://css-tricks.com/the-siren-song-of-arianotify/; confidence high)
  - The dominant real-world failure reported by blind users of ChatGPT and Claude is not the live region at all — it is that AI responses are structurally unnavigable (no headings, regions or landmarks, no shortcut to jump prompt-to-prompt) and that nothing tells the user when generation has finished.
    ("I look at it as the king of knowledge": How Blind People Use and Understand Generative AI Tools (ASSETS '24), 2024 — https://maitraye.github.io/files/papers/BLV_GenAI_ASSETS24.pdf; confidence high)
  - Structure should be exposed as HEADINGS per message, not landmarks: headings are how ~72% of screen-reader users find information on a page, whereas landmarks are the primary method for only 3.7%.
    (WebAIM: Screen Reader User Survey #10 Results, 2024 — https://webaim.org/projects/screenreadersurvey10/; confidence high)
  - A chat/Q&A interface measurably degrades blind users' comprehension of a body of information relative to a navigable document interface — while users subjectively prefer the chat. Satisfaction is an actively misleading success metric for this population.
    (Q&A or Document-Based? The Effects of Interface Type on How Screen Reader Users Access Interconnected Documents (ASSETS '26 preprint), 2026 — https://arxiv.org/html/2608.25382v1; confidence medium)
  - Blind users of conversational AI are structurally on the losing end of an input/output asymmetry — they type ~10× less than they must listen to — and current systems ship no verbosity control, forcing users to invent prompt-engineering workarounds.
    (Say It My Way: Exploring Control in Conversational Visual Question Answering with Blind Users (CHI '26), 2026 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16930; confidence high)
  - Markdown/code output read at default screen-reader verbosity is ambiguous or silently lossy: NVDA drops backticks, brackets, braces, dashes, ellipses and tildes; parentheses are spoken only by JAWS. Code fences, inline code and nested structure therefore do not survive being read aloud.
    (How screen readers read special characters: an update — Eleven Ways, 2023 — https://www.elevenways.be/en/articles/screenreaders-special-characters; confidence high)
  - Math in AI answers is rendered by KaTeX's hidden-MathML approach, which is a known-broken accessibility strategy that MathJax itself abandoned; MathJax 4 now generates speech strings into aria-label/aria-braillelabel instead so screen readers need not understand MathML at all.
    (Accessibility Features — MathJax 4.0 documentation; KaTeX issue #820, 2025 — https://docs.mathjax.org/en/latest/basic/accessibility.html; confidence medium)
  - A "generating…" / "response complete" cue is not a nicety — it is a WCAG 2.2 AA conformance obligation under SC 4.1.3 Status Messages, which almost no AI chat product satisfies. Meanwhile the most widely used AI chat UI SDK ships no accessibility guidance at all in its chatbot documentation.
    (Understanding SC 4.1.3: Status Messages (WCAG 2.2), 2023 — https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/status-messages.html; confidence high)
  - W3C already has a published requirements document for exactly this problem space — Natural Language Interface Accessibility User Requirements (NAUR) — with numbered requirements covering repeat-on-request, synchronized text alongside speech, configurable rate/pitch/volume, and multiple output devices. It is a Draft Note that no shipping LLM chat product cites.
    (Natural Language Interface Accessibility User Requirements (NAUR) — W3C, 2022 — https://www.w3.org/TR/naur/; confidence high)
  - Focus after send should move to the new message (a programmatically focusable heading/container with tabindex="-1"), not stay in or bounce back to the composer; and when the chat is not focused, notification must be multi-modal (sound + title change + live region), not live-region-only.
    (Best practices for a chatbot — Orange digital accessibility guidelines; Web Chat accessibility considerations — Craig Abbott, 2018 / 2023 — https://a11y-guidelines.orange.com/en/articles/chatbot/; confidence medium)
  - Open-source LLM chat UIs are in the same state or worse, and blind users are explicitly asking for message-boundary structure — with heading-based message separation named as the desired pattern.
    (HuggingChat chat-ui — accessibility discussion #653, 2025 — https://huggingface.co/spaces/huggingchat/chat-ui/discussions/653; confidence medium)
  - Government teams building production LLM chat have not published streaming/screen-reader guidance either — the GOV.UK Chat write-ups discuss text sizing, contrast and character-limit announcements but contain no live-region, streaming-announcement or AT-user research findings. There is no authoritative public pattern to copy.
    (Designing GOV.UK Chat for the GOV.UK app — Inside GOV.UK; alphagov/govuk-design-system discussion #3804, 2026 — https://insidegovuk.blog.gov.uk/2026/08/19/designing-gov-uk-chat-for-the-gov-uk-app/; confidence high)
DESIGN IMPLICATIONS:
  - Separate the ANNOUNCEMENT channel from the CONTENT channel. The visible transcript must carry zero live semantics (no aria-live, no role=log on the streaming message). A single, app-root-mounted, visually-hidden, primed-empty live region — created once, before any content — carries short status strings only. This is the only pattern with production evidence behind it (BotFramework-WebChat #3236) and it dodges the per-mutation queue flood entirely.
  - Never announce the streaming text itself by default. Announce state transitions: "Generating…" on send (debounced ~500ms so fast answers don't chatter), then "Response ready, 340 words, 3 headings, 1 code block, 1 table" on completion. That completion cue is the single highest-value fix in the whole dimension — it is the exact gap ASSETS '24 documented ("dig for it") and it is a WCAG 4.1.3 AA obligation, not a nicety.
  - Ship an explicit, user-facing announcement-mode control with at least four settings, persisted in the portable accessibility profile: (a) silent — status only, (b) completion summary (default), (c) progressive by sentence/paragraph boundary — flush a buffer only at a sentence terminator or block boundary, never per token, (d) full streaming for users who genuinely want it. Do not bury this in a settings page; expose it in the chat toolbar. Evidence for the need is strong (10× input/output asymmetry, no verbosity controls in shipping systems); evidence for which default is best is absent, so make it switchable and instrument it.
  - Emit a heading per turn, not a landmark per turn. Render each assistant/user turn with a real heading element (e.g. h2 "You said" / h2 "Assistant replied, 12:04") because 71.6% of screen-reader users navigate by heading and only 3.7% primarily by landmark. Sub-headings inside a long answer (from the model's own markdown h2/h3) then give free 'read from here' jump points via H/1/2/3 keys — no custom keybinding needed, and no shortcut conflicts with the screen reader.
  - Provide explicit message-boundary keyboard commands anyway, and document them in an on-page keyboard-help dialog: previous/next turn, jump to latest turn, jump to composer, jump to first/last. Users are currently abusing the "next graphic" (G) shortcut against avatar icons to fake this. Bind them so they work in browse mode without colliding with NVDA/JAWS single-letter navigation (use modifier chords, not bare letters).
  - After send, move focus to the newly created assistant turn container (heading with tabindex="-1"), not back to the composer and not to an auto-scrolled bottom. Never auto-scroll or auto-focus during streaming: that steals the virtual cursor from a user who is reading earlier text. Return-to-composer must be a single documented key.
  - Treat ariaNotify() as progressive enhancement only, feature-detected, with the hidden-live-region path as the always-present fallback: it works with NVDA on Chrome/Edge/Firefox and TalkBack on Android as of June 2026, but WebKit/iOS VoiceOver announces nothing, and 70.6% of the survey's mobile screen-reader users are on iOS. Anything ariaNotify says must ALSO exist as navigable on-page text, because ariaNotify output is ephemeral, speech-only and unreviewable.
  - Do not build the design on aria-busy, aria-atomic or aria-relevant. Support is undocumented or inconsistent, and aria-busy is read through by everything except JAWS. Any design whose correctness depends on one of these attributes will fail for most users.
  - Use exactly ONE live region for the whole component. The spec is silent on simultaneous multi-region updates and in practice only one is announced — so a separate "status" region plus a separate "message" region will silently drop announcements.
  - Post-process the model's markdown for aural rendering, and expose that as a switchable 'aural view' of the same answer: strip or name decorative emoji; convert inline code to a spoken-safe form; give code blocks an accessible name and a summary ("Python code block, 24 lines") with the code itself in a labelled, focusable region users opt into rather than fall into; render tables as real HTML tables with th/scope (never as ASCII art in a code fence); and expand nested-list nesting depth into speech. Punctuation-critical content cannot rely on default verbosity — NVDA silently drops backticks, brackets, braces, dashes and ellipses.
  - For math, do not ship KaTeX hidden-MathML and call it accessible. Follow MathJax 4: generate a speech string and attach it via aria-label plus aria-braillelabel so the announcement works regardless of the screen reader's MathML support, and keep the LaTeX source available as copyable text.
  - Design for braille, not only speech. Eight of nineteen participants in the ASSETS '24 study used braille displays, and VoiceOver treats polite regions assertively in braille, destroying the line being read. A per-token or per-sentence live update is far more destructive on a 40-cell display than in speech. This is another argument for completion-only announcement as the default.
  - Make 'stop generating', 'repeat last response', and 'read from the start of this answer' first-class keyboard actions. W3C NAUR REQ 19a already requires repeat-on-request; nothing ships it. Repeat must re-render text a screen reader can re-read, not trigger a separate TTS voice.
  - Never auto-play the product's own text-to-speech over a screen reader. Two voices talking is a regression, not a feature; NAUR REQ 7 asks for spoken output accompanied by a synchronized transcript, so TTS must be opt-in, pausable and mutually exclusive with the live-region path.
  - Validate the component with task-based comprehension measures, not satisfaction. Blind participants performed measurably worse with a chat interface (40% fewer main topics, double the error rate) while rating it better for learning — so a component library that ships only a satisfaction study is shipping an unmeasured regression.
  - Publish the layer with a conformance story attached: map each behaviour to SC 4.1.3 (status messages), 1.3.1 (info and relationships, for speaker identification and turn structure), 2.4.11 (focus not obscured) and the NAUR requirement numbers. For model operators the compliance hook is the adoption argument — the AI SDK docs currently contain no accessibility guidance at all, so there is no incumbent to displace.
PITFALLS:
  - Putting aria-live="polite" on the visible streaming message container. This is the single most repeated piece of advice in AI-chat accessibility blog posts and it is the primary cause of the failure: every token flush queues a change event, and there is no specified coalescing for non-atomic regions. Depending on the screen reader you get token-by-token stutter, whole-message re-announcement, or nothing.
  - Believing that aria-atomic="false" plus aria-relevant="additions text" makes screen readers announce 'only the new tokens'. Support for both attributes is inconsistent and untested in the current published matrices, and markdown-rendering frameworks replace whole text nodes on re-render rather than appending, so the 'addition' is the entire message again.
  - Wrapping the stream in aria-busy="true" and clearing it at the end. Most screen readers other than JAWS read the busy region's content anyway; and where it does work you get one uninterruptible several-minute announcement with no way to navigate inside it.
  - Using aria-live="assertive" (or role="alert") for the 'response is ready' cue. Assertive interrupts whatever the user is reading — including the previous answer they are still working through — and role="alert" is not consistently prefixed or even announced (Orca ignores it; NVDA sends only the word 'alert' to braille).
  - Mounting the live region at the same moment as the message it should announce. A pre-populated live region announces nothing; this is the most common silent failure and it looks correct in code review.
  - Running two live regions (status + content). The spec does not define simultaneous behaviour and in practice one is dropped, so the announcement you consider critical is the one that disappears.
  - Auto-scrolling to the newest content and/or moving focus during streaming. It yanks the virtual cursor away from a user reading earlier text and produces exactly the 'unexpected screen changes' that rank in WebAIM's top five most problematic items.
  - Adding a landmark region per message. It clutters the landmark list for the 3.7% who navigate that way and does nothing for the 71.6% who navigate by heading.
  - Treating ariaNotify() as the fix. It has no iOS/WebKit support, produces ephemeral speech that cannot be reviewed or brailled, offers no user opt-out, and practitioners are already warning it will be abused into a narration flood.
  - Emoji and decorative markdown in model output. Screen readers speak emoji names, so a heading garnished with emoji becomes noise, and repeated emoji can cause users to abandon the message entirely.
  - Rendering tables as ASCII art inside code fences, or math as KaTeX hidden MathML. Both look fine visually and are unusable aurally; the KaTeX iOS VoiceOver bug has been open since 2017.
  - Adding a product 'read aloud' TTS button as the accessibility feature. Auto-playing it puts a second voice on top of the screen reader; blind users generally already have speech and want structure and control, not more audio.
  - Shipping the layer validated on user satisfaction. Blind participants rated a Q&A interface higher for learning while building mental models with 40% fewer topics and twice the errors — subjective preference actively conceals the comprehension loss.
  - Assuming the interface is fine because the output is plain text. The ASSETS '24 participants describe text-based GenAI tools as only 'technically' accessible while being unnavigable in practice; the barriers are structure, notification and control, not text rendering.
  - Announcing the same string twice in a row. Screen readers commonly suppress an identical repeat, so a naive 'Response ready' after every turn can be silently swallowed — the live region must be cleared between announcements or the string varied.
OPEN QUESTIONS:
  - I found NO published empirical study that compares streaming announcement strategies (per-token vs sentence-boundary chunking vs completion-only vs earcon-only) with blind users and measures comprehension or preference. Every recommendation in the practitioner literature — including 'buffer to sentence boundaries' — is reasoned from first principles, not measured. This is the single largest evidence gap in this dimension and a genuine research contribution the project could make.
  - There is no published support matrix for aria-atomic, aria-relevant or aria-busy across current screen readers. Adrian Roselli's January 2026 test explicitly does not cover them; Sara Soueidan says they are unreliable but cites older work. Any design leaning on them is unvalidated. Building and publishing that matrix would be cheap and high-value.
  - What ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Copilot actually ship TODAY is contested and moving. ASSETS '24 (interviews conducted before Oct 2024) found no headings/landmarks in ChatGPT or Claude; a HuggingChat commenter in March 2025 describes ChatGPT as already using heading-based message separation. I could not verify the current DOM first-hand (the products require authentication). A fresh, dated, first-hand audit of the four major web UIs with NVDA + JAWS + VoiceOver is needed before making competitive claims.
  - I could not verify whether OpenAI, Anthropic or Google publish a VPAT/ACR for their consumer chat UIs. A search summary asserted OpenAI has not, but I did not retrieve a primary source, so I am not asserting it.
  - JAWS behaviour with ariaNotify() is untested in the only public support survey I found (which covered NVDA and TalkBack only). Given JAWS is primary for 40.5% of survey respondents, this gap matters for the fallback strategy.
  - Braille-display behaviour under streaming output is essentially undocumented. Roselli's note that VoiceOver treats polite regions assertively in braille and loses content is the only hard datum I found. Given ~42% of the ASSETS '24 sample used braille displays, streaming's effect on a refreshable display deserves direct testing.
  - Screen-reader behaviour in mobile NATIVE AI apps (iOS VoiceOver / Android TalkBack against UIKit/Compose, not the web) is not covered by any source I retrieved, yet 49.5% of WebAIM respondents use mobile as much as desktop and 58% prefer apps over websites for common tasks. A portable layer aimed at 'desktop apps, mobile apps, agent harnesses' has essentially no evidence base outside the browser.
  - There is no evidence on how streaming announcements should behave for AGENT harnesses — multi-step tool calls, interleaved reasoning, and progress updates — which produce far more state transitions than a single streamed answer. Nothing I found addresses this at all.
  - How much of the answer should the completion summary carry? 'Response ready' vs 'Response ready, 340 words, 2 code blocks' vs an actual one-sentence gist. The Q&A-vs-document study suggests users struggle to judge completeness, which argues for a structural summary, but I found no study testing summary formats aurally.
  - Whether users want the answer's structure announced before the text (a 'table of contents' preamble) is untested; it is a plausible mitigation for the 'didn't know what to ask / am I getting the full answer' problem but is my inference, not a finding.
MISSED ANGLE (per verifier): The pass never asks whether streaming should be delivered as streaming at all for AT users, and never covers the failure mode that is independent of announcement: DOM mutation destroying the reading position. Every claim is about how to announce a growing message; none considers a "render on complete" / stop-the-stream preference, or WCAG 2.2.2 Pause, Stop, Hide, which plausibly applies to auto-updating content and would make an author-provided way to halt the update the normative baseline rather than a nice-to-have. Underneath that sits an unexamined mechanism: JAWS and NVDA browse mode read from a virtual buffer that is rebuilt as the DOM mutates, so a user arrow-keying through a response that is still growing loses their place regardless of what any live region does — the ASSETS participants describe exactly this as "hide and seek... because sometimes the page scrolled" (Frank) and by copying responses out to a notepad (Kevin), and no cited source measures virtual-buffer refresh under token-level mutation. Two adjacent gaps compound it: braille is treated as a footnote although eight of the study's nineteen participants used displays and Roselli's braille findings are the most severe in his matrix (VoiceOver destroying the braille line, NVDA showing "alert" with no message) — braille is a persistent, re-readable channel with different failure modes than speech, and a speech-first design will not serve it. And there is no compliance layer at all: no EN 301 549 / European Accessibility Act obligations (applicable since 28 June 2025), no WCAG 2.2 mapping beyond SC 4.1.3, and no consideration of non-screen-reader AT such as voice control or switch access, for which a continuously mutating target is its own distinct problem.

### Low vision (the user's core interest)
FINDINGS:
  - Low vision is a large, highly heterogeneous population that uses several assistive technologies at once — there is no single 'accessible mode' that serves it. [verified: confirmed]
    (W3C: Accessibility Requirements for People with Low Vision (FPWD) / WHO Blindness and vision impairment fact sheet / WebAIM Survey of Users with Low Vision #2, 2016 / 2026 / 2018 — https://www.w3.org/TR/low-vision-needs/; confidence high)
  - Real screen-magnifier users work at 8x–45x, an order of magnitude beyond WCAG's 400% reflow ceiling — so 320px reflow is a floor to clear, not the design target. [verified: confirmed]
    (SteeringWheel: A Locality-Preserving Magnification Interface for Low Vision Web Browsing (CHI '18), 2018 — https://oscarlab.github.io/papers/chi18-billah.pdf; confidence high)
  - Dynamically changing content is the documented catastrophic failure mode for magnifier users — they physically cannot pan fast enough to keep up. This is exactly what token-by-token LLM streaming is.
    (Towards Making Videos Accessible for Low Vision Screen Magnifier Users (IUI '20), 2020 — https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10186664; confidence high)
  - The dominant cost in magnified GUIs is panning between spatially separated regions — which is precisely the chat-UI layout (answer above, composer below, per-message actions elsewhere, sidebar to the side).
    (Bringing Things Closer: Enhancing Low-Vision Interaction Experience with Office Productivity Applications (PACM HCI) / Quantifying the Cost of Manual Navigation (arXiv), 2021 / 2026 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8871469/; confidence high)
  - Line switching is the hardest single reading operation under magnification, and low vision readers are roughly 3x slower than sighted readers — so measure, leading and streaming pace matter more than font family. [verified: overstated → Line switching is a measurable, specific difficulty under magnification — low-vision readers spend significantly longer fixation time at the first 10% of each line, and a narrow magnification window correlates with slower reading (r(26) = −0.67, p < 0.001). Prior work (Bruggeman & Legge 2002, cited by the CHI '23 paper, not measured by it) puts low-vision text-block reading at roughly 3x slower. This supports treating measure and leading as first-order variables; it does not establish that line switching is the single hardest operation, and it says nothing at all about font family or streaming pace.]
    (Understanding How Low Vision People Read Using Eye Tracking (CHI '23), 2023 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.16346; confidence high)
  - WCAG gives a precise, implementable numeric baseline for a low-vision layer; the AAA criterion 1.4.8 is effectively a spec for the component being proposed. [verified: confirmed]
    (W3C Understanding WCAG 2.2 — Reflow, Text Spacing, Non-text Contrast, Target Size, Focus Not Obscured, Focus Appearance, Visual Presentation, 2023 — https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/visual-presentation.html; confidence high)
  - Code blocks, tables and diagrams — the bulk of what AI assistants emit — are an explicit, legitimate exception to reflow, so 'we're responsive, we're fine' is false for AI output.
    (Understanding SC 1.4.10: Reflow, 2023 — https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/reflow.html; confidence high)
  - On the web, in-app font-size widgets are largely redundant with OS/browser tooling that users already prefer — but in desktop/Electron apps the opposite is true, because Windows text scaling does not reach them. [verified: overstated → The web half stands as written. The desktop half should be softened: Windows text scaling reaches desktop apps inconsistently rather than not at all. Microsoft's own docs cover WinUI (supported by default), DirectWrite, GDI, XAML SwapChainPanels and Win32 — they say nothing about Electron or Chromium. Chromium in fact implements Windows text-scale-factor handling and scales web contents by the system font-scaling value; CEF and Electron developers have filed threads asking how to *disable* it. Electron apps also ship their own Ctrl +/− zoom. The accurate statement is that text-scale support in desktop apps is inconsistent and non-uniform, which is why an in-app control earns its place there — not that Windows text scaling fails to reach them.]
    (WebAIM Survey of Users with Low Vision #2 / Overlay Fact Sheet / W3C Understanding SC 1.4.4 / Microsoft Learn: Text scaling, 2018 / 2024 / 2023 / 2022 — https://webaim.org/projects/lowvisionsurvey2/; confidence high)
  - Windows/OS forced-colors mode keys off NATIVE element semantics, not ARIA roles — so an accessibility layer built from divs with role attributes silently loses high-contrast support.
    (MDN: forced-colors and prefers-contrast CSS media features, 2026 — https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/Reference/At-rules/@media/forced-colors; confidence high)
  - Dark mode is not universally better; polarity preference splits by eye condition, and the evidence favouring dark mode for low vision is old and display-confounded.
    (NN/g: Dark Mode vs. Light Mode: Which Is Better? / WebAIM Survey of Users with Low Vision #2, 2020 / 2018 — https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dark-mode/; confidence medium)
  - The widely repeated 'dark mode causes halation for people with astigmatism' claim is folklore in accessibility guidance — it is plausible optically but I could not locate a primary peer-reviewed study, and the usual citation is not a published paper.
    (Level Access: Astigmatism and Web Accessibility, 2024 — https://www.levelaccess.com/blog/accessibility-for-people-with-astigmatism/; confidence low)
  - OpenDyslexic has no measured benefit — a 'dyslexia font' toggle is cargo cult, and shipping one as a headline feature would misallocate the layer's credibility. [verified: confirmed]
    (The effect of a specialized dyslexia font, OpenDyslexic, on reading rate and accuracy (Annals of Dyslexia), 2016 — https://doi.org/10.1007/s11881-016-0127-1; confidence high)
  - Serif vs sans-serif is genuinely unresolved for low vision; size, letter spacing and crowding have far better evidence. Font family should be a preference, not a prescription.
    (The Legibility of Typefaces for Readers with Low Vision: A Research Review (JVIB), 2007 — https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ772089.pdf; confidence high)
  - Colour vision deficiency affects roughly 8% of men and 0.5% of women, and the specific AI-UI risk surface is red/green coding in diffs, syntax highlighting and generated charts — GitHub has already shipped the fix pattern.
    (W3C Accessibility Requirements for People with Low Vision / GitHub Changelog and roadmap: Protanopia & Deuteranopia colorblind themes, 2016 / 2022 — https://github.blog/changelog/2022-04-19-protanopia-deuteranopia-colorblind-themes-beta/; confidence high)
  - The user's premise checks out empirically: AI products ship essentially nothing for low vision, and even the AI stack itself collapses under magnification.
    (A11y-CUA Dataset: Characterizing the Accessibility Gap in Computer Use Agents (CHI '26) / Screen Reader Experience Analysis on claude.ai / OpenAI Developer Community: Increasing font size of ChatGPT macOS app / anthropics/claude-code issue 11002, 2026 / 2026 / 2024 / 2025 — https://guoanhong.com/papers/CHI26-A11y-CUA.pdf; confidence high)
  - A portable accessibility profile is not a new idea — ISO/IEC 24751 AccessForAll standardised it in 2008 and it has barely been implemented. Clara should map to it rather than invent a rival schema, and should not assume portability alone drives adoption.
    (ISO/IEC-24751 (AccessForAll), in Professional Web Accessibility Auditing Made Easy (Toronto Metropolitan University Pressbooks), 2008 / 2023 — https://pressbooks.library.torontomu.ca/pwaa/chapter/iso-iec-24751-accessforall/; confidence medium)
  - Streaming LLM output legally and practically requires a pause/stop control, and WCAG gives auto-updating content no five-second grace period.
    (Understanding SC 2.2.2: Pause, Stop, Hide, 2023 — https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/pause-stop-hide.html; confidence high)
DESIGN IMPLICATIONS:
  - TREAT STREAMING AS THE CORE ACCESSIBILITY PROBLEM, NOT TYPOGRAPHY. The single strongest evidence-backed differentiator for a low-vision layer in LLM UIs is a delivery-mode control on the token stream: (a) 'settle and reveal' — buffer the response and paint it once, complete; (b) paragraph-at-a-time reveal; (c) raw stream. Justification: magnifier users 'do not have the luxury of time to manually pan ... before the content changes' (Aydin et al., IUI '20), and SC 2.2.2 requires a mechanism to pause, stop or control the frequency of auto-updating content with no five-second grace. No AI product ships this. It is cheap to implement and nobody else has it.
  - NEVER AUTO-SCROLL A MAGNIFIED VIEWPORT. Auto-scroll-to-follow-the-stream is the default in every chat UI and is the worst possible behaviour at 8x–45x: the CHI '23 eye-tracking work shows a small window displacement makes 'a whole line disappear'. Default to pinning the reading position at the top of the newest message and providing an explicit 'jump to latest' control; anchor point of regard on every content insertion.
  - COLLAPSE SPATIAL SEPARATION. Put the composer, the streaming answer, the stop-generation control and the per-message actions (copy/regenerate/cite) into one narrow column that fits inside a single magnifier viewport, or float them adjacent to the current reading position (the MagPro pattern from Lee et al., 2021). Do not scatter controls into a top bar, a right rail and a hover-revealed toolbar; that is the ribbon problem that made all 10 participants in that study lose their place.
  - SHIP SC 1.4.8 AS THE COMPONENT'S FEATURE LIST. User-selectable foreground/background from a full palette; measure capped at 45–80 characters (40 CJK); never justified text; line-height >= 1.5 with paragraph spacing >= 1.5x that; 200% resize with no horizontal scroll. Express measure in ch and all sizing in rem/em so it tracks text scaling — a max-width in px silently defeats the whole thing.
  - MEET THE NUMBERS EXPLICITLY AND TEST BEYOND THEM. 4.5:1 body text (7:1 as an opt-in AAA theme), 3:1 for every icon button, border, focus ring and chart element; 24x24 CSS px minimum targets (44x44 for message actions); reflow at 320 CSS px; focus indicator >= a 2px perimeter at 3:1 against both states, and never obscured by a sticky composer (SC 2.4.11). Then also test at 8x and 16x lens magnification and at 400% browser zoom, because that is where real users live.
  - READ THE OS FIRST, OVERRIDE SECOND. Wire up prefers-color-scheme, prefers-contrast (more/less/custom), forced-colors, prefers-reduced-motion, and on mobile iOS Dynamic Type / Android font scale, before offering any in-app control. In-app controls should be labelled and behave as overrides of a detected OS state, and should never fire on first load in a way that contradicts the OS.
  - BUILD FROM NATIVE SEMANTIC ELEMENTS. Real <button>, <input>, <a>, <table>. Forced-colors mode assigns system colours by native semantics and explicitly ignores ARIA roles, so a div+role component set loses high-contrast support silently. Correspondingly, never encode meaning in box-shadow, text-shadow or background-image — all three are forced to none.
  - TWO FIRST-CLASS POLARITIES PLUS A SOFTENED VARIANT. Ship light-on-dark and dark-on-light as equal citizens (71.2%/25.6% split in WebAIM), plus a low-brightness/sepia option for photophobia and a near-black rather than #000 dark theme. Do not make dark mode the 'accessible' one.
  - MAKE SIZE, LINE-HEIGHT, LETTER-SPACING AND MEASURE THE PRIMARY KNOBS; FONT FAMILY A SECONDARY PREFERENCE. Offer a small curated family list (a humanist sans, a serif, a high-legibility face, the user's system UI font) and let people choose, but do not claim a font fixes dyslexia. Default to >= 16px effective body size and generous spacing at or above the 1.4.12 metrics rather than merely tolerating them.
  - NON-COLOUR REDUNDANCY IN EVERYTHING THE MODEL EMITS. Diffs need +/- glyphs and shape/underline, not just red/green; syntax themes need a protanopia/deuteranopia variant (orange/blue rather than red/green, per GitHub) and a separate tritanopia variant; generated charts need direct labels and pattern/shape encoding, not legend-by-colour.
  - HANDLE CODE BLOCKS AND TABLES AS THE DECLARED REFLOW EXCEPTION THEY ARE. Give each one its own horizontally scrollable container with a visible, keyboard-reachable scrollbar, a soft-wrap toggle, a per-block zoom, and a copy button — so the surrounding prose still reflows to 320px while the code does not have to.
  - PUBLISH THE PROFILE AS AN ISO/IEC 24751 PNP PROFILE, NOT A NEW SCHEMA. Express Clara's portable JSON accessibility profile as functional needs (magnification factor, polarity, contrast level, measure, spacing, motion tolerance, delivery pace) mapped onto AccessForAll PNP vocabulary, with an explicit crosswalk table. This gets standards credibility for free and is exactly the model regulators and procurement (EN 301 549 / EAA, enforced since 28 June 2025) recognise.
  - KEEP PROFILE STORAGE LOCAL AND EXPLICIT. The Overlay Fact Sheet's privacy criticism — overlays that detect assistive technology and persist settings via cookies, exposing disability status — applies directly to a portable profile. Local-first storage, explicit user-initiated export/import, no AT sniffing, no telemetry on accessibility settings.
  - TARGET THE DESKTOP/ELECTRON GAP DELIBERATELY. Browser zoom already discharges SC 1.4.4 on the web, so an in-app text-size control there is low value. In Electron and native desktop AI clients the Windows text-scaling slider does not reach the app at all, and users are reduced to filing feature requests for two years — that is where an embeddable in-app scaling primitive earns its keep. Ship the layer with a documented desktop/Electron integration path, not only a web one.
  - OFFER THE LAYER AS A HEADLESS CORE PLUS RENDERERS. Model operators will not adopt a visual widget that clashes with their design system. A tokenised core (CSS custom properties + a small preference state machine + an OS-preference reader) with reference React/web-component/SwiftUI/Compose renderers is far more adoptable than a drop-in panel — and avoids becoming the third-party JS overlay the disability community has spent a decade rejecting.
PITFALLS:
  - A floating 'A- A A+' font-size widget on a web UI. It duplicates browser zoom (which already satisfies SC 1.4.4), typically scales only body text while leaving the composer, buttons and icons unchanged, does not persist across the user's other sites, and is precisely the pattern 1,031 accessibility practitioners and disabled users signed a document against.
  - A single bundled 'Accessibility mode' toggle. Low-vision needs are contradictory — some users want maximum brightness and contrast, others need low brightness for photophobia; some with tunnel vision want smaller text. Bundling contrast, size, spacing, polarity and motion behind one switch guarantees it is wrong for most of the population it targets.
  - Dark mode shipped as 'the accessible theme'. Piepenbrock (2013) and Dobres (2017) both favour light mode for reading performance in the general case; only cloudy-media conditions such as cataract show a clear dark-mode reading advantage (Legge 1985, on CRTs). A quarter of WebAIM's high-contrast users want dark-on-light.
  - Pure #FFFFFF on #000000 as the 'high contrast' preset. Maximum luminance ratio is not maximum legibility; it is uncomfortable for photophobia and is the configuration the (poorly evidenced but widely reported) astigmatic halation complaint targets. Offer it, do not default to it.
  - forced-color-adjust: none to 'protect the brand' in Windows High Contrast. MDN is explicit that the forced palette and text backplates are 'often essential for some users to be able to read or use a given website'. Overriding it is a net accessibility regression dressed as polish.
  - A component library of styled divs with ARIA roles. It will pass an axe scan and fail completely in forced-colors mode, because UAs assign system colours by native semantics and ignore role attributes.
  - Auto-scrolling the transcript to follow streamed tokens. At any real magnification this throws the user's viewport away from the text they were mid-line on, and there is no way to get back except manual panning — the exact behaviour SViM was built to eliminate.
  - Solving 'streaming accessibility' with an ARIA live region and calling it done. Live regions serve screen-reader users; a low-vision user with residual sight and a magnifier gets nothing from them. The claude.ai audit that found live-region gaps did not examine zoom, contrast or font size at all — that blind spot is itself the industry pattern to avoid repeating.
  - Shipping OpenDyslexic (or any 'dyslexia font') as a marquee feature. It measurably did not improve reading rate or accuracy, and no participant preferred it. It signals that the layer was designed from folk knowledge rather than evidence, which will cost credibility with the exact organisations whose endorsement the project needs.
  - Assuming responsive design equals reflow compliance for AI output. Prose reflows; the code blocks, tables and diagrams that dominate assistant answers are a declared exception and need their own scroll, wrap and zoom affordances.
  - max-width in pixels on the message column. It looks like a good 'measure' decision and then refuses to grow when the user scales text, forcing horizontal scrolling at exactly the moment it matters. Use ch/rem.
  - Removing or thinning focus rings for visual cleanliness, or letting a sticky composer/toolbar cover the focused element. SC 2.4.11 (AA) and 2.4.13 (AAA) exist because low-vision keyboard users cannot detect small appearance changes.
  - Red/green as the only encoding in diffs, test results, or generated chart series. ~8% of men cannot reliably separate them, and this is the single most common colour failure in developer-facing AI output.
  - Detecting assistive technology to auto-apply settings. It is a disability disclosure the user did not consent to, is called out as a GDPR/CCPA problem in the Overlay Fact Sheet, and will get the project categorised as an overlay.
  - Validating only at 400% browser zoom. Real users run 8x–45x. A layout that is fine at 400% can still be unusable at 16x if related controls are far apart or the reading position is not anchored.
  - Building the layer as an injectable third-party script for other people's products. That is structurally an overlay, however good the intent. Ship it as a library model operators compile in, which is also what the stated goal (embeddable component, MIT/CC0) already implies.
OPEN QUESTIONS:
  - No published study anywhere examines low-vision or screen-magnifier users interacting with an LLM chat interface specifically. The evidence chain here is inferential: magnifier + dynamic content (SViM, video, IUI '20), magnifier + spatially separated controls (MagPro, 2021), magnifier + reading/line-switching (CHI '23). A small contextual inquiry with 8-12 magnifier users on ChatGPT/Claude/Gemini would be genuinely novel and is probably the highest-value thing Clara could fund or run.
  - What is the right streaming rate or chunk granularity for magnifier users? Nothing measures this. Low-vision readers are roughly 3x slower than sighted readers, which suggests token streaming tuned to sighted reading speed is far too fast — but whether the right answer is paragraph-chunking, a fixed WPM cap, or full settle-then-reveal is unknown.
  - Does 'settle and reveal' hurt perceived responsiveness or trust enough that users turn it off? Streaming is widely believed to improve perceived latency and let users abort bad answers early; no one has tested the trade-off with low-vision users.
  - The astigmatism/halation claim: I could not find a primary peer-reviewed source. If it is real it changes the default dark theme; if it is folklore, guidance repeating it is misleading practitioners. Worth an actual literature check by someone with vision-science database access.
  - WCAG 2.x contrast (4.5:1) is under active criticism as a poor predictor of legibility — it ignores font weight and size, and behaves badly for dark-on-dark pairs — with APCA proposed for WCAG 3. I did not find a settled empirical resolution. A layer that hard-codes 4.5:1 may be shipping a metric that is superseded; one that adopts APCA early may fail legal conformance checks against EN 301 549, which references WCAG 2.1 AA.
  - How do AI chat mobile apps behave at the largest iOS Dynamic Type / Android font-scale settings? I found no audit. This matters because mobile is the one platform where the OS text-size setting genuinely should reach the app.
  - Whether portable preference profiles can achieve adoption now when ISO/IEC 24751 and GPII/Cloud4all did not. I found the standard, and a statement that 'very few systems have yet implemented' it, but no published post-mortem explaining why — which would be the most useful document Clara could read before finalising its profile spec.
  - The true state of vision accessibility in major AI products is unmeasurable from outside: OpenAI has no public VPAT/ACR that I could find, and Anthropic's claude.ai VPAT is reported to require an NDA. All claims about 'no AI product ships a vision layer' rest on observable UI plus community feature requests, not on vendor conformance documentation.
  - No evidence found on whether per-region magnification (different zoom for prose vs code vs images, the SteeringWheel finding that users wanted different zoom levels for product descriptions vs product images) is achievable or wanted inside a single web component, versus being properly the magnifier's job.
  - Colour-blind palettes for AI-generated charts: GitHub's split into separate Protanopia/Deuteranopia and Tritanopia themes after user complaints suggests one 'colorblind mode' is inadequate, but I found no controlled study establishing a recommended default categorical palette for the specific case of model-generated data visualisations.
MISSED ANGLE (per verifier): The pass has no evidence base for the one property that makes an AI chat interface different from every surface its sources studied: the content moves while you are reading it. Every cited study targets static content — Office documents, newspaper layouts, fixed web pages, static text blocks under eye-tracking. Nothing addresses token-by-token streaming into an auto-scrolling viewport, which under 15x–45x magnification is a distinct and probably severe failure mode: the magnifier window is parked on a region whose content is being appended to and reflowed beneath it, and the auto-scroll continuously moves the reader's target out from under a lens they navigate manually. The CHI '23 finding that "a small deviation on the position of the magnification window would cause a whole line to disappear" is exactly the mechanism, applied to a surface that deviates itself. This also has direct WCAG hooks the pass never touches — 2.2.2 Pause, Stop, Hide (auto-updating content), 3.2.5 Change on Request (AAA), 2.3.3 Animation from Interactions, and 1.4.13 Content on Hover or Focus for the per-message action toolbars that appear on hover — and it implies concrete, testable requirements (a stop-streaming or render-on-complete mode, anchored scroll position, no layout shift above the insertion point) that none of the nine claims generate. A secondary gap: the pass contains no regulatory forcing function at all — no European Accessibility Act obligations (applicable from 28 June 2025 for consumer-facing digital services), no DOJ ADA Title II web rule with its WCAG 2.1 AA compliance dates, and no audit of what ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini already ship for text size and contrast, which is the baseline any "no AI product does X" positioning would have to clear.

### The accessibility-overlay controversy (critical)
FINDINGS:
  - The Overlay Fact Sheet currently carries 1,031 named signatories and names 40+ overlay products, and its position is absolute: it endorses no legitimate role for overlays, not even as a temporary stopgap during remediation. [verified: confirmed]
    (Overlay Fact Sheet, 2021–2026 (living document) — https://overlayfactsheet.com/en/; confidence high)
  - The FTC action against accessiBe was a consent order, not a litigated finding. The proposed order was announced 3 January 2025; the final order was approved 22 April 2025 with a $1,000,000 payment. It prohibits two distinct things — unsubstantiated WCAG-compliance claims, AND presenting paid/affiliated reviews as independent opinion.
    (FTC Order Requires Online Marketer to Pay $1 Million for Deceptive Claims that its AI Product Could Make Websites Compliant with Accessibility Guidelines / FTC Approves Final Order Requiring accessiBe to pay $1 Million, 2025 — https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/04/ftc-approves-final-order-requiring-accessibe-pay-1-million; confidence high)
  - Overlays do not reduce ADA legal risk and correlate with elevated risk: in the first half of 2025, 659 lawsuits were filed against sites that already had an accessibility widget installed — roughly a third of all 2,019 digital accessibility filings in that period.
    (2025 Midyear Accessibility Lawsuit Report: Key Legal Trends (UsableNet), 2025 — https://blog.usablenet.com/2025-midyear-accessibility-lawsuit-report-key-legal-trends; confidence medium)
  - Overlay vendors are being sued by their own paying customers for breach of contract — the 'buy this and you won't get sued' promise is itself now litigated. Two putative class actions: Tribeca Skin Care v. accessiBe (filed 24 June 2024, S.D.N.Y.) and Bloomsybox.com LLC v. UserWay (filed 19 July 2024, D. Del., 1:24-cv-00844).
    (New Class Action Lawsuit against AccessiBe — Law Office of Lainey Feingold, 2024 — https://www.lflegal.com/2024/07/accessibe-class-action/; confidence high)
  - The strongest empirical evidence available shows that disabled users simply ignore a floating accessibility button. In a 2024 study with 21 participants with permanent visual impairments, participants 'almost entirely ignored' overlay floating buttons during tasks and preferred their existing assistive technology; none of the three overlays tested achieved WCAG 2.1 AA, and usability deteriorated once users did engage with the overlay.
    (The Impact of Web Accessibility Overlays (master's thesis, Daniela Kubesch), 2024 — https://overlays.dnikub.dev/; confidence medium)
  - Accessibility practitioners rate overlays as ineffective, and practitioners who are themselves disabled rate them worse: 67% overall say 'not at all' or 'not very' effective, rising to 72% among respondents with disabilities, with only 2.4% of disabled respondents calling them very effective.
    (WebAIM: Survey of Web Accessibility Practitioners #3 Results, 2021 — https://webaim.org/blog/practitioners-survey-3/; confidence high)
  - Blind users and blind-led companies build and ship tooling specifically to BLOCK overlays. AccessiByeBye — from Pneuma Solutions, co-founded by Mike Calvo and Matt Campbell, a visually impaired developer — blocks accessiBe, AudioEye, EqualWeb, MaxAccess, TruAbilities, User1st and UserWay by default; parallel uBlock Origin / Adblock Plus filter lists exist.
    (AccessiByeBye (Pneuma Solutions), 2021–2026 — https://www.accessibyebye.org/; confidence high)
  - The documented technical harms are specific and reproducible: overlays misuse aria-hidden, insert non-semantic headings in place of real table headers, break keyboard operability (WCAG 2.1.1 Level A), remove visible focus indicators when the user declines the overlay, interrupt screen reader page announcements with an alert, and fail their own 'stop animations' control (WCAG 2.2.2 Level A).
    (#accessiBe Will Get You Sued — Adrian Roselli, 2020 (updated 2025) — https://adrianroselli.com/2020/06/accessibe-will-get-you-sued.html; confidence high)
  - Overlays create a distinct privacy harm, not just a usability one: detecting assistive technology and combining it with a browser fingerprint identifies a specific person AS DISABLED, which is special-category data under GDPR.
    (AccessiBe and data protection? — Tink (Léonie Watson), 2021 — https://tink.uk/accessibe-and-data-protection/; confidence high)
  - Every major disability-led organisation that has taken a position has condemned overlays, on both sides of the Atlantic — NFB (2021), ACB (2021), AFB (2022) and the European Disability Forum with IAAP (2023). AFB's language is a blanket prohibition on third-party remediation scripts.
    (European Disability Forum and International Association of Accessibility Professionals joint statement on accessibility overlays, 2023 — https://www.edf-feph.org/publications/joint-statement-on-accessibility-overlays/; confidence high)
  - The single decisive test the community applies is REDUNDANCY: an overlay's user-facing controls (font size, contrast, text-to-speech) are condemned because users already have those capabilities configured at OS/browser/AT level and want them consistent across every site — not re-learned per site.
    (Overlay Fact Sheet (redundancy argument) / EDF-IAAP joint statement, 2021–2023 — https://overlayfactsheet.com/en/; confidence high)
  - UK government guidance (NHS service manual) is explicit that widgets neither achieve compliance nor confer legal protection, and it enumerates the exact UI failure modes of a floating widget — which is the shape a naive embeddable layer would take.
    (NHS service manual — Accessibility guidance: Design, 2024–2026 (living guidance) — https://service-manual.nhs.uk/accessibility/design; confidence high)
  - There IS an accepted first-party pattern, and Wikipedia's 2024 'Accessibility for reading' work is the clearest worked example: it changed the DEFAULT (base font 14px→16px), shipped controls in the product's own chrome rather than a floating widget, offers an 'automatic' mode that follows the device/browser preference, and was designed with community testing rather than imposed.
    (Reading/Web/Accessibility for reading — MediaWiki, 2024 — https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Reading/Web/Accessibility_for_reading; confidence high)
  - CONTENT-level adaptation — plain language, simplification, summaries — sits in a completely different category from presentation-level overlay controls, and is affirmatively recommended by W3C, not condemned. This is the standards-backed opening for an embeddable layer that overlays do not have.
    (Making Content Usable for People with Cognitive and Learning Disabilities (W3C Note) / WAI-Adapt Overview, 2021 / 2023 — https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/; confidence high)
  - Automation covers far less than overlay marketing implies, and detection is much easier than repair — roughly 30% of WCAG 2.1 AA success criteria are machine-testable at all (about 15–16 of 50), though automated tools catch ~57% of issues by raw volume because common issues repeat.
    (The Automated Accessibility Coverage Report — Deque, 2021 (report), cited through 2026 — https://www.deque.com/automated-accessibility-coverage-report/; confidence medium)
  - The overlay industry has litigated against its critics, which makes proximity to the category reputationally dangerous even for well-intentioned tools. AudioEye sued accessibility expert Adrian Roselli over his criticism; he was served in March 2023 and AudioEye dropped the suit on 3 January 2024, with the settlement acknowledging his statements were opinion rather than fact.
    (Case is Over! New Low in the Accessibility "Industry" — Law Office of Lainey Feingold / #AudioEye Has Dropped Its Suit Against Me, 2023–2024 — https://adrianroselli.com/2024/01/audioeye-has-dropped-its-suit-against-me.html; confidence medium)
DESIGN IMPLICATIONS:
  - THE REDUNDANCY TEST is the gate every feature must pass. Ask: can the user's OS, browser or AT already do this everywhere? If yes, do not build it. Font size, contrast, colour inversion, magnification, text-to-speech, cursor size, reduced motion — all fail the test and are precisely what the Overlay Fact Sheet, EDF/IAAP and NHS condemn as duplicative. Clara's content transformation (plain-language rewrite, Easy Read, deterministic number/date/negation verification) PASSES the test: no OS setting can rewrite a sentence. Scope the layer to content transformation only and explicitly document that it does not offer presentation controls, with the reason.
  - NO FLOATING BUTTON, NO PERSON-ICON, NO TOOLBAR. This is the single most recognisable overlay signature, it is what NHS guidance enumerates as harmful ('obscuring key functionality', 'may pop up and cover up information'), and Kubesch's 21 visually impaired participants 'almost entirely ignored' it anyway. Integrate into the host product's own chrome — a message-level action, a settings entry, a keyboard command — the way Wikipedia put its controls in the Appearance menu.
  - NEVER MODIFY THE HOST PAGE'S DOM, ARIA OR FOCUS. Every documented technical harm (aria-hidden misuse, fake headings, broken keyboard operability, removed focus indicators, interrupted screen reader announcements) comes from an overlay rewriting someone else's markup. An embeddable layer must render its output in its own region and leave host markup untouched — ideally as a first-party component compiled into the adopter's build, not a third-party <script> tag. 'One line of code' is the overlay industry's exact marketing phrase; never use it.
  - NEVER DETECT ASSISTIVE TECHNOLOGY, AND COLLECT NO TELEMETRY THAT COULD INFER DISABILITY. Watson's analysis shows AT-detection plus fingerprint uniquely identifies a person as disabled — special-category data under GDPR. Make this architecturally impossible rather than a policy promise: no AT sniffing, no server round-trip for preferences, no cross-site persistence, no analytics on which simplification modes are used. Publish the data-flow diagram.
  - RESPECT PLATFORM PREFERENCES INSTEAD OF OFFERING YOUR OWN TOGGLES: honour prefers-reduced-motion, prefers-contrast, prefers-color-scheme, prefers-reduced-transparency and the user's root font size (size everything in rem). Where a preference must be product-specific, follow Wikipedia's 'automatic' pattern — default to the platform signal, allow override, never fight the OS.
  - FIX THE DEFAULT, DON'T ADD A SETTING. Wikipedia's most important move was raising the base font from 14px to 16px for everyone, not shipping a font-size widget. For an LLM accessibility layer the equivalent is: make the default answer readable — reasonable reading level, short paragraphs, real headings, no wall-of-text — and make the deeper transformation the opt-in. A layer that only adds a control implicitly concedes the default is inaccessible.
  - MAKE NO COMPLIANCE CLAIM, EVER. The FTC consent order defines the exact prohibited representation: that an automated product makes a site WCAG-compliant or keeps it compliant, absent supporting evidence. Adopt this as a hard rule in README, marketing, and the outreach letter: Clara improves comprehensibility and verifies specific factual invariants; it does not make anything WCAG, ADA, EAA or EN 301 549 compliant, and it does not reduce legal risk. State this prominently — it is a credibility asset with this audience, not a weakness.
  - SCOPE THE VERIFIER'S CLAIM PRECISELY. Deque's data shows ~30% of WCAG AA criteria are machine-testable and automated tools catch ~57% of issues by volume — and that is detection, not repair. Clara's verifier checks preservation of numbers, dates, negations and obligations across a rewrite. That is a narrow, falsifiable, genuinely deterministic claim. Say exactly that and resist any broader phrasing; the overlay industry died of claim inflation.
  - SHIP A DOCUMENTED KILL SWITCH AND MAKE IT BLOCKLIST-FRIENDLY. AT users built AccessiByeBye and adblock filter lists because overlays gave them no way out. Give the layer a stable, documented CSS selector / script name, honour a user-set opt-out, never re-prompt after dismissal, and say publicly that you will not obfuscate to evade blockers. Offer the signature to blocklist maintainers proactively.
  - GROUND THE PROJECT IN W3C COGA, NOT IN ACCESSIBILITY-TOOL MARKETING. Cite Objective 8 'Support Adaptation and Personalization', the 'Support Simplification' pattern, User Story 3.8.1 on easy-to-understand language, and Pattern 4.4.8 on summaries; align the portable accessibility profile with WAI-Adapt's author-marks-up / user-agent-adapts architecture. This puts Clara in the standards lineage rather than the overlay lineage, and gives adopters a defensible citation.
  - PRESERVE THE ORIGINAL ALONGSIDE THE SIMPLIFICATION. COGA endorses offering an easy-to-understand version in addition to the source, and ACB Resolution 2021-09 opposes anything 'that creates a separate experience for users of assistive technology'. Simplified output must be one view of the same content available to everyone, reversible in one action, with the original always reachable — not a segregated 'accessible version' and not a silent replacement.
  - CO-DESIGN AND SAY WHO WITH. EDF/IAAP advise buyers to 'engage with digital accessibility experts, persons with disabilities and their representative organisations'; ACB requires 'meaningful feedback from users who are blind or have low vision'; NHS recommends testing with users with access needs. Recruit disabled testers before wide release, publish who tested and what they found including the negative findings, and commission an independent audit. Publishing an unflattering audit is the strongest available anti-overlay signal.
  - POSITION AS A COMPONENT ADOPTERS OWN, NOT A SERVICE THAT PHONES HOME. First-party is the structural difference between Wikipedia's Appearance menu and accessiBe. MIT/CC0 licensing, no vendor runtime dependency, no hosted call-back, self-hostable, forkable. If model operators embed it, it becomes their own first-party feature — which is the only category the disability community has accepted.
PITFALLS:
  - Adding a floating accessibility button or person-icon toolbar 'because users expect it'. It is the visual signature of a condemned product, NHS guidance lists it as actively harmful, and empirical user testing shows disabled participants ignore it. It would sink the project's credibility on sight.
  - Bundling font-size, contrast, colour-inversion or text-to-speech controls because they are easy wins. These are exactly the redundant features the Overlay Fact Sheet and EDF/IAAP name. Clara already ships browser speech I/O and a pictogram board — audit these against the redundancy test and reframe or scope them, since TTS in particular duplicates what every screen reader user already has configured better.
  - Auto-detecting whether a screen reader is present in order to 'improve' the experience. This is the specific behavior that converts a usability tool into a GDPR special-category-data problem, and it is the harm Léonie Watson documented in detail. It is tempting because it enables better defaults; it is disqualifying.
  - Persisting user preferences in a cross-site cookie or a vendor-hosted profile. The Overlay Fact Sheet flags precisely this — cross-site settings persistence with no opt-out — as creating 'GDPR and CCPA risk for the overlay customer'. A portable accessibility profile is a genuinely good idea, but it must be user-held and user-transported, never vendor-observed.
  - Any compliance or legal-risk language: 'helps you meet WCAG', 'supports ADA compliance', 'EAA-ready'. The FTC consent order makes the first category actionable, and UsableNet's data plus the Tribeca and Bloomsybox class actions show the legal-protection promise is what customers sue over.
  - Announcing the layer on page load, injecting a live-region alert, or moving focus on init. Roselli documented overlay alerts interrupting screen reader page announcements — a small implementation detail that produces disproportionate user rage.
  - Rewriting or 'repairing' the host application's markup — adding alt text, relabelling controls, patching ARIA. Even where technically achievable, this is definitionally overlay behavior, breaks correct existing ARIA, and puts Clara in the category it needs to stay out of.
  - Collecting testimonials and formatting them as independent reviews or third-party articles. This was FTC count two against accessiBe, separate from the compliance claims, and it is an easy trap for an open-source project soliciting adopter quotes. Disclose every material connection.
  - Treating the browser extension as the flagship. A Manifest V3 extension that overlays simplified text on any page is structurally closer to an overlay than the embeddable library is — it modifies pages the user does not own. It is defensible ONLY because the USER installs it for themselves rather than a site owner imposing it on visitors. That distinction is real but subtle; make it explicit in the docs, keep the extension strictly user-invoked, and lead outreach with the embeddable first-party component instead.
  - Assuming plain-language rewriting is uncontroversial because presentation overlays are the thing under attack. Automated simplification can drop nuance, alter legal meaning, or condescend — and 'we simplified it for you' has its own history of paternalism complaints in the cognitive-disability community. The deterministic verifier is the mitigation, so make its guarantees and its LIMITS the headline claim.
  - Reading the community's rejection of overlays as merely 'overlays don't work yet, ours is better'. The objection is architectural — third-party injection, imposed by the site owner, duplicating what users already control, without consent. A better-engineered overlay is still an overlay. Only changing the architecture changes the verdict.
OPEN QUESTIONS:
  - I could not find any case where the disability community has explicitly BLESSED a site-level content-simplification layer. The accepted first-party examples (Wikipedia, GOV.UK/NHS design systems) are all presentation and information-architecture, not automated content rewriting. Clara's category may be genuinely novel — which means the acceptance criteria are untested, and the project should validate them with disabled users directly rather than reasoning by analogy from the overlay debate.
  - No peer-reviewed CHI/ASSETS/W4A study on overlays surfaced in this research. The strongest empirical work found is Kubesch's 2024 master's thesis (n=21). The evidence base against overlays is broad and consistent but rests mostly on practitioner testing, survey data, litigation records and organisational statements rather than controlled academic studies. Worth flagging honestly rather than overstating.
  - Final dispositions of Tribeca Skin Care v. accessiBe (S.D.N.Y., filed June 2024) and Bloomsybox.com LLC v. UserWay (D. Del. 1:24-cv-00844, filed July 2024) are unresolved in the sources retrieved. Whether either achieved class certification or a merits ruling would materially strengthen or weaken the 'overlays create customer-side liability' claim.
  - The UsableNet lawsuit figures vary noticeably across secondary reports (503 vs 449 vs 1,023 vs 659 for overlapping periods), likely because different counts measure different things — sites with a widget installed vs complaints that explicitly name the widget as a barrier. I fetched the 2025 midyear primary source directly, but the 2024 full-year PDF could not be parsed in this environment. Anyone citing a specific number should pull the primary PDF and check which definition it uses.
  - Adrian Roselli's allegation that accessiBe detects accessibility checkers (WAVE) and alters its output is serious and, if true, would be strong evidence of bad faith rather than mere incompetence. It rests on one investigator's reverse-engineering and I found no independent replication. Do not repeat it as established fact.
  - I found no systematic evidence about what accessibility settings, if any, major LLM chat products currently ship. Search returned only incidental commentary and vendor-authored comparisons. The user's core observation — that no AI chat product ships a vision-accessibility layer — is plausible and matches what I saw, but I could not verify it rigorously. It should be established by direct audit of the actual products (settings panels, keyboard maps, screen reader behavior, prefers-* support) before being used as a load-bearing claim in outreach.
  - NFB's own pages (nfb.org) returned HTTP 403 to direct fetching, so NFB Resolution 2021-17 and NFB's comments to the FTC on the accessiBe consent order are sourced from search snippets and secondary coverage only. The exact resolution text and NFB's specific asks of the FTC should be verified from nfb.org directly before quoting.
  - Where exactly the line falls for the browser extension is genuinely unsettled. A user-installed extension that transforms pages for its own installer is philosophically the opposite of a site-imposed overlay — but it is also technically page injection, and I found no community statement addressing this specific case. This deserves a direct question to disabled testers rather than a designer's assumption.
MISSED ANGLE (per verifier): The pass never separates the two things that make an overlay an overlay - third-party injection into someone else's site, and substitution for source remediation - from what this project is actually building, which is first-party accessibility affordances inside its own product. Nothing in the Overlay Fact Sheet, EDF/IAAP, NFB, ACB, AFB or NHS guidance condemns a product shipping its own accessibility settings; every one of those sources condemns a bolted-on script that promises to fix code the vendor does not own. A settings pane in Slack, VS Code or iOS is not an overlay and has never been treated as one. Without that distinction the pass reads as evidence against the project rather than as a set of design constraints for it. Two consequences follow that the pass also missed. First, the entire evidence base is about blind screen-reader users on third-party websites - Kubesch and Kohler both recruited only visually impaired participants, and NFB/ACB/AFB are blindness organisations. Users with cognitive, motor, dyslexia, photosensitivity or fatigue-related needs are almost absent from it, and those are precisely the constituencies for whom in-product controls (pacing, density, reduced motion, plain-language rendering, output length) do something the OS and browser genuinely cannot do globally - which is the one test the redundancy argument actually poses. Second, the regulatory clock is missing entirely: the European Accessibility Act became applicable 28 June 2025 and the DOJ's ADA Title II web rule carries April 2026/2027 compliance deadlines, both of which reshape the overlay market's incentives and set the conformance target an AI chat accessibility layer would be measured against, including the rules on conforming alternate versions that bear directly on whether a parallel accessible mode is defensible.

### W3C standards to conform to / build on
FINDINGS:
  - NAUR — the only W3C document specifically about natural language interfaces — is a non-normative Group Draft Note frozen at 3 September 2022, and it is NOT a deliverable in the current APA Working Group charter (29 July 2025 – 31 July 2027). It can never be cited as a conformance obligation.
    (Natural Language Interface Accessibility User Requirements (W3C Group Draft Note) / Accessible Platform Architectures Working Group Charter, 2022 (charter 2025) — https://www.w3.org/TR/naur/; confidence high)
  - NAUR nonetheless supplies a ready-made, itemised conformance checklist that maps almost one-to-one onto an embeddable accessibility layer — and it never once mentions LLMs or generative AI, so Clara would be the first implementation to apply it to them.
    (Natural Language Interface Accessibility User Requirements, 2022 — https://www.w3.org/TR/naur/; confidence high)
  - No W3C standard requires that a machine-generated or simplified text preserve the meaning of its source. The nearest requirement, NAUR REQ 13b, is about speech-recognition confidence, not content fidelity — so Clara's deterministic verifier occupies a genuine standards gap rather than implementing an existing rule.
    (NAUR REQ 13b / Cognitive Accessibility Research Modules — Voice Systems and Conversational Interfaces, 2022 / 2026 — https://www.w3.org/TR/2026/DNOTE-coga-voice-20260205/; confidence high)
  - W3C's only document on AI accessibility is an incomplete Editor's Draft whose three most decision-relevant sections are still unwritten `@@` placeholders. There is no standard to conform to for generative-AI accessibility, and the venue is open for contribution.
    (Accessibility of machine learning and generative AI (W3C Editor's Draft), 2026 — https://w3c.github.io/ai-accessibility/; confidence high)
  - W3C's own AI draft states that generative AI currently fails at exactly the plain-language transformations Clara performs — literal language, tense, nested clauses — which is direct standards-body support for a verify-don't-trust architecture.
    (Accessibility of machine learning and generative AI / AI and Accessibility Research Symposium 2023, 2023–2026 — https://www.w3.org/WAI/research/ai2023/; confidence high)
  - ATAG 2.0 (W3C Recommendation, 24 September 2015) does apply to LLMs that draft content for publication — but its glossary Note 4 explicitly excludes the ordinary case where the user is the only consumer of the output. A blanket 'AI is an authoring tool' claim would be wrong.
    (Authoring Tool Accessibility Guidelines (ATAG) 2.0, W3C Recommendation, 2015 — https://www.w3.org/TR/ATAG20/; confidence high)
  - ATAG 2.0 Part B already contains a 'preserve accessibility information through a transformation, or warn / auto-check / prompt to check' rule (B.1.2.1) that is structurally identical to Clara's rewrite-then-verify design — giving Clara an existing W3C Recommendation to claim conformance against rather than inventing a frame.
    (ATAG 2.0 Part B success criteria, 2015 — https://www.w3.org/TR/ATAG20/; confidence high)
  - EN 301 549 turns ATAG-style authoring-tool duties into legally operative EU requirements via clause 11.8, and clause 11 covers non-web software — operating systems, native applications, kiosk software and mobile apps — not just websites. This is the regulatory hook for an embeddable layer in desktop apps and agent harnesses.
    (On authoring tools in EN 301 549 (Hidde de Vries) / Technical Summary of EN 301 549 v3.2.1 (Government of Canada), 2024 / 2021 — https://hidde.blog/authoring-tools-in-en-301-549/; confidence high)
  - EN 301 549 clause 11.7 already requires software to follow the platform's user preferences for colour, contrast, font type, font size, units and focus cursor — but its applicability to web and mobile is unresolved inside ETSI. A portable preference layer must defer to OS settings first, and must not over-claim 11.7 conformance.
    (ETSI Human Factors TC — EN 301 549 issue #23: Re-examine appropriateness of 11.7 for websites and mobile applications, open, undated resolution — https://labs.etsi.org/rep/HF/en301549/-/issues/23; confidence medium)
  - The European Accessibility Act applies from 28 June 2025 but does not cover general-purpose software or AI services. An LLM chat product is out of EAA scope unless it is embedded in a covered service (e-commerce, banking, transport, e-books, electronic communications, AVMS) — so the EU adoption pitch is 'your customers need this', not 'you are legally required'.
    (Directive (EU) 2019/882 (European Accessibility Act), EUR-Lex, 2019 (applies 2025) — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32019L0882; confidence high)
  - WCAG 2.2 contains no chat-specific criterion. The single load-bearing one for streaming AI output is 4.1.3 Status Messages (Level AA), and the thing Clara does best — plain language — sits at 3.1.5 Reading Level, which is Level AAA and therefore legally optional almost everywhere.
    (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2 / WCAG Overview, 2023, updated 2024 — https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/; confidence high)
  - The ARIA Authoring Practices Guide has no pattern and no practice page for chat, conversation, message logs, streaming or even live regions. There is no authoritative W3C pattern for streaming LLM output — whoever publishes a good one defines the de-facto standard.
    (ARIA Authoring Practices Guide — Patterns and Practices indexes; WCAG technique ARIA23, 2026 (retrieved) — https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/patterns/; confidence high)
  - role="feed" is the wrong primitive for a chat transcript. It is a browsing contract for infinitely-scrolling articles, not a conversation, and its keyboard model (Page Up/Down between articles) does not match a message stream.
    (ARIA Authoring Practices Guide — Feed Pattern, 2026 (retrieved) — https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/patterns/feed/; confidence medium)
  - ariaNotify() is now normative in the WAI-ARIA 1.3 Working Draft (4 June 2026) as ARIANotifyMixin with a Permissions Policy hook — but it is explicitly not Baseline, and disabled users and accessibility engineers are publicly warning it will be abused. Use it as progressive enhancement over a live-region fallback, never as the sole channel.
    (Accessible Rich Internet Applications (WAI-ARIA) 1.3 W3C Working Draft / MDN Element.ariaNotify / The Siren Song of ariaNotify(), 2026 — https://www.w3.org/TR/2026/WD-wai-aria-1.3-20260604/; confidence high)
  - WAI-Adapt is not a usable substrate for a portable preference layer: the W3C TAG forced the Content Module down to a single attribute. Only `adapt-symbol` survives, stuck at Candidate Recommendation Snapshot since 5 January 2023 with no finalised implementations and a chartered REC target of Q2 2027.
    (W3C TAG telcon minutes 19 Dec 2022 / WAI-Adapt: Symbols Module CR Snapshot / APA WG Charter, 2022–2023 (charter 2025) — https://www.w3.org/TR/adapt-symbols/; confidence high)
  - The only shipping, OS-mediated substrate for user preferences is the CSS user-preference media features in Media Queries Level 5 (W3C Working Draft, 19 February 2026). A portable profile should read these first and persist only what the OS cannot express.
    (Media Queries Level 5, W3C Working Draft, 2026 — https://www.w3.org/TR/mediaqueries-5/; confidence high)
  - COGA published a brand-new Draft Note on 'Voice Systems and Conversational Interfaces' on 5 February 2026 — the most current live W3C venue touching conversational AI — but it covers IVR and voice assistants, never names chatbots or LLMs, and offers nothing on hallucination or verification. Its 'Ensure Human Backup is Available and Reachable' recommendation is a design requirement most AI products omit.
    (Cognitive Accessibility Research Modules — Voice Systems and Conversational Interfaces (W3C Group Note Draft), 2026 — https://www.w3.org/TR/2026/DNOTE-coga-voice-20260205/; confidence high)
  - COGA 'Making Content Usable' (W3C Working Group Note, 29 April 2021) supplies the mandate for both halves of Clara: Objective 3 'Use Clear and Understandable Content' for the rewrite, and Objective 8 'Support Adaptation and Personalization' for the portable preference layer.
    (Making Content Usable for People with Cognitive and Learning Disabilities (W3C Working Group Note), 2021 — https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/; confidence high)
  - WCAG 3.0 is a Working Draft dated 3 March 2026 with 'several years of work' remaining and no AI content. Building against it would be building against a moving target, though its Guideline 2.2.3 shows plain language is heading toward normative status in a way WCAG 2.x never allowed.
    (W3C Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 3.0, W3C Working Draft, 2026 — https://www.w3.org/TR/wcag-3.0/; confidence high)
  - ISO/IEC 24751 'AccessForAll' is the pre-existing international standard for exactly what Clara's portable JSON accessibility profile does — a machine-readable personal needs and preferences record matched against resource descriptions — but it is scoped to e-learning and has essentially no mainstream adoption.
    (ISO/IEC 24751 (AccessForAll) — Professional Web Accessibility Auditing Made Easy, 2008–2023 — https://pressbooks.library.torontomu.ca/pwaa/chapter/iso-iec-24751-accessforall/; confidence medium)
DESIGN IMPLICATIONS:
  - Ship a NAUR conformance table as a first-class artefact. NAUR's ~27 requirements are the only itemised, W3C-published checklist for natural-language interfaces, and nobody has ever applied them to an LLM chat product. Publishing 'Clara vs NAUR REQ 1–27, with evidence' costs little, is checkable, and makes Clara the reference implementation of a dormant W3C document — which is far more persuasive to a model operator than an aspirational claim. Prioritise REQ 7 (synchronized text transcript for all speech output), REQ 14 (rate/pitch/volume), REQ 15 (font + text-spacing control), REQ 16a/16b (help and 'what can this system do'), REQ 24 (error correction) and REQ 25 (reversible actions) — Clara already partly has 7, 14 and 16.
  - Frame the deterministic verifier as an implementation of ATAG 2.0 SC B.1.2.1, not as a novel invention. B.1.2.1's structure — after a transformation, either preserve the information, or warn, or automatically check, or prompt to check — is precisely rewrite-then-verify. Citing an existing W3C Recommendation converts 'a nice safety feature' into 'the mechanism a 2015 W3C Recommendation already asks for, which no AI product implements'. Pair it with B.1.1.2 (automatically generated content must be accessible or the author prompted/checked) and B.4.1.1 (accessible-content features on by default) as the argument for defaulting the verifier ON.
  - Scope the ATAG claim honestly and it becomes sharper, not weaker. ATAG's Note 4 excludes output only the asker experiences, so do not claim 'all AI chat is an authoring tool'. Claim instead: the moment an LLM product drafts content for publication — a post, a page, an email to others, an agent writing to a CMS — ATAG Part B applies, and no major LLM product implements it. That is a defensible, under-discussed argument and it targets exactly the integrations (agent harnesses, CMS copilots) where Clara is embeddable.
  - Target EN 301 549 clause 11, not just clause 9. Clause 11 covers operating systems, native applications, kiosk software and mobile apps, and clause 11.8 makes authoring-tool duties normative in the EU. Designing the layer as embeddable in desktop apps, mobile apps and agent harnesses — not only web chat — is what makes clause 11 reachable. A web-only component forfeits the strongest regulatory hook.
  - Build the preference layer on CSS user-preference media features first, and treat the JSON profile as the residue. Read prefers-reduced-motion, prefers-contrast, prefers-color-scheme, prefers-reduced-transparency, prefers-reduced-data, forced-colors from the OS; persist in the portable profile only what no OS setting expresses — target reading level, Easy Read on/off, symbol set, verification strictness, preferred language, verbosity of streaming announcements. This satisfies the spirit of EN 301 549 11.7 (follow platform preferences unless the user overrides) and avoids fighting the OS.
  - Align the AAC pictogram board with `adapt-symbol` and BCI concept-index values. Symbols is the one WAI-Adapt piece the TAG endorsed as architecturally sound, it is chartered for Recommendation in Q2 2027, and its CR exit criteria require two user agents or extensions to react to the attribute. Clara's browser extension plus its board could plausibly be one of those implementations — a cheap, concrete route to being cited by a W3C spec rather than merely citing one.
  - Do NOT build the portable profile on WAI-Adapt content attributes. Only `adapt-symbol` survived the TAG review; the rest were stripped and the Content/Tools modules are stalled with no browser support and no support data on WAI's own overview page. Cite ISO/IEC 24751-2 vocabulary for credibility if useful, but design the profile as a self-contained JSON spec with an explicit mapping table to CSS media features and to 24751-2 terms.
  - Design the streaming announcement model deliberately, because no W3C pattern exists. Use role="log" (implicit aria-live=polite, aria-atomic=false, per ARIA23) on the transcript; announce at semantic boundaries — sentence, paragraph, or completed answer — never per token; expose the boundary granularity as a user preference in the profile; and use ariaNotify() as progressive enhancement with a live-region fallback, since it is normative in the ARIA 1.3 WD but explicitly not Baseline. Never use aria-live="assertive" or priority:'high' for ordinary model output.
  - Publish the missing pattern as a spec-shaped contribution. The APG has no chat, conversation, log, streaming or live-region page, and the Feed pattern is a browsing contract for articles, not a conversation. A well-argued 'Conversational Stream' pattern — roles, announcement cadence, verification-result exposure, keyboard navigation between turns — filed against w3c/aria-practices is a genuine standards contribution and a distribution channel to every developer who reads the APG.
  - Add a human-backup escape hatch. The COGA voice module (Feb 2026) lists 'Ensure Human Backup is Available and Reachable' as a solution for conversational systems. For an accessibility layer this means: an always-available route to the original unmodified text, and a way to reach a person when the simplification or verification fails — not just a retry button.
  - Sell plain language on reach and usability, never on compliance. Reading Level is WCAG 3.1.5 Level AAA, which almost no law requires; EN 301 549 clause 9 adopts Level AA. The compliance story Clara can actually make is 4.1.3 Status Messages (AA) for streaming output, 11.7 user preferences, and 11.8 authoring tools. Conflating the two would get the pitch dismissed by anyone who knows the standard.
  - Contribute text to the two live W3C venues while they are still open. The AI-accessibility Editor's Draft has `@@` placeholders in exactly the sections Clara has evidence for — 'How can we have confidence that AI/ML generated content is meeting real user needs?', guardrails for repair heuristics, and potential harms — and the COGA research modules had a comment deadline of 15 April 2026 with more revisions expected. Supplying working code plus a verification method into an unwritten section is the cheapest possible route to standards influence.
  - Do not wait on NAUR being revived. It is absent from the 2025–2027 APA charter and has not moved since September 2022. Treat it as a design checklist and a citation, not as a standard that will one day carry legal weight — and do not build a roadmap item that depends on it advancing.
PITFALLS:
  - Announcing streamed tokens through an aria-live region. Live regions announce DOM changes; a token-by-token stream produces a flood of speech that makes the answer unusable for a screen reader user. There is no W3C guidance forbidding this — which is precisely why products get it wrong. Announce at sentence/paragraph/completion boundaries and let the user choose the granularity.
  - Using aria-live="assertive" or ariaNotify priority:'high' for model output. Assertive interrupts whatever the user is currently reading. AI output is almost never urgent enough to justify interruption, and repeated interruption is one of the fastest ways to make a screen reader user abandon a product.
  - Reaching for ariaNotify() as the primary announcement channel because it is easier than live regions. MDN reports it is not Baseline; more importantly, the accessibility community is already warning about it. Mat Marquis compares it to alert(), and screen reader user Samuel Proulx has publicly asked for the ability to 'go into the website properties and deny permission' if sites abuse it. A layer shipped to many products multiplies whatever mistake it encodes.
  - Claiming WCAG conformance for plain-language output. Reading Level is 3.1.5, Level AAA. Marketing a simplifier as 'WCAG compliance' is the kind of overclaim that gets a tool grouped with accessibility overlays — the category the disability community most distrusts — and will be caught immediately by procurement reviewers.
  - Building the portable profile on WAI-Adapt content attributes because the spec's stated goals match Clara's. Those attributes were stripped out by the W3C TAG; only `adapt-symbol` survived, and even that has unfinalised implementations three years into CR. Betting the profile format on it means betting on a spec that has not moved since January 2023.
  - A blanket 'generative AI is an authoring tool, therefore ATAG applies' claim. ATAG's Note 4 explicitly rules out applications where the person is the only consumer of the content. Anyone who reads the glossary will find the carve-out, and the overclaim discredits the genuinely strong narrower argument about publication and agent workflows.
  - Overriding the operating system's accessibility settings with the layer's own preferences. EN 301 549 11.7 requires software to follow platform preferences for colour, contrast, font type, font size, units and focus cursor except where the user overrides them. An embedded layer that imposes its own font sizing or contrast on top of a user's OS settings actively makes things worse for the people most likely to have configured those settings.
  - Auto-simplifying by default with no path back to the original. W3C's own AI-accessibility draft documents that generative AI fails at literal language, tenses and nested clauses — the 'it snowed sheep hair' example is in the draft. A layer that silently replaces authoritative text (a dosage, a legal obligation, a deadline) with a plausible simplification is a safety hazard, not an accessibility feature. The original must be one interaction away, always.
  - Presenting AI as the fix for accessibility. The 2023 W3C symposium recorded that LLMs cannot assess their own confidence and lack 'self awareness of what's wrong, what is right', and that statistical systems structurally disadvantage outliers — Treviranus: 'Disability is the Achilles' heel of AI applying statistical reasoning in disability.' A layer positioned as 'AI solves accessibility' will be read by disabled reviewers as overlay marketing; positioned as 'deterministic checking of AI output', it reads as the opposite.
  - Using role="feed" for the chat transcript because it looks like the closest APG pattern. Feed is an interoperability contract for infinitely-scrolling articles browsed in reading mode, with Page Up/Down article navigation. Applying it to turns in a conversation gives screen reader users a navigation model that does not match what they are reading.
  - Pitching EU legal necessity to model operators. The EAA's service list does not include general-purpose software or AI services, so most LLM chat products are out of scope as products in their own right. Leading with a compliance threat that does not apply will damage credibility with exactly the legal and procurement people who need to approve adoption.
OPEN QUESTIONS:
  - Is there any W3C or WAI guidance anywhere on how assistive technology should handle incrementally streamed text? I found none — no APG practice page (the live-regions URL 404s), no WCAG technique beyond ARIA23's static append example, nothing in NAUR or the COGA voice module. The widely repeated developer advice to 'suppress the live region during streaming and announce the complete message once generation finishes' appears only in secondary blog sources I could not trace to a primary source, so I have not treated it as evidence. This is the single biggest empirical gap for Clara's streaming design and should be settled by screen reader testing, not by citation.
  - Has any LLM vendor published an ATAG 2.0 conformance claim, or an Accessibility Conformance Report (VPAT/ACR) covering ATAG or EN 301 549 clause 11.8, for a chat product? I could not verify either way within this dimension's scope. If the answer is genuinely 'none', that is a strong, checkable claim for the outreach letter — but it needs to be confirmed by directly checking the published ACRs of the major operators rather than asserted.
  - The exact normative text of EN 301 549 V4.1.1 (2026-09) could not be retrieved: ETSI returned HTTP 403 for every deliver/ PDF and for its own landing pages. The v4.1.1 clause 11.7 and 11.8 wording, and whether the WCAG 2.2 uplift changed the software-clause structure, are unverified. The clause text I have cited (11.7, 11.8.2) is from v3.2.1 via secondary but expert sources (Hidde de Vries; the Government of Canada technical summary).
  - The date on which EN 301 549 V4.1.1 will be cited in the Official Journal — reported as around November 2026 — comes only from commercial accessibility-vendor blogs, not from a primary EC or ETSI source. The European Commission page I did retrieve still names v3.2.1, harmonised 18 August 2021, as current. Treat any 'WCAG 2.2 is legally required in the EU' framing as not yet true.
  - Will NAUR be revived? It is absent from the 2025–2027 APA charter and unchanged since September 2022, but I found no APA or RQTF statement explicitly retiring or deferring it. The RQTF's own page lists no publications at all. Whether the working group considers it finished, superseded by the COGA voice module, or simply dormant is unknown, and it matters for whether investing in a NAUR conformance table has durable value.
  - Has ETSI resolved issue #23 on whether clause 11.7 applies to websites and mobile applications? The issue as retrieved shows only the submissions from Pär Lannerö and Monika Miazdrová with no recorded resolution or date. If 11.7 is narrowed or removed for web and mobile in v4.1.1, one of the regulatory arguments for a preference layer weakens considerably.
  - Have ISO/IEC 24751 Parts 1–3 been revised since 2008? Searches returned only the 2008 editions for Parts 1–3 and 2023 for Part 4, but ISO's catalogue pages returned HTTP 403 so I could not confirm current status, withdrawal, or an active revision project. I also found no adoption data for AccessForAll outside e-learning, so the claim that it has 'essentially no mainstream adoption' is an inference from absence of evidence rather than a documented finding.
  - Does the ARIA WG or the APG task force have an open issue or plan for a chat/conversational pattern? I confirmed no such pattern exists today but did not search the w3c/aria-practices issue tracker for in-flight proposals. Worth checking before investing in writing one, both to avoid duplicating work and to find the right thread to contribute to.
  - Whether AI-generated content provenance or labelling has any accessibility-specific treatment in W3C work. The 2023 symposium recommended 'transparency labeling for machine-generated content', but I found no follow-through in any spec or draft — including the AI-accessibility Editor's Draft, whose harms section is still a placeholder. If Clara surfaces verification results, the labelling vocabulary is currently unstandardised and Clara would be inventing it.
MISSED ANGLE (per verifier): The entire regulatory analysis is EU-only, and it therefore misses the jurisdiction where dated, enforceable obligations actually drive procurement decisions today: the United States. Two specific gaps. (1) Section 508 of the Rehabilitation Act binds federal agencies and, through the FAR, everything they buy — meaning a US federal buyer of an AI chat product has a live accessibility obligation right now, with no EAA-style scope debate about whether general-purpose software counts. (2) The DOJ ADA Title II web rule (finalised 2024, effective June 2024, WCAG 2.1 AA) applies to state and local government web content and mobile apps, which covers every public university, city service portal and school district deploying an AI assistant. Critically, the deadlines MOVED this year and the pass would have had them wrong: DOJ published an interim final rule effective 20 April 2026 extending compliance to 26 April 2027 for entities serving populations of 50,000 or more and 26 April 2028 for smaller entities and special districts, with comments open to 22 June 2026. That is a concrete, near-term, named-buyer pipeline the pass did not surface. Secondary to this, the pass also missed the EU AI Act Article 16(l) cross-reference, which imports the accessibility requirements of Directives 2016/2102 and 2019/882 into the obligations of high-risk AI system providers — the one place in EU law where an AI-specific accessibility duty already exists. And on the standards side rather than the legal side, the pass never examined the APA's actual live normative track — the WAI-Adapt Symbols, Help and Support, and Tools modules, plus the Specification for Spoken Presentation in HTML — which are the only things in the charter that will become Recommendations, and which a preference-and-adaptation layer could plausibly implement and cite rather than building on a frozen 2022 Note.

### Prior art: portable accessibility preference profiles
FINDINGS:
  - ISO/IEC 24751-2:2008 already standardises exactly what Clara's profile does — a machine-readable statement of a person's accessibility needs and preferences — and it is a live, currently-confirmed International Standard, not a dead one. Clara's draft therefore does re-tread standardised ground at the concept level.
    (ISO/IEC 24751-2:2008 — ISO committee record (and ISO/IEC 24751-1:2008), 2008 (reconfirmed 2026) — https://committee.iso.org/es/sites/isoorg/contents/data/standard/04/36/43603.html; confidence high)
  - The user-profile half of AccessForAll has effectively no field implementation, and peer-reviewed evidence says so directly. Adopting ISO 24751-2 would buy Clara standards legitimacy but no ecosystem.
    (The use of accessibility metadata in e-learning environments: a systematic literature review, 2022 — https://ebuah.uah.es/dspace/handle/10017/63764; confidence high)
  - 1EdTech's AccessForAll PNP — the most actively maintained descendant of ISO 24751-2, and the closest thing to a shipping portable preference profile — has never reached Final Release in 14 years, and 1EdTech does not certify PNP implementations at all.
    (1EdTech QTI v3 Conformance and Certification; 1EdTech AccessForAll Specification Index, 2023 (index 2012–2024) — https://www.imsglobal.org/spec/qti/v3p0/conf; confidence high)
  - The half of AccessForAll that DID succeed at web scale is the resource-description half, not the user-profile half — and it succeeded precisely by being cut down and rehosted on a vocabulary the mainstream already consumed (schema.org). This is the single most transferable adoption lesson for Clara.
    (Schema.org Accessibility Properties for Discoverability Vocabulary (W3C CG Final Report); EPUB Accessibility 1.1 (W3C Recommendation), 2024 — https://w3c.github.io/cg-reports/a11y-discov-vocab/CG-FINAL-vocabulary-20241209/; confidence high)
  - GPII's 'preferences follow the user' infrastructure is not merely under-adopted — its public services are offline. Anything Clara builds that depends on a hosted preference server or hosted registry has a demonstrated durability failure mode.
    (GPII GitHub organisation; Raising the Floor — GPII; direct HTTP liveness checks, 2020–2026 — https://github.com/GPII; confidence high)
  - Morphic, GPII's surviving product, achieves portability by installing an agent on every machine rather than by any standard. Its 'preferences follow you' promise is bounded by installed base, and it is a desktop app — it does not reach web or mobile apps at all.
    (Morphic — Save and Change/Apply Setups; Vanderheiden & Jordan, 'An Open-source Tool for Simplifying Computer and Assistive Technology Use', ASSETS 2021, 2021–2026 — https://morphic.org/save-apply-setups/; confidence high)
  - The W3C TAG formally rejected the closest web-standards analogue to Clara's profile — W3C Personalization Semantics — closing the design review 'unsatisfied' and arguing that a single broad accessibility-preference vocabulary is the wrong architecture. Their stated reasons apply almost verbatim to a broad LLM accessibility profile.
    (W3C TAG design review #476: Personalization Semantics Explainer and Module 1, 2020–2021 — https://github.com/w3ctag/design-reviews/issues/476; confidence high)
  - The consequence of that critique is measurable: WAI-Adapt was cut to a single attribute and has then sat frozen for over three and a half years with roughly two production implementations. A rich preference vocabulary is not a viable path through W3C.
    (W3C transitions #478 (CR request); WAI-Adapt: Symbols Module publication history; w3c/adapt Implementations wiki, 2018–2023 — https://www.w3.org/standards/history/adapt-symbols/; confidence high)
  - The one portable preference mechanism that actually won is the CSS user-preference media query — and it won because it is OS-sourced, zero-configuration and a handful of enumerated values. The moment the vocabulary got richer, adoption collapsed. This is the sharpest available guide to how big Clara's profile should be.
    (Web Almanac 2024 — Accessibility; Media Queries Level 5; Can I Use / MDN, 2024–2026 — https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2024/accessibility; confidence high)
  - Every OS-level accessibility API is read-only, per-signal and boolean-ish — none of them is a profile, none is transferable between vendors, and Android is materially worse than iOS. Clara can auto-detect a few signals for free but cannot obtain a profile from the platform.
    (Apple UIAccessibility documentation; Eevis Panula, 'Android, Animations and Reduced Motion', 2022–2026 — https://developer.apple.com/documentation/uikit/uiaccessibility; confidence high)
  - The Web Preferences API (navigator.preferences / PreferenceManager) is the one emerging mechanism that would let a web app persist an accessibility override that feeds back into the CSS media queries — but it is Chrome-only behind a flag and shipped in no other engine. Clara must not depend on it.
    (MDN PreferenceManager + mdn/browser-compat-data api/PreferenceManager.json; CSS Media Queries Level 5 §13, 2026 — https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/PreferenceManager; confidence high)
  - An accessibility profile is disability disclosure, and the disability community has already condemned the closest commercial analogue — settings-overlay widgets — partly on exactly that ground. A profile that travels to a model operator is a GDPR Article 9 special-category disclosure to a third party.
    (Overlay Fact Sheet; WebAIM Survey of Web Accessibility Practitioners #3; Aboulafia, 'Internet Privacy Is A Disability Rights Issue', 2021–2024 — https://overlayfactsheet.com/en/; confidence high)
  - Users already have populated accessibility settings at the OS level in large numbers, which means the winning move is to derive from what exists rather than ask people to author a new profile by hand. Hand-authored profiles are the step that has repeatedly not happened.
    (Appt — Accessibility statistics (Netherlands, ~5M users), 2025 — https://appt.org/en/stats; confidence medium)
  - There is genuinely no established prior art for accessibility preferences aimed at AI output — the field is open, but the only existing proposals are non-normative notes and an unevaluated concept paper. Clara's `verify.*` fields in particular have no precedent in any of the profile standards.
    (W3C Accessibility of machine learning and generative AI (ED); W3C NAUR; Ramineni et al. arXiv:2608.24898; Mankoff et al. arXiv:2503.14522, 2022–2026 — https://w3c.github.io/ai-accessibility/; confidence high)
  - The W3C AAC Symbols Registry is complementary to, not duplicative of, Clara's `symbols.set` field — W3C standardises the concept identifier, Clara standardises the rendering set. Clara should adopt the registry's concept indices rather than treat symbol identity as free text.
    (W3C AAC Symbols Registry (Draft Registry); WAI-Adapt: Symbols Module, 2022 — https://www.w3.org/TR/aac-registry/; confidence medium)
  - The premise that no AI chat product ships any vision-accessibility settings is now slightly too strong — ChatGPT has shipped an appearance/contrast control that follows the OS accessibility contrast preference. The gap is real but narrower than 'nothing exists', and the honest framing is that what exists is per-vendor, presentation-only, and non-portable.
    (OpenAI Help Center — Updating your visual experience on ChatGPT; anthropics/claude-code issue #11002, 2025 — https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/issues/11002; confidence low)
DESIGN IMPLICATIONS:
  - Do not re-scope Clara's profile onto ISO 24751-2 or AfA PNP wholesale — but do not pretend the vocabulary is novel either. The honest position, and the one that survives contact with reviewers: cite ISO/IEC 24751-2:2008 and 1EdTech AfA PNP v3.0 in docs/accessibility-profile.md as the prior art for `language`, `reading.level`, `reading.define_hard_words` and `symbols`, name-map Clara's fields to their AfA equivalents (e.g. AfA's simplified-language and language-of-interface attributes) in an appendix, and state plainly why Clara does not adopt their transport (paywalled ISO text, e-learning scoping, XML/OAuth REST service model, no certification programme, no field implementations).
  - Copy the schema.org playbook, not the ISO playbook. The only piece of AccessForAll that reached mass adoption did so by being cut to seven properties and rehosted on a vocabulary mainstream consumers already parsed — and is now mandatory in EPUB Accessibility 1.1. Clara's equivalent move is to keep the profile at roughly its current size and get it consumed by something that already exists (an OpenAI-compatible request field, a browser extension, a client library), rather than to grow it toward completeness.
  - Treat the TAG's 2021 verdict as a design review of Clara's own spec. Their three objections all land: a single broad vocabulary with many heterogeneous programmatic consumers; consumers not brought in as stakeholders; and overlap with mechanisms that already exist. Concretely: (a) split the spec into a tiny stable core and clearly-marked extensions; (b) get at least one non-Clara consumer to implement before calling anything v1.0; (c) explicitly document where `format.*` overlaps CSS prefers-* and OS settings and say which layer owns which.
  - Never ship a hosted profile server, and say so in the spec. ds.gpii.net, ul.gpii.net and unifiedlisting.org are all offline today; gpii.net redirects to an org page. A profile that lives as a local JSON file the user owns, rendered to instructions client-side, has no single point of failure. If sync is ever added, make it bring-your-own-storage rather than a Clara-operated service.
  - Seed the profile from signals that already exist rather than asking a person to author one. Roughly half of iOS and three quarters of Android users already have at least one accessibility setting on; browsers hand you prefers-reduced-motion, prefers-contrast, prefers-color-scheme, forced-colors and Accept-Language for free. The web/extension surfaces should offer a pre-filled starter profile derived from those, with the user confirming and correcting — the hand-authoring step is precisely where GPII-style personalization stalled.
  - Keep the profile client-side by default and make transmission an explicit, per-destination choice. A profile stating reading level, symbol-set preference and 'define hard words' is special-category disability data under GDPR Article 9 once it reaches a model operator. Mode A (render to instructions locally, user pastes) should be the documented default for third-party services; Mode B (send to a Clara-operated or self-hosted proxy) should carry an explicit disclosure notice about what is being revealed and to whom.
  - Adopt W3C AAC Symbols Registry BCI concept indices as the concept layer, and keep `symbols.set` only as the rendering-set selector. This resolves the governance question — Clara does not have to run a symbol registry — and makes Clara's symbol output interoperable with WAI-Adapt's `adapt-symbol` attribute, which is the one piece of that spec that survived TAG review.
  - Lead with `verify.*`, because it is the only genuinely novel part. Nothing in ISO 24751, AfA PNP, WAI-Adapt, CSS media queries or any OS API expresses 'I need the simplification checked against the source and to be told inline when facts were lost.' Position the profile publicly as 'AfA-style needs plus a verification contract', not as a new needs vocabulary — the second framing invites the reinvention charge, the first does not.
  - Do not depend on navigator.preferences / PreferenceManager. It is Chrome 120+ behind #enable-experimental-web-platform-features, absent in Firefox and Safari. Read the CSS media queries for detection; use localStorage or the extension's own storage for overrides.
  - Model the profile as read-only preference signals plus explicit user override, exactly as every OS API does. Apple's UIAccessibility, Windows UISettings/AccessibilitySettings and macOS NSWorkspace all expose read-only state plus change notifications; the equivalent contract for Clara is that a stated preference is applied but a per-conversation user action outranks it — which the draft already gets right in rule 4 and should keep.
  - Fix the Android asymmetry in any mobile/embedded guidance. There is no first-class Android reduce-motion API; implementers must read Settings.Global.ANIMATOR_DURATION_SCALE inside a try/catch and treat 0f as reduced. If Clara ships platform detection helpers, ship that one, because most developers will otherwise silently detect nothing on Android.
  - Design so the layer cannot be mistaken for an overlay. The Overlay Fact Sheet's two core charges — duplicating settings the user already has, and detecting AT use without consent — are the exact charges a chat-embedded accessibility layer will attract. Defences to build in from the start: never override an OS or browser preference, never sniff for assistive technology, never persist cross-site state without consent, and never claim conformance the layer does not deliver.
PITFALLS:
  - Adopting ISO/IEC 24751-2 or AfA PNP as the wire format 'because it's a standard'. It is a paywalled, e-learning-scoped, XML-and-OAuth-REST design whose own steward does not certify implementations and which a 746-document systematic review found essentially unused in practice. Standards legitimacy without an ecosystem buys nothing and costs implementability.
  - Growing the profile toward completeness. Every richer preference vocabulary in this lineage — Personalization Semantics Content Module, AfA v3.0, ISO 24751-2's display/control/content taxonomy — either got cut to one attribute, stayed a draft for 14 years, or was never implemented. Meanwhile the six-value CSS set won. Field count is negatively correlated with adoption here.
  - Running a hosted profile server or registry to make preferences 'follow the user'. This is the GPII vision literally, and its public endpoints are dead. A hosted dependency converts a portable file into a service outage waiting to happen, and it is the piece funders stop paying for first.
  - Sending the profile to third-party model operators by default. It is disability disclosure to a commercial party, GDPR Article 9 special-category data, and precisely the practice the Overlay Fact Sheet's 1,031 signatories condemn in overlays. 'It's just preferences' is not a defence when the preferences are diagnostic.
  - Requiring the user to author the JSON. The population least able to re-explain their needs in every chat is also least likely to hand-write a schema-valid file; the whole point is to remove that labour, not relocate it. A profile that only power users can produce reproduces GPII's adoption curve.
  - Building on navigator.preferences / PreferenceManager, or on WAI-Adapt Content Module attributes like adapt-simplification and adapt-numberfree. The first is Chrome-only behind a flag; the second were dropped from the spec after TAG review and formally do not exist.
  - Duplicating OS and browser controls inside the chat UI. Font size, contrast and motion are already the user's, already populated, and already exposed to web pages. Re-implementing them is the defining overlay antipattern, and it makes the genuinely new parts (reading level, verification) look like more of the same.
  - Free-text symbol-set names (`"arasaac"`, `"mulberry"`) as the only symbol identity. Set names are not concept identifiers; without a registry-backed concept index the field cannot be interoperable, and Clara would end up owning a symbol registry it does not want to own. ISO/IEC 24751-4:2023 exists as a whole standard part precisely because AccessForAll discovered it needed a registry server.
  - Claiming the profile is 'honoured' when Mode A only injects instructions. The draft is already candid about this — do not let marketing erode it. Rule 3 ('never fail silently') is the most defensible thing in the spec and is what distinguishes it from overlay vendors' compliance claims.
  - Asserting that no AI product ships any vision-accessibility settings. ChatGPT now has an Appearance pane with a Contrast option that follows the OS accessibility contrast preference. The accurate and still-damning claim is that what exists is per-vendor, presentation-only, non-portable, and does nothing about the readability of the generated text itself.

MISSED ANGLE (per verifier): The pass never examined the one large-scale natural experiment on Clara's actual mechanism — a portable, machine-readable user preference signal transmitted to every service the user touches — outside the accessibility domain. Do Not Track and Global Privacy Control are the closest structural analogues to what Clara proposes, and they ran the same experiment to opposite outcomes: DNT was a technically clean, W3C-processed header that collapsed because nothing obliged anyone to honour it, while GPC — technically simpler and far less mature as a spec — achieved real adoption because CCPA/CPRA regulations made ignoring it unlawful and California's AB 3048 and the Sephora enforcement action gave it teeth. The lesson transfers directly and is the single most decision-relevant thing missing: adoption of a portable preference signal tracks legal enforceability, not spec quality or vocabulary richness, which reframes every finding above (the WAI-Adapt freeze, the AfA PNP stall, the absence of certification) as evidence about incentives rather than about design. The unexamined corollary is the regulatory hook itself — whether the European Accessibility Act (applicable 28 June 2025), the DOJ ADA Title II web rule, or the EU AI Act's transparency and accessibility provisions create any obligation on an AI vendor to accept a user-supplied accessibility preference, which is what would determine whether Clara can ever be more than opt-in. Tied to that, and equally absent: a disability preference profile is special-category personal data under GDPR Article 9, so a profile designed to be transmitted to arbitrary third-party AI vendors raises explicit-consent, purpose-limitation and data-minimisation problems that constrain the format itself (local-first, per-session, selective disclosure) — Mankoff et al. flag the disclosure risk but the pass did not carry it through to design or legal consequences.

### Blind/low-vision users × generative AI (empirical)
FINDINGS:
  - Mainstream LLM chat UIs (ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, Gemini) contain concrete, enumerable screen-reader defects: unlabeled copy/regenerate/downvote buttons, no headings/landmarks/regions, no shortcut to jump between prompts and responses, and no notification when generation finishes. All 19 blind participants demonstrated the unlabeled-button defect live. [verified: overstated → As of a Jan-March 2024 interview study (n=19 blind screen-reader users, live screen-share with screen-reader audio), ChatGPT (3.5 interface) and Claude showed four concrete defects: all 19 participants demonstrated that copy/regenerate/downvote buttons were unlabeled; neither tool provided heading labels, regions or landmarks, nor shortcuts to jump between previous and next prompts/responses; and there was no notification when generation finished, forcing users to 'dig for it'. Copilot and Bard were criticised in the same study for a different defect (sample-prompt and ad clutter before the chat field). Gemini was not the subject of any defect finding. Whether these specific defects persist in 2026 needs a fresh audit.]
    ("I look at it as the king of knowledge": How Blind People Use and Understand Generative AI Tools (ASSETS '24), 2024 — https://maitraye.github.io/files/papers/BLV_GenAI_ASSETS24.pdf; confidence high)
  - The richness of LLM output is a double-edged sword: more detailed, fluent responses are MORE believable and therefore reduce fact-checking. Making an accessibility layer produce richer output without verification would increase harm, not reduce it.
    ("I look at it as the king of knowledge": How Blind People Use and Understand Generative AI Tools (ASSETS '24), 2024 — https://maitraye.github.io/files/papers/BLV_GenAI_ASSETS24.pdf; confidence high)
  - Blind users decide whether to verify using four competing factors — context of use, stakes, verifiability, and believability — and verifiability is structurally lower for them than for sighted users, because checking a visual claim requires recruiting a sighted person. This is the asymmetric-harm mechanism, stated explicitly in the literature.
    ("I look at it as the king of knowledge": How Blind People Use and Understand Generative AI Tools (ASSETS '24), 2024 — https://maitraye.github.io/files/papers/BLV_GenAI_ASSETS24.pdf; confidence high)
  - An accessibility defect can directly cause a false mental model of the AI. Because upvote/downvote buttons were unlabeled, blind users did not know they existed, and instead believed that typing corrections into the chat would train the model for future users — so they spent unpaid advocacy labour correcting ableist responses in a channel that does nothing.
    ("I look at it as the king of knowledge": How Blind People Use and Understand Generative AI Tools (ASSETS '24), 2024 — https://maitraye.github.io/files/papers/BLV_GenAI_ASSETS24.pdf; confidence high)
  - How uncertainty is FRAMED changes blind users' trust, but the numeric-vs-verbal format does not. Negatively framed hedges ('There's a small chance I could be wrong, but I think that's...') significantly reduced trust in incongruent captions while preserving reliance on congruent ones; positively framed hedges ('I'm 98% sure') did not.
    (Understanding Blind People's Experiences with Computer-Generated Captions of Social Media Images (CHI 2017), 2017 — https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/captions_chi2017.pdf; confidence high)
  - Showing disagreement across multiple model responses is the single best-evidenced intervention: it increased blind users' identification of unreliable claims by 4.9x and significantly lowered perceived reliability. But the output must be AGGREGATED into a summary — a raw list of model outputs was rejected by 13 of 15 participants as too much to hold in memory via a screen reader.
    (Surfacing Variations to Calibrate Perceived Reliability of MLLM-generated Image Descriptions (ASSETS '25), 2025 — https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.15692; confidence high)
  - There is a measured gap between how much blind users trust MLLM assistance and how accurate it actually is, and the gap widens across a conversation: initial descriptions were 91.8% flawless but only 56.6% of follow-up answers were fully correct, spatial localisation was 42.9% correct, and text/number extraction was 34.6% incorrect — yet users still rated trust 3.76/5.
    (How Multimodal Large Language Models Support Access to Visual Information: A Diary Study With Blind and Low Vision People (CHI 2026), 2026 — https://arxiv.org/html/2602.13469; confidence high)
  - Trust in AI visual assistance rose sharply when MLLMs replaced classic computer vision — from 2.43/4 trust and 2.76/5 satisfaction in 2024 to 3.76/5 trust and 4.13/5 satisfaction in the same lab's later study — and blind users now extend that trust into high-stakes scenarios such as medical dosage advice.
    (Investigating Use Cases of AI-Powered Scene Description Applications for Blind and Low Vision People (CHI 2024); Towards Understanding the Use of MLLM-Enabled Applications (CHI 2025 EA), 2024 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.15604; confidence high)
  - Blind users are divided on numeric confidence scores, and some reject them on validity grounds — the model has no ground truth to score itself against. Confidence display is NOT a settled win, and the research community's recommendation has shifted to 'explain the explanation' rather than emit a bare number.
    (Misfitting With AI: How Blind People Verify and Contest AI Errors (ASSETS '24), 2024 — https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10557171; confidence high)
  - Blind users want to CONTEST AI output conversationally, the way they negotiate with a sighted helper — and current tools often refuse to update. In one documented case Be My AI kept describing a white cane as a 'hiking pole' even after the user directly challenged it.
    (Misfitting With AI: How Blind People Verify and Contest AI Errors (ASSETS '24), 2024 — https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10557171; confidence high)
  - Blind users systematically blame themselves for model errors — attributing bad output to their photo or prompt rather than to model limits — which suppresses the error signal a product would otherwise receive.
    ("I look at it as the king of knowledge" (ASSETS '24); "This is My Fault", Really? Understanding BLV People's Perception of Hallucination in LVLMs (UIST '25), 2024 — https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3746059.3747597; confidence high)
  - What blind users want described is scenario-dependent (information goal + where the image was found), not fixed — which means a one-size-fits-all description setting is known to be wrong, and a portable per-user/per-context profile is the empirically supported alternative.
    (Going Beyond One-Size-Fits-All Image Descriptions to Satisfy the Information Wants of People Who are Blind or Have Low Vision (ASSETS '21), 2021 — https://cs.stanford.edu/~merrie/papers/assets2021_scenarios.pdf; confidence high)
  - Streamed and proactive AI output is an accessibility hazard for screen reader and braille users: visually impaired developers asked for 'AI timeouts' because the continuous stream of suggestions overwhelmed speech output and broke the sequential navigation screen readers depend on.
    (The Impact of Generative AI Coding Assistants on Developers Who Are Visually Impaired, 2025 — https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16491; confidence high)
  - Conversational fluency actively masks unreliability in real-time assistance: with ChatGPT live video, BVI participants perceived the system as human-like yet got inaccurate spatial and distance information, and the system made assumptions about their visual abilities that created safety risks.
    (Probing the Gaps in ChatGPT Live Video Chat for Real-World Assistance for People who are Blind or Visually Impaired, 2025 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.03651; confidence high)
  - Non-English and non-Western blind users are harmed twice over: AI visual assistance fails on their languages and cultural artifacts, with commercial Arabic OCR estimated below 75% accuracy for printed text against 99% for printed English.
    (Misfitting With AI: How Blind People Verify and Contest AI Errors (ASSETS '24), 2024 — https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10557171; confidence medium)
  - The blind community is not uniformly credulous: a CHI 2025 interview study frames blind screen reader users as bringing an 'everyday uncertainty' mindset — habitual skepticism toward AI-, human-, and information sources alike — which complicates the simpler 'blind users overtrust AI' narrative that dominates design discourse.
    (Everyday Uncertainty: How Blind People Use GenAI Tools for Information Access (CHI '25), 2025 — https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3706598.3713433; confidence medium)
  - The deployment base is large enough that layer-level design choices affect roughly a million blind and low-vision people through one product alone — Be My Eyes reports ~1.09M blind/low-vision users and ~10.9M volunteers across 150+ countries and 180+ languages, with Be My AI (GPT-4V) launched March 2023.
    (Be My Eyes — About, 2026 — https://www.bemyeyes.com/business/about/; confidence medium)
DESIGN IMPLICATIONS:
  - Ship the boring screen-reader fixes as the layer's floor, because they are enumerable and nobody has done them: label every action control, expose headings/landmarks/regions per turn, provide keyboard shortcuts to jump between previous/next prompt and response, and fire a polite live-region announcement when generation completes. Adnin & Das found all four missing in ChatGPT and Claude, and all 19 participants hit the unlabeled-button defect. This is the highest-certainty, lowest-cost part of Clara's proposition.
  - Make Clara's verification output the primary accessibility feature, not a footnote. The single best-evidenced intervention in this literature is surfacing disagreement (4.9x more unreliable claims identified, Chen et al. 2025). Clara already does deterministic cross-checking of numbers, dates, negations and obligations — expose that as an aggregated 'what is contested / what is agreed' summary attached to each response, and let a screen reader jump straight to the contested spans.
  - Aggregate, never enumerate. 13 of 15 BLV participants rejected a raw list of model outputs as unmanageable via screen reader; 11 of 15 preferred a summary that names agreements, disagreements and unique mentions. Clara's verifier report should default to a short 'agreed / disagreed' digest with drill-down on demand, not a diff dump.
  - Frame uncertainty negatively and per-claim, not positively and globally. MacLeod et al. showed 'There's a small chance I could be wrong, but...' significantly reduced trust in wrong captions while preserving reliance on right ones, whereas 'I'm 98% sure' did not. Attach the hedge to the specific unverified claim (the number, the date, the obligation) rather than as a blanket disclaimer on the turn — blanket disclaimers had no effect on trust in that experiment.
  - Do not ship a bare numeric confidence score as the flagship uncertainty signal. Blind users are split on it and some reject it as invalid ('unless it has some way to measure against the original document... there's really no way to calculate those statistics'). Clara's advantage is that its verifier DOES have a ground truth — the source text — so it can say 'this number does not appear in the source' rather than 'I am 80% confident'. Say what was checked and against what; that is 'explaining the explanation' as Alharbi et al. recommend.
  - Distinguish 'the model may be wrong' from 'your input was unreadable'. Blind users default to self-blame for model errors, which both misleads them and starves the product of error signal. Clara should explicitly attribute failures to source, model, or input, and never present 'try again with a better photo/prompt' as the default explanation.
  - Make verbosity and content selection a profile parameter, not a fixed style. Stangl et al. (n=28, 700 responses) established that content wants shift by information goal and source; the CHI 2026 diary study found divergent verbosity preferences within the same 20 participants. This is a direct empirical mandate for Clara's portable JSON accessibility profile — carry detail level, preferred content categories, and hedging style across products.
  - Give the user an interrupt and a mute. Visually impaired developers explicitly asked for 'AI timeouts' because streamed proactive output overwhelms speech and braille output and breaks sequential navigation. Any Clara overlay that injects simplified text must be user-triggered or interruptible, must not steal focus, and must not re-announce on every token.
  - Build contestation into the loop, since users want to interrogate a description the way they negotiate with a sighted helper — and current tools sometimes refuse to update (the white cane that stayed a 'hiking pole'). A 'challenge this claim' affordance that re-runs verification against the source is closer to what users described than a thumbs-up/down.
  - Design for cross-language failure explicitly. OCR and object recognition degrade badly outside English and Western artifacts (Arabic printed-text OCR under 75% vs 99% English). Clara already ships 5 UI languages; the verifier should flag low-confidence extraction on non-Latin scripts rather than passing through a fluent, wrong rendering.
  - Target the follow-up turns, not just the first answer. The diary study found first descriptions 91.8% flawless but follow-up answers only 56.6% correct and spatial localisation 42.9% correct — trust is set by turn one and spent on turns two through ten. Verification should run on every turn, and be most aggressive on the categories that fail most: numbers, text extraction, and spatial claims.
  - Position the layer for the high-stakes cases users actually bring it: medication verification, food safety, financial documents, dosages and expiry dates. These recur across every study and map exactly onto Clara's existing number/date/negation checks.
PITFALLS:
  - Making descriptions richer and more fluent to be 'more helpful'. Adnin & Das found richness increases believability and therefore suppresses fact-checking — the double-edged sword. A more articulate accessibility layer with no verification is a net harm.
  - Positive-framed confidence ('I'm 90% sure that's...'). MacLeod et al. found this did NOT induce warranted skepticism on wrong captions; only negative framing did. The industry default is positive framing.
  - Treating hedging language as an unambiguous safety signal. Chen et al. found participants called verbal hedges 'ambiguous' and 'confusing', 4 of 15 preferred no indicator at all — and, critically, P6 judged a description reliable BECAUSE it lacked hedges ('it didn't say perhaps or there may be, it sounded more factual') when that image in fact contained the most factual errors. Absence of hedging gets read as accuracy.
  - Bare self-reported confidence scores. Blind users questioned their validity outright, since the model has no ground truth to measure against, and some found parsing an extra number an unwelcome workflow interruption ('Either it's working or it's not working. I don't really need too much information about it').
  - Feedback prompts after every interaction. P12: constant post-scan feedback requests 'would drive me crazy'. P9 framed feedback as labour: 'pay me well to sit down and honestly give my time and energy, and I will.' A thumbs-up/down on every turn is both fatiguing and, per Alharbi et al., of unclear accountability value against an automated system.
  - Raw lists of alternative outputs. 13 of 15 participants found list presentation unusable via screen reader because they had to hold every variant in memory; the benefit of variation comes only when it is aggregated.
  - Explaining errors by blaming the input. 'Try a better photo' or 'try a clearer prompt' as the default reinforces a documented self-blame pattern in which blind users absorb model failure as personal failure, and invent false causal stories to explain it.
  - Humanlike voice and anthropomorphic persona as an accessibility feature. Both Alharbi et al. and Chang et al. found conversational fluency inflates trust and masks unreliable spatial reasoning; participants explicitly worried about taking output 'more realistically because it sounds human'.
  - Streaming/proactive suggestion by default. What reads as responsiveness on screen is a continuous interruption in speech or braille — hence the 'AI timeouts' request.
  - Removing capability to mitigate a harm. When Be My AI stopped describing faces for privacy reasons there was public outcry from blind users; Adnin & Das class this as trading a privacy harm for a quality-of-service harm. Safety guardrails in an accessibility layer need a disability-impact check, and the CHI 2026 diary study likewise flagged 'inconsistent privacy guardrails' as a user-reported gap.
  - Assuming blind users are uniformly naive about AI. Tang et al. describe a practiced 'everyday uncertainty' mindset; a layer that lectures users about AI fallibility, or that cannot be turned down, will read as patronising to experienced users while the overtrust risk concentrates in less experienced ones.
  - Designing only for totally blind screen-reader users. Nearly every sample in this literature is majority totally blind (Alharbi: 25 of 26; the CHI 2026 diary study is the exception at 13 blind / 7 low vision). Low-vision needs — magnification, contrast, reflow, font control in a chat UI — are almost entirely unstudied and would be silently omitted if the layer is built from this evidence alone.
OPEN QUESTIONS:
  - No study I found evaluates a THIRD-PARTY, embeddable accessibility layer for BLV users. Every system in this literature is either a vendor app (Be My AI, Seeing AI) or a bespoke research prototype (VisionPal, the variations prototype). Whether a layer adopted by a host product produces the same benefits — and whether host UIs would let it announce, focus and interrupt correctly — is untested.
  - Clara's actual mechanism (deterministic verification of numbers, dates, negations, obligations in TEXT) has never been tested with blind users. All BLV verification evidence is about image descriptions. The transfer is plausible — the diary study's worst categories were text/number extraction (34.6% incorrect) and dosages — but it is an inference, not a finding.
  - Low-vision users as distinct from blind users are badly under-sampled, so there is essentially no empirical basis for the magnification/contrast/reflow half of a 'vision accessibility layer' in an AI chat UI. This is the biggest gap relative to the project's framing observation about vision-accessibility settings on ordinary websites.
  - I could not retrieve any accessibility conformance report, VPAT comparison, or systematic screen-reader audit across the major AI chat products. The best available evidence is the incidental audit inside Adnin & Das (2024, ChatGPT 3.5/4, Claude, Copilot, Gemini) and a 2025 WCAG 2.2 evaluation by Leporini et al. whose full text I could not access (ACM DL 403). The claim 'no AI chat product ships a vision-accessibility layer' is consistent with everything I found but I did not verify it product-by-product.
  - Long-run trust calibration is unmeasured. Every study is a single session or two weeks. Whether negative framing or variation-surfacing still changes behaviour after months — or whether users habituate and start ignoring the warnings, as with cookie banners — is unknown.
  - Latency and cost of cross-model variation checking were not evaluated as adoption barriers, and Chen et al. note conveying variation in real-time or streaming contexts 'may be difficult'. Clara's overlay and voice modes are exactly those contexts.
  - Whether uncertainty signalling helps or hurts users who are multiply disabled (e.g. blind plus a cognitive disability) is raised in Alharbi et al. as a concern — P23 asked what happens for 'folks who don't type but who use augmentative communication aids' — but no study measures it. This matters for Clara because Easy Read and BLV needs may pull in opposite directions on verbosity.
  - Non-English BLV users of LLM interfaces are near-absent from the evidence; the cross-cultural findings concern OCR and object recognition, not chat UIs. Clara ships 5 UI languages with no data on whether verification framing translates.
  - The 4.9x variation-detection result comes from a single 15-participant within-subjects study by one group and has not been replicated. It is the strongest single number supporting Clara's thesis, so it is the one most worth treating cautiously.
MISSED ANGLE (per verifier): The entire evidence base is audio-screen-reader-centric and evaluates only IN-APP changes, which is the wrong shape for the thing you are building. Two concrete gaps. (1) Output modality: every study assumes a speech screen reader on a desktop or phone. Nobody in this set studies refreshable braille display users, for whom token-by-token streaming is a qualitatively different problem - a braille line re-renders on every aria-live update, so a streaming response plus a polite live region can be unreadable in a way that speech users never experience. Streaming generation itself is barely treated as an accessibility object in this literature; Adnin & Das only note the absence of a completion notification, not what happens to the live region during the 20 seconds before it. Low-vision users (magnification, reflow, contrast, and a completely different verification economics because residual vision partially restores the verifiability the whole asymmetric-verification argument depends on) are also nearly absent: 7 of 20 in one diary study, zero elsewhere. (2) Form factor: all five intervention studies (variation surfacing, negative framing, confidence ratings, camera guidance, contestation UI) evaluate changes made INSIDE the AI product by its own developers. None evaluates a third-party overlay or middleware layer sitting on someone else's chat DOM - which is what the project is. That leaves the load-bearing feasibility questions untested: whether an overlay can reliably identify and relabel unlabeled controls across a shipping product's frequently changing DOM, whether injecting your own landmarks and live regions conflicts with the host page's, and whether the announced result is coherent when the host app and the overlay both speak. Worth adding a WCAG 2.2 / EN 301 549 and European Accessibility Act scoping pass too - the pass has no regulatory grounding at all, and whether AI chat products are in scope determines whether an overlay is a stopgap or a permanent product."

### Cognitive & intellectual disability × AI
FINDINGS:
  - W3C COGA's 'Making Content Usable' gives concrete, testable targets that map almost one-to-one onto LLM output — break paragraphs over ~50 words; avoid sentences with more than one linking word ('and'/'but'); use the most common 1500 words; use literal language, not metaphor; provide a summary of long documents; separate each instruction. These are the closest thing to a spec for an AI accessibility layer.
    (Design Guide — Making Content Usable for People with Cognitive and Learning Disabilities (W3C Working Group Note), 2021 — https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/design_guide.html; confidence high)
  - There is NO W3C standard for machine-readable 'simplify this for me' preferences. WAI-Adapt has shipped only a Symbols Module (one attribute, adapt-symbol, carrying BCI concept codes) — it explicitly does not cover simplified language, and it has been stalled at Candidate Recommendation since January 2023 without the two user-agent implementations its own exit criteria require.
    (WAI-Adapt: Symbols Module (W3C Candidate Recommendation Snapshot), 2023 — https://www.w3.org/TR/personalization-semantics-content-1.0/; confidence high)
  - Default LLM chat output is written at roughly a college reading level — far above the grade-6 target for general public health material, and vastly above Easy Read. This is a measurable, reproducible property of the default, not an edge case.
    (Evaluating of the readability of ChatGPT generated responses on travel health risks (International Maritime Health), 2025 — https://journals.viamedica.pl/international_maritime_health/article/view/104400; confidence high)
  - LLM text simplification does improve comprehension for the general population, but the effect is modest — about 4 percentage points absolute — so a simplification layer should be sold as a real but incremental gain, not a solved problem.
    (LLM-based Text Simplification and its Effect on User Comprehension and Cognitive Load (Guidroz et al.), 2025 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.01980; confidence high)
  - Automatically simplified text is not interchangeable with manually simplified text for readers with intellectual disabilities, and the only reliable way to know whether simplification worked is comprehension questions — not readability scores, not difficulty ratings, not reading speed. [verified: overstated → In a CHI 2024 tablet study, comprehension outcomes for readers with intellectual disabilities differed significantly depending on both reader group and whether text was automatically or manually simplified — the two are not measurement-equivalent. Of the four measures tested, comprehension questions were the most reliable for this group; reading speed was still informative about reading behavior. Readability formulas were not evaluated.]
    (Digital Comprehensibility Assessment of Simplified Texts among Persons with Intellectual Disabilities (CHI '24), 2024 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13094; confidence high)
  - When LLMs generate Easy Read against a formal standard, they fail in specific, recurring, machine-checkable ways — grammatical agreement errors, inconsistent terminology for the same referent, and undefined technical terms — and the researchers state flatly that expert human validation is mandatory before deployment.
    (Exploring large language models to generate Easy to Read content (Frontiers in Computer Science / arXiv), 2024 — https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/computer-science/articles/10.3389/fcomp.2024.1394705/full; confidence high)
  - The evidence base for Easy Read itself is weak — the most rigorous RCT in the field found NO significant improvement in comprehension or retention, and intrinsic language ability predicted understanding better than the format did. Building an accessibility layer that assumes Easy Read reliably works would be building on contested ground.
    (Easy Read Health Information for People With Intellectual Disabilities: A Systematic Review of the Evidence (Journal of Applied Research in Intellectual Disabilities), 2026 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12893875/; confidence high)
  - Composing a prompt is itself a distinct cognitive barrier with three separable components — not knowing what the model can do, not knowing how to instruct it, and not being able to judge whether the answer is good. Any accessibility layer must address all three, and the third is the one nobody ships.
    (Bridging the Gulf of Envisioning: Cognitive Design Challenges in LLM Interfaces, 2023 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14459; confidence high)
  - Underspecified, multi-turn conversation — exactly the interaction style produced by someone who cannot compose a complete prompt — is the failure mode where LLMs degrade most, and the degradation is unreliability rather than incapacity. A model that answers a well-formed question correctly may not answer the same need correctly when it arrives in fragments.
    (LLMs Get Lost In Multi-Turn Conversation (Microsoft Research / Salesforce), 2025 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06120; confidence high)
  - Sycophancy is a systemic property of RLHF-trained assistants, and both humans and preference models sometimes prefer a convincingly-written sycophantic answer to a correct one. This means the model will tend to agree with a user who pushes back — which is the exact opposite of what a suggestible user needs.
    (Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models (Anthropic), 2023 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548; confidence high)
  - Adults with mild intellectual disabilities are measurably more suggestible to leading questions, confabulate more, and are more acquiescent (say yes regardless of content) than average-ability adults. Combined with model sycophancy, this creates a compounding failure: the user agrees with the model, the model agrees with the user, and nothing in the loop is checking truth.
    (Interrogative suggestibility, confabulation, and acquiescence in people with mild learning disabilities: implications for reliability during police interrogations (British Journal of Clinical Psychology, 32, 295–301), 1993 — https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.2044-8260.1993.tb01059.x; confidence medium)
  - When chatbots are stress-tested on questions that matter to disabled users, roughly a quarter of answers are actively harmful — and every model tested hallucinated, including inventing laws and inventing disability rights organisations to refer users to. Fabricated support referrals are a specific, under-discussed harm class.
    (Generating Confusion: Stress-Testing AI Chatbot Responses on Voting with a Disability (Center for Democracy & Technology), 2024 — https://cdt.org/insights/brief-generating-confusion-stress-testing-ai-chatbot-responses-on-voting-with-a-disability/; confidence high)
  - LLMs carry a large, measurable paternalism bias about people with intellectual disabilities — so an AI 'simplification' layer risks importing an infantilising register along with the shorter sentences. Simplification and condescension must be separated deliberately.
    (Identifying Implicit Bias in LLM-based Chat AI Toward People with Intellectual Disabilities (Disability and Health Journal / Special Olympics), 2026 — https://www.specialolympics.org/about/press-releases/is-ai-fair-new-evidence-suggests-bias-against-people-with-intellectual-disabilities-is-built-in; confidence medium)
  - Shorter is not automatically better. Medium-length responses (~42–150 words, mean 82) produced the best error detection when the model was wrong; both very short and very long responses made users worse at catching errors. A 'make it short' accessibility toggle can measurably reduce a user's ability to spot a false answer.
    (Not Too Short, Not Too Long: How LLM Response Length Shapes People's Critical Thinking in Error Detection (IUI MIRAGE workshop), 2026 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06878; confidence medium)
  - Autistic users find LLMs uniquely valuable precisely because they are literal and non-judgemental — but the documented risks are uncritical adoption of AI advice as behavioural instruction, and 'automated masking', where sustained reliance replaces the user's authentic voice with neurotypical-normalised output.
    (Unlock Life with a Chat(GPT) (CHI '24); "I Use ChatGPT to Humanize My Words": Affordances and Risks of ChatGPT to Autistic Users (IH '26), 2024/2026 — https://dasomchoi.com/papers/ChatGPT.pdf; confidence medium)
  - For people with aphasia, the useful AI affordances are not simplification alone but visual verification, grammar construction support, error correction and reduced language-processing load — meaning the accessibility layer needs an image/symbol channel for confirming meaning, not just plainer text.
    (Design Probes for AI-Driven AAC: Addressing Complex Communication Needs in Aphasia, 2025 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.09435; confidence high)
  - AAC users report that AI-suggested phrasing costs them agency — choosing from generated options made them feel they had not made their own choice. This is a direct warning for Clara's pictogram board: symbol-to-prompt expansion must preserve authorship, not substitute for it.
    ("The less I type, the better": How AI Language Models can Enhance or Impede Communication for AAC Users (CHI '23), 2023 — https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3544548.3581560; confidence medium)
  - For attention-related challenges, the benefit comes from restructuring rather than shortening — condensation plus visual hierarchy plus a toggle back to the original text. Preserving access to the unmodified response is part of what makes the adaptation acceptable.
    (Too Long to Read? Restructuring LLM Responses for Users with Attention-Related Challenges (Creativity & Cognition 2026), 2026 — https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3803784.3816862; confidence medium)
  - The disability-policy position on AI-generated plain language and Easy Read is permissive but conditional: human review is described as 'nonnegotiable', co-design must happen at product level, and rights/benefits/legal/sensitive content is singled out as requiring extra caution — which is precisely Clara's domain.
    (AI Can Create Plain-Language and Easy-Read Versions of Every Written Material on Earth (Impact, Institute on Community Integration, University of Minnesota), 2025 — https://publications.ici.umn.edu/impact/38-3/ai-can-create-plain-language-and-easy-read-versions-of-every-written-material-on-earth-by-rylin-rodgers; confidence high)
  - Query formulation is an empirically documented barrier for people with intellectual disability using conversational interfaces — ambiguous phrasing, slow formulation, forgetting wake words — and spelling/typing requirements are themselves a barrier to text search. This predates LLMs and is not solved by them.
    (Use of voice activated interfaces by people with intellectual disability (OzCHI '18), 2018 — https://dx.doi.org/10.1145/3292147.3292161; confidence medium)
  - For dyslexia, the intervention with the strongest experimental evidence is increased letter/character spacing, not special 'dyslexia fonts' — which do not show a consistent reading advantage. An accessibility layer should expose spacing and size controls and should not ship OpenDyslexic as the headline feature.
    (Extra-large letter spacing improves reading in dyslexia (PNAS); Inter-letter spacing, inter-word spacing, and font with dyslexia-friendly features (Annals of Dyslexia), 2012 / 2020 — https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1205566109; confidence low)
DESIGN IMPLICATIONS:
  - Make plain-language output a DEFAULT-CAPABLE mode with a persisted preference, not a per-message ask. The readability data (FKGL 12.5–15 across health domains) shows the untreated default excludes most users; requiring the user to type 'explain simply' every turn re-imposes the prompting barrier the layer exists to remove. This is the single clearest gap the user's observation identifies: sites ship a vision settings panel, chat UIs ship nothing equivalent.
  - Ship a COGA-derived checker, not a readability score. Flesch-Kincaid is a proxy that Easy Read practice does not accept. Implement the testable COGA patterns directly as deterministic checks on generated output: paragraph >50 words, sentence with more than one 'and'/'but', words outside a common-1500 list, double negatives, metaphor/idiom flags, numerical concepts without an alternative, instructions not separated. This is the same architecture as Clara's existing number/date/negation verifier and reuses it.
  - Verify simplification, do not just perform it. Clara's differentiator — deterministic checking that numbers, dates, negations and obligations survived — is exactly the mitigation the Easy Read literature demands ('must always be reviewed and validated by a professional'). Position the verifier as the machine half of a validation requirement that currently has no scalable human half, and be explicit that it does not replace validator sign-off for published Easy Read.
  - Separate 'simpler' from 'smaller' from 'friendlier' as three independent controls. The paternalism finding (100x more likely paternalistic tone) and the response-length finding (medium beat both short and long for error detection) mean a single 'simplify' slider will silently trade away register and verifiability. Expose reading level, output length, and tone as distinct settings, and default length to medium (~80–150 words per answer chunk) rather than minimum.
  - Always keep the original one click away. The attention-related-challenges work found the toggle between adapted and original view was part of why the adaptation was acceptable, and the AAC agency findings show substitution without reversibility reads as loss of authorship. Never destroy the source response.
  - Build a symbol channel for verification, not just for input. Clara already has a pictogram board for composing questions; the aphasia design-probe work says the higher-value use is visual verification of the ANSWER. Render key entities/actions from the verified output as symbols so the user can confirm meaning without re-reading dense text.
  - Reuse BCI concept codes and the adapt-symbol attribute in Clara's portable profile spec. WAI-Adapt is stalled and covers only symbols, so Clara's profile can legitimately define the simplification/length/tone fields W3C has not — but it should not reinvent symbol identifiers, and aligning here gives the profile a credible standards story plus a path to becoming a second implementation of a spec that needs two.
  - Attack the prompting barrier at all three gulfs, not just the first. Templates and symbol boards address 'how do I instruct it'. Capability framing (what can this answer?) and — critically — output evaluation (is this answer trustworthy?) are unaddressed by every current product. Clara's verifier is an evaluation-gulf tool; surface it as a per-claim confidence/verification badge, since that is the gulf nobody else is closing.
  - Resolve underspecification in ONE turn, not across many. The 39% multi-turn degradation and +112% unreliability mean the layer should consolidate a fragmented request into a single well-formed prompt before it reaches the model, and restate the consolidated question back to the user for confirmation, rather than letting the model guess early and never recover.
  - Design against acquiescence explicitly. Given documented yes-saying and susceptibility to leading questions, never confirm understanding with a yes/no question ('Did that make sense?'). Use forced-choice or comprehension-probe patterns instead, mirroring the CHI 2024 finding that comprehension questions are the only reliable measure for this population.
  - Counter sycophancy at the layer, since the model will not. Because pushback flips models toward agreement, the layer should hold verified facts (numbers, dates, negations, obligations) stable across turns and warn when a later turn contradicts an earlier verified claim — a conversation-level consistency check, not just a per-message one.
  - Make 'find a human' a first-class, always-present control. COGA Objective 7 requires accessible human help; the Easy Read review suggests the support person may matter more than the document; and the CDT finding that models invent nonexistent disability organisations means referral targets must come from a curated, verifiable list, never from generation.
  - Give long threads a persistent, plain-language state summary. COGA Objective 6 ('processes do not rely on memory') plus the multi-turn degradation evidence argue for a visible running summary of what has been asked and decided, which the user can read instead of scrolling back.
PITFALLS:
  - Shipping 'shorter' as the accessibility feature. Very short responses produced the WORST error detection when the model was wrong (24.5% vs 54.2% for medium). A terse answer strips the redundancy and context that let a user notice something is off, and does so for exactly the users least able to detect it independently.
  - Letting simplification carry an infantilising register. Models are over 100 times more likely to adopt a paternalistic tone about people with intellectual disabilities. 'Plain' will drift to 'childlike' unless tone is constrained separately and tested against adult-register criteria. This is the complaint the self-advocacy community makes most often about Easy Read done badly.
  - Auto-rewriting the USER's words. Autistic users report 'automated masking' — functional atrophy where sustained reliance on AI phrasing replaces authentic voice; AAC users report that selecting a generated phrase feels like losing agency over their own choice. An input-side 'improve my prompt' feature that silently replaces the user's phrasing causes this harm directly. Suggest, show the diff, and default to the user's own words.
  - Calling LLM output 'Easy Read'. Inclusion Europe's standards and the whole Easy Read tradition require validation by people with intellectual disabilities; the LLM Easy-Read study itself insists on professional review. Labelling unvalidated generated text as Easy Read is both a standards violation and the fastest way to lose credibility with the organisations whose endorsement the project needs. Use 'Easy Read draft — not yet validated'.
  - Assuming Easy Read reliably improves comprehension. The most rigorous RCT in the systematic review found no significant improvement in comprehension or retention, with intrinsic language ability the stronger predictor. Marketing the layer on comprehension gains it cannot demonstrate invites a debunking. Ground claims in what is actually verifiable: information preservation (Clara's verifier can prove this) rather than comprehension (which requires user testing per deployment).
  - Trusting readability formulas as the acceptance test. Flesch-Kincaid rewards short words and short sentences and is blind to unexplained jargon, inconsistent terminology, missing referents and lost negations — which are precisely the LLM Easy-Read failure modes actually observed. A layer that optimises FKGL will produce text that scores well and reads worse.
  - Generating support referrals. Every model in the CDT test hallucinated, including inventing disability rights organisations to refer users to. For a tool aimed at benefits, legal and medical text, a fabricated helpline is a severe harm with a vulnerable, trusting user at the other end. Referrals must be curated data, never model output.
  - Confirming comprehension with yes/no questions. Acquiescence is the response bias most strongly tied to intellectual functioning; 'Does that make sense?' will return yes regardless of whether it did.
  - Treating a simplified response as a replacement rather than a view. Losing access to the original removes the user's ability to check, removes a supporter's ability to verify, and makes the simplification unfalsifiable.
  - Leading with dyslexia fonts. OpenDyslexic-style typefaces are the most visible 'cognitive accessibility' signal and among the weakest-evidenced; spacing, size and line length have better support. Shipping the font as the headline feature signals to expert reviewers that the project followed folklore rather than evidence.
OPEN QUESTIONS:
  - No study was found that evaluates LLM-generated Easy Read with VALIDATORS WITH INTELLECTUAL DISABILITIES doing the accept/reject judgement — the Spanish UNE 153101 study used a single text-adaptation expert and explicitly excluded people with intellectual disabilities from the evaluation phase. Whether Easy Read validators would accept AI drafts, and at what edit distance, is unmeasured. This is the highest-value empirical gap and one Clara could close cheaply with a small validator panel.
  - No study was found measuring overtrust, hallucination detection, or persuasion susceptibility in people with intellectual disabilities interacting with LLMs specifically. The argument here is assembled from two separate literatures — 1993 suggestibility/acquiescence research and 2023–2026 sycophancy/overtrust research on general populations — and the bridge between them is inference, not evidence. Treat the compounding-risk claim as a strong hypothesis, not a finding.
  - Whether an accessibility layer's verification badges actually change behaviour for cognitively disabled users is untested. It is plausible that confidence indicators are themselves too cognitively demanding, or are read as endorsement ('the computer checked it, so it is right') — a known failure pattern for security indicators. This needs testing before it becomes the product's central claim.
  - The COGA 'most common 1500 words' guidance has no authoritative word list attached, and no cross-lingual equivalent — a serious problem for Clara's five UI languages. Whether existing lists (e.g. New General Service List, Spanish/German/French/Russian frequency lists) are acceptable substitutes to practitioners in each language is unresolved.
  - Optimal response length for people with intellectual disabilities is unknown. The medium-is-best finding comes from 24 non-disabled participants aged 24–65 on error-detection tasks; it is the only quantitative anchor available and may not transfer. Chunking-with-continue may dominate any fixed length, but that comparison has not been run.
  - The evidence for AI accessibility layers in production is essentially absent. No peer-reviewed evaluation of a shipped cognitive-accessibility layer in a commercial LLM product was found — consistent with the user's observation that none exists. This means Clara has no benchmark to beat, but also no prior art establishing that model operators will adopt such a component.
  - Whether the EU AI Act, the European Accessibility Act, or Section 508 create an actual enforceable obligation for conversational AI interfaces to meet cognitive accessibility requirements was not established in this research. This is likely the strongest adoption lever for a free MIT component and warrants its own investigation.
  - The ADHD/attention restructuring result (10 participants, self-reported rather than diagnosed attention challenges) and the paternalism-bias result (retrieved via press release rather than the journal article) both need primary-source verification before being relied on as load-bearing.
MISSED ANGLE (per verifier): The pass has no legal, regulatory, or safeguarding layer at all — it is entirely HCI and NLP evidence, which leaves out the two forces that will actually constrain what this product can ship. On regulation: the European Accessibility Act has been enforceable since 28 June 2025, its technical yardstick EN 301 549 incorporates WCAG, text and voice chatbots on websites and apps are treated as in scope by practitioners, and penalties run to roughly EUR 100,000 or a percentage of turnover in some member states — yet none of the nine claims touch it. Nor do they touch WCAG 2.2 (published October 2023), which added the first success criteria aimed squarely at cognitive load (3.3.7 Redundant Entry, 3.3.8 Accessible Authentication), or the EU AI Act's accessibility obligations. That gap matters concretely: claim 1's finding that no W3C standard exists for a machine-readable simplification preference is a much sharper design opportunity when paired with the fact that a binding legal duty already applies while the enabling standard does not exist — the layer would be filling a live compliance vacuum, which is a stronger case than 'nobody built it.' On safeguarding, the second half of the same gap: the pass establishes that models are sycophantic (claim 7) but never connects it to the well-documented acquiescence and suggestibility bias in people with intellectual disabilities — the tendency to agree with an interlocutor regardless of belief — which makes a sycophantic model and a suggestible user a compounding failure, not two independent risks. Related and unexamined: who consents to an AI mediating for someone under guardianship or supported decision-making, and the fact that in every study cited people with intellectual disabilities contributed corpora or sat as test subjects but never evaluated the LLM output themselves (the Frontiers paper says so explicitly), so the pass has no participatory-design evidence about what this population actually wants from a simplification layer.

### Motor impairment & speech disability × AI
FINDINGS:
  - Alternative text-entry methods used by people with physical disabilities run roughly 3x to 20x slower than standard keyboard typing: scanning on-screen keyboards average 1.67 wpm, cursor-selection on-screen keyboards 4.24 wpm, and brain-computer interfaces 0.66 wpm, versus 12.47 wpm for standard keyboard use by the same population and 15.42 wpm for automatic speech recognition.
    (Text Entry Rate Of Access Interfaces Used By People With Physical Impairments (RESNA 2017; journal version in Assistive Technology), 2017 — https://www.resna.org/sites/default/files/conference/2017/cac/Koester.html; confidence high)
  - At these rates, composing an ordinary LLM prompt is a multi-minute-to-half-hour task, not a few seconds — which is why chat-shaped interfaces are structurally hostile to switch and gaze users even when every WCAG checkbox is ticked.
    (Text Entry Rate Of Access Interfaces Used By People With Physical Impairments (rates); derived estimate, 2017 — https://www.resna.org/sites/default/files/conference/2017/cac/Koester.html; confidence medium)
  - Sustained real-world eye-gaze typing sits around 8 words per minute, and individual lab baselines can be half that — so the 'order of magnitude below speech' gap is a measured longitudinal fact, not an estimate.
    (Using large language models to accelerate communication for eye gaze typing users with ALS (Nature Communications), 2024 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11530652/; confidence high)
  - Switch-scanning speed varies by nearly two orders of magnitude between individuals (0.15 to 12.94 wpm), so any design that assumes a single 'slow user' profile will be wrong for most of the population.
    (Effectiveness and usability of Scanning Wizard software: a tool for enhancing switch scanning (Disability and Rehabilitation: Assistive Technology), 2017 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5967995/; confidence high)
  - LLM-driven abbreviation expansion gives real but modest end-to-end speedups for gaze users — roughly 29% in the field and 46-61% in the lab — far below what offline keystroke-savings simulations predict.
    (Using large language models to accelerate communication for eye gaze typing users with ALS (Nature Communications), 2024 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11530652/; confidence high)
  - Keystrokes saved does not equal time saved: in the same study, non-AAC mobile users saved 45-56 absolute percentage points of keystrokes yet showed no overall speed improvement, because reviewing AI suggestions costs 3-6x more time per decision than a single keystroke.
    (Using large language models to accelerate communication for eye gaze typing users with ALS (Nature Communications), 2024 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11530652/; confidence high)
  - Off-the-shelf ASR fails disordered speech by an order of magnitude: 3.4% word error rate for typical speakers versus 36.3% for dysarthric speech, and even after fine-tuning on 151 hours of matched disordered speech, novel sentences still come back at 23.7% WER.
    (Community-Supported Shared Infrastructure in Support of Speech Accessibility (Journal of Speech, Language, and Hearing Research), 2024 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12379581/; confidence high)
  - Every commercially available ASR system and multimodal LLM tested collapses on severe dysarthria — above 51% WER across the board — and multimodal LLMs offer no advantage over conventional ASR at default settings. [verified: overstated → Across eight commercial speech-to-text services benchmarked zero-shot on the TORGO corpus — four conventional ASR engines and four multimodal LLMs — severe dysarthria produced above 51% WER for every system, against under 5% on typical speech, and the multimodal LLMs showed no advantage over conventional ASR at default settings. This covers general-purpose products at default settings on a small read-speech corpus; personalised and accessibility-specific systems were not tested.]
    (Zero-Shot Recognition of Dysarthric Speech Using Commercial Automatic Speech Recognition and Multimodal Large Language Models (arXiv; International Journal of Intelligent Systems), 2025 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.17474; confidence high)
  - Personalisation is the only intervention that reliably works for disordered speech — it can beat expert human transcribers — but it still leaves severe speakers at roughly one word in four wrong, and it requires per-user enrollment data that a general chat product cannot assume.
    (Automatic Speech Recognition of Disordered Speech: Personalized Models Outperforming Human Listeners on Short Phrases (Interspeech 2021); State-of-the-Art Dysarthric Speech Recognition with MetaICL (2025), 2021 — https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2021/green21_interspeech.html; confidence high)
  - The state of the art on shared disordered-speech benchmarks is now roughly 8% WER, so the gap is narrowing — but it is still 2-3x the typical-speech error rate, and the best systems are research entries, not what ships in consumer products.
    (The Interspeech 2025 Speech Accessibility Project Challenge (arXiv); Speech Accessibility Project site, 2025 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.22047; confidence high)
  - People who stutter are a second, distinct group excluded by voice input — a measured 13.64 absolute percentage points worse word error rate than fluent speakers on a production voice assistant — and they are not helped by dysarthria-focused fixes.
    (Analysis and Tuning of a Voice Assistant System for Dysfluent Speech (arXiv), 2021 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.11759; confidence high)
  - Mainstream voice assistants understand dysarthric speech only 50-60% of the time, meaning a voice-first AI interface is not a degraded experience for these users — it is a non-functional one.
    ("Hey Siri, Do You Understand Me?": Virtual Assistants and Dysarthria (Intelligent Environments 2018, IOS Press), 2018 — https://ebooks.iospress.nl/volumearticle/49411; confidence medium)
  - W3C explicitly names voice-only interaction as an accessibility barrier for people with speech disabilities — so shipping voice mode as 'the accessibility feature' is a documented anti-pattern, not merely an incomplete one.
    (How People with Disabilities Use the Web: Speech Disabilities (W3C WAI), 2017 — https://www.w3.org/WAI/people-use-web/abilities-barriers/speech/; confidence high)
  - The barriers W3C names for physical disabilities map almost exactly onto how LLM chat UIs are built: incomplete keyboard support, insufficient time limits, and unpredictable/shifting page functions.
    (How People with Disabilities Use the Web: Physical Disabilities (W3C WAI), 2017 — https://www.w3.org/WAI/people-use-web/abilities-barriers/physical/; confidence high)
  - WCAG 2.2 (October 2023) added two Level AA criteria aimed squarely at motor access — 24x24 CSS pixel minimum target size and a no-dragging-required rule — and WCAG 2.2.1 requires that any time limit be turnable off, extendable to 10x, or warned about with 20 seconds and 10 renewals.
    (Understanding WCAG 2.2 SC 2.5.8, SC 2.5.7 and SC 2.2.1 (W3C), 2023 — https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum.html; confidence high)
  - AAC users want AI to cut their typing effort but consistently refuse AI that speaks in a voice that is not theirs — authenticity is a hard constraint, not a nice-to-have, and current AI voices already fail marginalised users most.
    ("The less I type, the better": How AI Language Models can Enhance or Impede Communication for AAC Users (CHI 2023); Me, Myself, and My Voice (2026); Design Probes for AI-Driven AAC (2025), 2023 — https://research.google/pubs/the-less-i-type-the-better-how-ai-language-models-can-enhance-or-impede-communication-for-aac-users/; confidence high)
  - Leading accessibility researchers name accessible AI interfaces as a specific unmet need — validating the premise that no model operator currently ships an embeddable accessibility layer.
    (Accessibility Considerations in the Development of an AI Action Plan (arXiv); Forgotten Again (NordiCHI 2024), 2025 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14522; confidence high)
  - A cloud LLM round-trip cannot sit inside the per-character input loop: AAC character prediction operates on a budget of tens of milliseconds, and cloud dependency is a documented limitation of existing LLM-AAC systems.
    (Adapting Large Language Models for Character-based Augmentative and Alternative Communication (arXiv); SpeakFaster limitations (Nature Communications), 2025 — https://arxiv.org/html/2501.10582v1; confidence high)
  - Typists with cerebral palsy trade speed for accuracy deliberately, achieving keyboard efficiency comparable to non-disabled controls — so aggressive autocorrect or auto-submit destroys a strategy the user has spent years building.
    (How They Type: Eye and Finger Movement Strategies in Typing of Individuals with Cerebral Palsy (CHI 2026), 2026 — https://doi.org/10.1145/3772318.3791184; confidence medium)
  - Fatigue is named as a barrier by both W3C and practitioner guidance but is essentially unquantified in the literature — the interaction-cost argument currently rests on time-per-word data rather than any validated fatigue measure.
    (How People with Disabilities Use the Web: Diverse Abilities and Barriers (W3C WAI); Motor Disabilities (WebAIM), 2017 — https://www.w3.org/WAI/people-use-web/abilities-barriers/; confidence low)
  - The addressable population is large: mobility disability affects 12.2% of US adults, more than vision (5.5%) or hearing (6.2%), yet vision-style accessibility settings are the ones websites conventionally ship.
    (Disability Impacts All of Us (CDC), 2024 — https://www.cdc.gov/disability-and-health/articles-documents/disability-impacts-all-of-us-infographic.html; confidence high)
DESIGN IMPLICATIONS:
  - Optimise for motor actions, not characters, and validate with end-to-end time. SpeakFaster's own data shows a system can save 45-56% of keystrokes and produce zero net speed gain. Any Clara feature that claims to reduce input burden must report wall-clock composition time for a slow input method, not keystroke-savings-rate.
  - Make short input a first-class input mode, not an accident. The one thing LLMs uniquely offer motor-impaired users is asymmetric expansion: a 5-word telegraphic or abbreviated prompt yielding a full-quality answer. Ship an explicit 'shorthand / telegraphic prompt' mode in the composer that expands abbreviations and keyword strings into a well-formed request, shows the expansion for confirmation, and always leaves the original editable. This is the same mechanism SpeakFaster's KeywordAE validated for AAC, applied to prompting.
  - Cap and rank suggestions hard. Option review costs 3-6x a keystroke interval for gaze users. Two or three candidates, ranked, in fixed screen positions beats a scrollable list. Every additional suggestion must earn its review cost; make the number of suggestions a profile setting (0 = off).
  - Never make voice the only low-effort path. Severe dysarthria yields >51% WER on every commercial system and multimodal LLM tested, and W3C names voice-only interaction as a barrier. Voice input in Clara must be one of several equally-supported low-effort paths alongside pictogram/AAC board, shorthand expansion, and saved prompt templates.
  - Design the ASR path to degrade rather than fail. Instead of requiring re-dictation on a bad transcript, show the hypothesis, let the LLM propose disambiguations, and let the user accept or correct with one or two switch hits. A 24-27% WER transcript is often still semantically recoverable — Interspeech 2025's semantic-score metric (88.44% best) exists precisely because WER understates usable output.
  - Support per-user speech personalization or, at minimum, a persistent personal vocabulary and correction memory. Personalization is the only intervention with strong evidence (31% -> 4.6% median WER), and MetaICL shows in-context personalization from ~10 enrollment utterances is now viable without training a per-user model. A portable accessibility profile is the natural carrier for those enrollment samples and the user's correction history.
  - Make every action keyboard-reachable with a single key or short chord, including stop-generating, copy, retry, edit-last-message, and the simplify/verify actions. Count the keystrokes needed to complete each task and publish that count — it is the motor equivalent of a contrast ratio.
  - Meet WCAG 2.2 motor criteria explicitly in the embeddable component: no drag-only interaction (2.5.7), 24x24 CSS pixel minimum targets and preferably 44x44 for primary controls (2.5.8), and no content-imposed time limit that cannot be turned off or extended tenfold (2.2.1).
  - Treat streaming output as a motor hazard, not just a reading one. Text that grows pushes buttons down the page; a user aiming with a head pointer or dwell-click can miss or mis-hit. Pin action controls in a fixed position, offer a 'render complete response only' setting, and never move focus during generation.
  - Persist drafts aggressively and never auto-submit. If a prompt takes 20 minutes to compose, losing it to a session expiry, a page reload, or a stray Enter is catastrophic. Make Enter-to-send optional, provide an explicit send control, and autosave the composer to local storage on every character.
  - Keep prediction local where latency matters. Per-character AAC prediction operates in a 10-90ms budget; a cloud LLM cannot live there. Split the layer: local/on-device for character- and word-level assistance inside the composer, remote LLM only for whole-utterance expansion that the user explicitly requests. This also removes the connectivity dependency SpeakFaster listed as a limitation.
  - Carry motor parameters in the portable accessibility profile alongside the reading-level ones Clara already has: input method (switch/scan/gaze/head/keyboard/voice), scan rate or dwell time, maximum suggestions to show, timeout policy (off), autocorrect aggressiveness, and confirm-before-send. This is the piece no model operator has, and it is exactly the kind of thing that must be portable rather than re-configured in every product.
  - Give the user a persistent style/voice profile that constrains generated text, and always attribute expansions visibly. AAC users accept effort savings but reject text that does not sound like them; make 'this was expanded from your shorthand' visible and one action away from rejection.
  - Do not let autocorrect override a deliberate accuracy strategy. CP typists achieve control-comparable efficiency by typing slowly and accurately; aggressive correction fights that. Make correction suggestive and reversible, never automatic.
PITFALLS:
  - Shipping voice input and calling it the motor-accessibility feature. It excludes the two groups most likely to need it: severe dysarthria (>51% WER across all eight commercial systems and multimodal LLMs tested) and people who stutter (13.64 absolute percentage points worse WER). Motor impairment and speech disability co-occur heavily — ALS, cerebral palsy, Parkinson's, stroke — so the users who most need an alternative to typing are the ones voice serves worst.
  - Reporting keystroke savings as if it were time savings. This is the single most common way LLM-AAC work overstates its benefit; SpeakFaster's own non-AAC arm saved ~50% of keystrokes with no speed gain, and its simulated KSR (0.64) was roughly double what users actually achieved (0.32-0.36).
  - Long or scrolling suggestion lists, carousels, and 'here are 5 ways to phrase this'. Each option costs review time worth 3-6 keystrokes. A generous-looking suggestion panel can make composition slower than typing unaided.
  - Auto-submit on Enter, aggressive autocorrect, or 'helpfully' rewriting as the user types. For a user 12 minutes into a 20-minute prompt, an accidental send or an unwanted rewrite is not an annoyance, it is the loss of the whole session's work.
  - Any session timeout, idle warning, or 'are you still there?' modal. Twenty minutes of silence is a normal composition interval at switch-scanning rates, not idleness. WCAG 2.2.1 exists for exactly this and is routinely broken by chat products' auth and websocket layers.
  - Drag-to-reorder, drag-to-upload, swipe gestures, and hover-only affordances. WCAG 2.5.7 was added in 2023 specifically because head pointer, eye-gaze, trackball and speech-mouse users cannot reliably hold contact while repositioning.
  - Small or moving controls — icon-only buttons under 24x24 CSS pixels, and action buttons that shift position as streamed text grows. Both defeat dwell-click and head-pointer aiming.
  - Cloud-only prediction inside the typing loop. It adds latency into a 10-90ms budget, breaks entirely offline, and — because it sends partial utterances upstream — raises the privacy concern the SpeakFaster authors flagged around conversation-partner context.
  - Assuming per-user personalization solves disordered speech. Even state-of-the-art 19-shot in-context personalization leaves severe speakers at 26.7% WER — roughly one word in four wrong. Personalization is necessary and insufficient.
  - Generating text in the user's name without visible provenance or an easy reject. AAC users across three separate studies (12 users CHI 2023, 53 users 2026, 11 people with aphasia 2025) consistently raised authenticity and intent-mismatch as the limiting concern, and current synthetic voices already under-serve non-binary and non-US-born users most.
  - Designing 'accessibility settings' as a vision panel — font size, contrast, dark mode — and shipping no motor controls at all. Mobility disability (12.2% of US adults) is more prevalent than vision (5.5%), yet the conventional website accessibility widget addresses only the latter.
  - Benchmarking the layer with non-disabled participants using a simulated switch. The Nomon authors are explicit that a webcam-based switch approximating motor-impaired response times "is not a replacement for testing with single-switch users," and the SpeakFaster authors describe their AAC participants as "an opportunistic cohort" rather than a representative sample. Both caveats apply to any evaluation Clara runs.
OPEN QUESTIONS:
  - No measured data on how long disabled users' actual LLM prompts are. I could not retrieve a primary source for prompt-length distributions (LMSYS-Chat-1M ~69.5 tokens and WildChat ~295 tokens appeared only in secondary summaries), and there is no published data at all on prompt length for switch, gaze or head-pointer users. The 'a prompt takes 30 minutes' figure is a defensible derivation but not a measured finding — Clara should measure it.
  - No accessibility audit of a major AI chat composer against WCAG 2.2 motor criteria that I could retrieve in full. A study titled 'Accessibility Fairness in AI: Case Study on ChatGPT and Gemini' (Journal on Technology and Persons with Disabilities) exists and reportedly found keyboard-access failures on buttons and links, but the full text was behind a 403 and I could not verify its method or violation counts. This is the single most valuable missing citation for the project's premise and is worth obtaining directly.
  - Whether any model operator currently ships motor-accessibility settings. I could not retrieve OpenAI's accessibility page (403) or any VPAT/ACR for a major AI chat product. The claim 'no AI chat product ships a motor-accessibility layer' is plausible and supported indirectly by Mankoff et al. 2025 calling for accessible AI interfaces, but I could not verify it product by product.
  - No validated quantitative model of fatigue or interaction cost for AT users. Fatigue is named as a barrier by W3C and WebAIM but never measured. There is no published dose-response between input actions and fatigue, so 'fatigue as a first-class design constraint' currently has to be argued from time-per-word data rather than from a fatigue metric.
  - Whether LLM expansion of abbreviated input preserves user intent at scale. SpeakFaster measured speed and keystroke savings; the aphasia design-probe study reported 'occasional mismatches with user intent' qualitatively. Nobody has published an intent-fidelity rate for abbreviation expansion — which is exactly the metric Clara's deterministic verifier could contribute, since verifying that an expansion did not add or drop a number, date, negation or obligation is the same problem Clara already solves for simplification.
  - Whether streaming output helps or harms users of slow input methods. The trade-off between earlier partial information and moving targets/shifting focus appears not to have been studied for motor-impaired users.
  - Whether 'stop generating' is reachable in time. A gaze or switch user may need 5-20 seconds to hit a control; no study measures whether abort affordances in LLM UIs are usable at those rates.
  - Real-world adoption and abandonment. There is no data on how many switch, scanning or gaze users have tried LLM chat products, what they use them for, or why they stop. Every quantitative source above is either an AAC lab study or an ASR benchmark; the field study of disabled users and general-purpose LLM chat has not been done.
  - Whether the SAP/Euphonia gains transfer to conversational speech in the wild. The SAP fine-tuning result drops from 5.18% WER on prompts seen in training to 23.69% on unshared prompts, which suggests benchmark numbers may substantially overstate real conversational performance.
  - Non-English disordered speech. Project Euphonia's international expansion involved only 132 participants total across Spanish, French, Japanese and Hindi, versus ~3,000 English speakers — so for Clara's ru/es/de/fr locales there is essentially no disordered-speech ASR evidence base.
MISSED ANGLE (per verifier): The pass has no legal or regulatory layer at all — nine claims, all of them performance measurements, and not one on what is actually required of the product. For an accessibility layer on an AI chat interface, the binding questions are: the European Accessibility Act, whose applicability date passed on 28 June 2025 and which covers consumer-facing e-commerce and electronic communications services sold in the EU (whether a general-purpose AI chat assistant falls inside its service categories is genuinely contested and needs checking, not assuming); the US DOJ ADA Title II web rule finalised in April 2024, which sets WCAG 2.1 AA as the standard with compliance dates in April 2026 and April 2027 by entity size, and which binds public-sector deployments of AI chat; Section 508 procurement rules for any US federal buyer; and the open question of whether WCAG's existing success criteria even have a hook for entry-rate cost, since nothing in 2.1 or 2.2 addresses input bandwidth — which is precisely the failure mode claims 1 and 2 identify. That determines whether this layer is a differentiator or a shipping requirement, and it is the difference between an interesting design study and a roadmap item. A second, smaller gap: the pass measures input cost and recognition accuracy but never the error-repair loop or the output side. A user at 26.7% WER must correct roughly one word in four using the same slow channel, so recognition error multiplies rather than adds to input cost; and an LLM's 400-word streaming reply is itself an accessibility burden for someone navigating by switch or listening at speech rate, with no way to interrupt or skim.

### Deaf / hard of hearing × AI
FINDINGS:
  - For many Deaf signers written English functions as a second language: the average Deaf adult reads English at roughly a fifth- to sixth-grade level, and Deaf ASL users are nearly 7x more likely than hearing peers to have inadequate health literacy. Dense written AI output is therefore a real, measurable barrier — not a hypothetical one.
    (Deaf Adults' Health Literacy and Access to Health Information: Protocol for a Multicenter Mixed Methods Study (JMIR Research Protocols), 2019 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6812478/; confidence high)
  - W3C explicitly recognises that sign-language-fluent Deaf people may not be able to read captions — but the remedy (sign language interpretation) sits at Level AAA, so no product is under normal conformance pressure to provide it. Text-only accessibility is not sufficient for this group by W3C's own reasoning, yet is all any AI product is effectively required to do.
    (Understanding SC 1.2.6: Sign Language (Prerecorded), W3C WCAG 2.2, 2023 — https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/sign-language-prerecorded.html; confidence high)
  - Any AI feature that emits audio or video (voice modes, generated video, audio overviews) is already covered by a Level A obligation to caption — including speaker identification and non-speech sound information, not just a raw ASR transcript.
    (Understanding SC 1.2.2: Captions (Prerecorded), W3C WCAG 2.2, 2023 — https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/captions-prerecorded.html; confidence high)
  - DHH people already use text LLMs heavily — but largely as English-language scaffolding, i.e. a use case current AI UIs do not acknowledge or support. Critically, a large minority struggle on the INPUT side: 44.1% reported difficulty crafting effective prompts in English. An accessibility layer that only simplifies output solves half the problem.
    ("We do use it, but not how hearing people think": How the Deaf and Hard of Hearing Community Uses Large Language Model Tools, 2024 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21358; confidence high)
  - The same study identifies the two gaps DHH users themselves name: absence of sign language support (22.1%) and absence of Deaf-cultural competence — including an overly formal register that feels disconnected from natural communication. Register control, not just reading level, is a design requirement.
    ("We do use it, but not how hearing people think": How the Deaf and Hard of Hearing Community Uses Large Language Model Tools, 2024 — https://arxiv.org/html/2410.21358v3; confidence high)
  - Voice-first AI input excludes many Deaf speakers because ASR fails on deaf-accented speech. The 2019 baseline was catastrophic: ~78% word error rate on deaf speech versus ~18% on hearing speech in commercial speech-controlled interfaces.
    (Feasibility of Using Automatic Speech Recognition with Voices of Deaf and Hard-of-Hearing Individuals, 2019 — https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.01167; confidence high)
  - That picture has improved but become BIMODAL, not fixed: in a 2026 Gallaudet-led study, half of DHH participants had zero ASR recognition errors while two had 100% error rates. And 45% rated a touch interface a 'very poor alternative' to speech. So the correct design is to keep voice AND text as equal first-class channels — not to assume Deaf users want voice removed.
    (Deaf and Hard of Hearing Access to Intelligent Personal Assistants: Comparison of Voice-Based Options with an LLM-Powered Touch Interface (ACM CHI 2026), 2026 — https://arxiv.org/html/2601.15209v2; confidence high)
  - AI voice modes shipped WITHOUT captions and retrofitted them late, partially, and asymmetrically. Gemini Live captions only began rolling out in June 2025, are off by default, show only Gemini's replies (not the user's own speech live), are three fixed lines that cannot be moved or resized, and appeared on mobile before web.
    (Gemini Live rolling out captions for quiet conversations (9to5Google) / Google Adds Captions to Gemini Live Conversations (Curb Cuts) / Talk naturally with Gemini Live (Google Support), 2025 — https://9to5google.com/2025/06/05/gemini-live-captions/; confidence high)
  - ChatGPT Advanced Voice offers a 'cc' button on iOS and Android that captions ChatGPT's responses, and OpenAI itself warns the stored transcripts are not verbatim. A caption channel the vendor disclaims the accuracy of is not an accessibility equivalent to text.
    (ChatGPT Voice Mode FAQ, OpenAI Help Center, 2025 — https://help.openai.com/en/articles/8400625-voice-mode-faq; confidence medium)
  - The standing position of the global Deaf-led bodies is that signing avatars must not replace human interpreters, and are acceptable only for limited, pre-recorded, static information — explicitly NOT for live, complex, or high-stakes content such as news, emergency announcements or political information. This is the exact content class Clara targets.
    (WFD and WASLI Statement on Use of Signing Avatars (WFD resource page; text mirrored by the British Deaf Association), 2018 — https://wfdeaf.org/resources/statement-on-use-of-signing-avatars/; confidence high)
  - Empirically, the people a signing avatar is built for like it least. Computer-synthesized avatars score worst of all options, Deaf respondents rate avatars lower than hearing respondents, and earlier ASL learners are the most negative. Building a synthesized signing avatar would optimise against the target users' own ratings.
    (Attitudes Toward Signing Avatars Vary Depending on Hearing Status, Age of Signed Language Acquisition, and Avatar Type, 2022 — https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.730917/full; confidence high)
  - Sign language AI as a research field is documented by Deaf researchers as systematically misaligned with Deaf priorities: majority-framed as fixing a deaf-hearing 'communication barrier', trained on interpreter (not Deaf) signing, and dependent on linguistically ungrounded glosses. An MIT-licensed component that shipped sign generation would be inheriting these defects.
    (Systemic Biases in Sign Language AI Research: A Deaf-Led Call to Reevaluate Research Agendas, 2024 — https://arxiv.org/html/2403.02563v1; confidence high)
  - There IS a workable, non-avatar architecture for sign output that has been deployed at scale: retrieve human-signed video segments and use the LLM only for translation/selection. In a worldwide production dialogue system, the ASL-enabled variant matched user ratings of the non-signing variant.
    (Generating Signed Language Instructions in Large-Scale Dialogue Systems, 2024 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.14026; confidence medium)
  - LLM post-editing of ASR captions measurably helps but demonstrably hallucinates — the exact failure mode Clara's deterministic verifier exists to catch. GPT-3.5 cut caption WER from 23.07% to 9.75%, but also inserted content that was not in the original; Llama2-13B made accuracy worse.
    (Empowering the Deaf and Hard of Hearing Community: Enhancing Video Captions Using Large Language Models, 2024 — https://arxiv.org/html/2412.00342; confidence high)
  - Word Error Rate is the wrong quality metric for a DHH-facing caption or simplification layer — it has little correlation with what DHH users can actually use. A word-importance-weighted metric predicted DHH preference where WER did not.
    (Evaluating the Usability of Automatically Generated Captions for People who are Deaf or Hard of Hearing (ASSETS '17), 2017 — https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.02033; confidence high)
  - Simplification is CONTESTED inside the Deaf community, not welcomed by default. Deaf and hard-of-hearing organisations have campaigned for verbatim text on the grounds that any deletion or change of words denies equal access; the pro-editing constituency is historically parents and teachers, not Deaf adults. This is the single biggest reputational trap for a tool whose core act is rewriting.
    (Captioning Key: Presentation Rate, Described and Captioned Media Program, 2024 — https://dcmp.org/learn/captioningkey/601; confidence high)
  - When Deaf users are given a bilingual sign/text interface, what they ask for first is AUTONOMY over modality — sign video only, transcript only, or both — plus a guarantee that the signed and written versions say the same thing. That is a portable-profile requirement, not a per-product one.
    (Joint Bilingual Navigation of Informed Consent and Intake Forms in American Sign Language and Written English (W4A 2025), 2025 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12701810/; confidence high)
  - International law already obliges states to push private internet companies toward accessible formats and to recognise sign languages — giving model operators a compliance argument for adopting a free embeddable layer, independent of goodwill.
    (UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, Article 21, 2006 — https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities/article-21-freedom-of-expression-and-opinion-and-access-to-information.html; confidence high)
  - The addressable population is very large and growing, which matters for the 'give it away to operators' pitch: 430 million people (>5% of the world) currently need rehabilitation for disabling hearing loss, projected to exceed 700 million by 2050.
    (Deafness and hearing loss, WHO fact sheet, 2026 — https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/deafness-and-hearing-loss; confidence high)
DESIGN IMPLICATIONS:
  - Default to VERBATIM, offer simplification as an explicit, reversible, per-message action. Deaf organisations have historically fought for unedited text (DCMP), so a layer that silently rewrites will be read as paternalism. Clara's existing 'show the original / show what changed' affordance is the right primitive — make it non-negotiable in the embeddable component, and make the original one tap away at all times.
  - Ship a reading-level control as a first-class output setting with a defensible default target of roughly grade 5-6 for the 'plain' tier, since that is the documented average English reading level of Deaf adults (McKee 2019). Do not hard-code it — expose it, and put it in the portable accessibility profile alongside register (formal/neutral/direct), because DHH users specifically flagged over-formal register as alienating.
  - Build the INPUT side, not just output. 44.1% of DHH LLM users report difficulty crafting prompts in English (Huffman 2024). Clara's AAC-style pictogram board and a 'rephrase my question' / 'is this prompt clear?' helper are directly on-target — and this is the single most under-served surface in every shipping AI chat UI.
  - Make text a complete, symmetrical channel for every voice/audio/video feature: live bidirectional captions covering the USER's turn as well as the model's (Gemini Live captions only the model's replies), persistent and exportable transcript, resizable and repositionable caption box, caption state remembered across sessions, and available on web as well as mobile. Every one of these is a gap in a currently shipping product.
  - Caption AI-generated media as a Level A obligation, not a nicety — including speaker identification and non-speech sound information (WCAG 1.2.2), which a raw ASR transcript does not provide. If an AI product emits audio or video, this is already legally in scope.
  - If Clara post-edits ASR captions or transcripts, run the deterministic verifier over that path too. GPT-3.5 improved caption WER by 57.72% but demonstrably inserted content not in the source (Fathallah 2024). Number/date/negation/obligation verification over caption post-editing is a genuinely novel and defensible product claim.
  - Do NOT ship a synthesized signing avatar. WFD/WASLI restrict avatars to pre-recorded static information and warn against live, complex or high-stakes content — exactly Clara's domain — and Deaf, early-acquiring ASL users rate computer-synthesized avatars lowest of all options (Quandt 2022, M=2.65/5).
  - If sign output is ever offered, use the retrieval-of-human-signed-video architecture that has actually shipped at scale (İnan 2024, NAACL Industry Track), keep it to pre-recorded static content, and make it Deaf-led. Treat it as a v2 optional plugin with a clear provenance/licensing story for the signer footage, not as part of the core MIT component.
  - Give the user explicit modality autonomy: sign video only / text only / both, per Seita 2025 — and guarantee that parallel representations carry identical content. This belongs in the portable accessibility profile spec as a first-class field so a Deaf user's preference travels across every product that adopts the layer.
  - Add a language-background field to the accessibility profile: primary language (including 'a signed language'), whether the interface language is an L2, preferred reading level, preferred register, and caption/transcript preferences. This is what makes the layer portable rather than per-product, and it is what DHH users asked for ('allow users to specify language backgrounds for tailored responses').
  - Do not remove or de-emphasise voice I/O for DHH users. The evidence is bimodal — half of DHH participants had zero ASR errors with Alexa, two had 100% (DeVries 2026), and 45% called touch a 'very poor alternative' to speech. Offer parallel channels and let the profile decide; never gate a feature behind voice.
  - Use a DHH-usability-weighted evaluation metric, not WER or generic readability scores, when benchmarking the layer. WER has little correlation with what DHH users can actually use (Kafle & Huenerfauth 2017). Clara should publish its evaluation methodology, since 'we measured the right thing' is a differentiator against vendor accessibility claims.
  - Expose a presentation-rate control for any timed text (live captions, streaming output), with defaults in the 130-160 wpm band DCMP recommends, rather than dumping tokens as fast as they generate.
PITFALLS:
  - Assuming 'AI chat is text, so Deaf users are already served.' Written English is a second language for many Deaf signers reading at ~grade 5-6, and the industry is actively moving toward voice-first and multimodal defaults that erode the text channel.
  - Simplifying by default. Deaf and hard-of-hearing organisations have explicitly campaigned for verbatim text on the grounds that 'any deletion or change of words' denies equal access; the pro-editing constituency documented in the literature is parents and teachers, not Deaf adults. Auto-simplification without consent will be read as the same paternalism.
  - Shipping a signing avatar because it demos well. It is the most visible thing a hearing team would build, it is restricted by WFD/WASLI to exactly the low-stakes content Clara does not handle, and it is rated worst by native Deaf signers themselves. Building it would poison adoption with the community whose endorsement the project needs.
  - Captioning only the AI's turn. Gemini Live does exactly this — a Deaf user cannot see live what the system heard them say, so they cannot catch a misrecognition until after the conversation ends.
  - Treating ASR captions as equivalent to human captioning. ASR errors concentrate on proper nouns, numbers and domain terms — precisely the tokens that carry meaning in bureaucratic, legal and medical text, and precisely what Clara claims to protect.
  - Optimising for WER. It is the field's default metric and it does not predict DHH usability; two outputs with identical WER can differ sharply in whether Deaf readers can use them.
  - Building sign-language features without Deaf leadership. A Deaf-led review of 101 papers found the field driven by hearing researchers' convenience, trained largely on interpreter rather than Deaf signing, and carrying ableist framing at twice the rate of linguistics. An MIT-licensed component would propagate those defects at scale.
  - Using gloss as the internal representation for sign. 51 of 101 reviewed papers depend on glosses with no standard linguistic grounding; it looks like a tractable intermediate format and is not one.
  - Assuming deaf-accented ASR is solved because averages improved. The distribution is bimodal: some Deaf speakers get near-perfect recognition, others get 100% error. An average-case design silently excludes the tail.
  - Letting an LLM 'clean up' captions or documents without verification. Measured LLM caption correction both improved WER and inserted content that was never said — the failure is invisible precisely to a reader who cannot check the audio.
  - Making accessibility settings per-product and non-portable. A Deaf user should not reconfigure reading level, register, caption and modality preferences in every AI app; if the layer does not carry the profile across products, it is just another settings panel.
  - Framing the whole thing as 'bridging the communication barrier between deaf and hearing people.' That framing is the specific thing Deaf researchers named as the field's dominant bias — it treats signed languages as access tools rather than languages, and Deaf people as a problem to be solved.
OPEN QUESTIONS:
  - Could not verify whether OpenAI, Anthropic or Google publish an accessibility conformance report (VPAT) covering their voice modes — openai.com/accessibility returned HTTP 403 to automated fetch. If none exists, that absence is itself a finding worth stating in outreach, but it needs manual confirmation.
  - No evidence found either way on whether AI video generation (Sora and equivalents) emits captions or subtitles for generated speech. A targeted search returned nothing substantive. This should be checked by hand before any claim is made.
  - Direct retrieval of OpenAI's Voice Mode FAQ was blocked (403 on two URL forms). The claims about the cc button, its platform limits, and OpenAI's 'not verbatim' disclaimer rest on search-index snippets of that page, not a full read. Verify manually before quoting in public materials.
  - The National Association of the Deaf's current position-statement index (retrieved directly) lists NO statement on automatic speech recognition, captioning quality, artificial intelligence, or signing avatars — only a telecommunications statement. An older NAD ASR statement may have existed and been dropped in a site redesign; this needs checking with NAD rather than asserting absence.
  - No WFD, WASLI or EUD statement specific to generative AI or LLMs could be found. The standing global Deaf-led position on this technology class is still the 2018 avatars statement. Whether newer guidance exists in signed-video form (which text search will not surface) is unknown and is a real methodological limitation of this review.
  - No study found that measures Deaf readers' comprehension of LLM output at controlled reading levels — i.e. there is no direct empirical basis for choosing Clara's plain-language target for this group. The grade 5-6 figure is a population reading level, not a validated output target.
  - No data found on what fraction of DHH users actually use AI voice modes, or whether captioning their availability changes uptake. Adoption evidence for this specific feature is absent.
  - Unknown whether caption/transcript preferences persist across sessions and devices in any AI product; Gemini's documentation says the caption toggle persists to the next chat, but nothing addresses cross-device or cross-product persistence — which is the core claim of a portable profile.
  - Almost all retrieved evidence is US-based and ASL-specific (Gallaudet, RIT/NTID, DCMP, NAD). Clara ships en/ru/es/de/fr. Evidence on Deaf users of Russian, Spanish, German and French Sign Languages with AI is thin to absent here, and reading-level and register guidance almost certainly does not transfer unchanged.
  - For a retrieval-based sign video approach, the licensing, consent and provenance model for the signers' footage is unresolved and is a hard blocker for an MIT/CC0 component — none of the retrieved sources address it.
  - Whether hard-of-hearing users (as distinct from culturally Deaf signers) have materially different needs from this layer is under-evidenced; several studies collapse the groups, and where they do not (Quandt 2022), attitudes diverge significantly.
MISSED ANGLE (per verifier): No evidence was gathered on whether DHH users actually want AI OUTPUT simplified — and the one study the pass leans on points the other way. Huffman et al. show DHH people using LLMs to make their own English more fluent, better organised and more polished (55.9% fluency, 48.5% vocabulary), i.e. demand for upward register control on their writing, not downward simplification of what the model says back. An 'accessibility layer' whose core feature is plain-language rewriting could be building the inverse of the stated need, and risks reading as paternalistic. Three specific gaps sit under this: (a) the pass never interrogates the ubiquitous 'median deaf school-leaver reads at a fourth-grade level' statistic, which traces to Traxler's 2000 Stanford Achievement Test analysis, is decades old, has been contested, and would be the single most-attacked premise in a public deliverable; (b) it treats 'DHH' as effectively ASL-signing culturally Deaf, when the large majority of people with hearing loss are late-deafened or hard-of-hearing non-signers with typical reading levels, for whom signed output and simplification are irrelevant — this determines whether Clara has one design or several; (c) it covers only ASL/English, with no BSL, RSL or International Sign evidence, which matters given the repo already carries a Russian validation package. Secondarily, the pass has no coverage of the enforceable legal layer that would actually set deadlines for an accessibility product — the DOJ ADA Title II web rule (published April 2024, compliance April 2026/2027), the European Accessibility Act (applicable 28 June 2025), Section 508 and EN 301 549 — all of which reference WCAG Level AA and none of which mandate signed output, which is the real reason the claim-1 argument holds.

### What AI products actually ship today (gap analysis)
FINDINGS:
  - The user's premise is materially wrong in one direction: Anthropic's Claude Code ships a real, documented, multi-feature accessibility layer — screen reader mode, reduced motion, colorblind-friendly themes and a magnifier-friendly cursor — making it the most accessibility-featured mainstream LLM interface found.
    (Use Claude Code with a screen reader — Claude Code docs, 2026 — https://code.claude.com/docs/en/accessibility; confidence high)
  - That layer is opt-in and undiscoverable: it does not activate when a screen reader is actually running, which Anthropic itself lists as a known limitation and which AT users have filed against.
    (anthropics/claude-code issue #89002 — auto-enable --ax-screen-reader for screen reader users, 2026 — https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/issues/89002; confidence high)
  - A blind Lead Accessibility Architect had to build an entire accessibility layer on top of Claude Code himself — using hooks, shell scripts and third-party TTS — because none of it ships. This is direct, expert validation of the thesis that an embeddable accessibility layer is the missing component.
    (anthropics/claude-code issue #70425 — Make Claude Code usable out-of-the-box for blind/screen-reader users, 2026 — https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/issues/70425; confidence high)
  - The user's core vision-accessibility claim survives for low vision specifically: no mainstream AI chat product found ships an in-app text-size control, and the request is open at Anthropic from a user with a named visual impairment.
    (anthropics/claude-code issue #78635 — in-app font/text size setting for accessibility (low vision), 2026 — https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/issues/78635; confidence high)
  - Google publishes free, public, downloadable VPAT/Accessibility Conformance Reports for Gemini — the only major AI vendor found doing so without a gate — audited with NVDA against WCAG 2.0/2.1/2.2 AA, Section 508 and EN 301 549.
    (Google Education - Gemini Accessibility Conformance Report (VPAT 2.5), 2025 — https://www.google.com/accessibility/static/pdf/google-gemini-web-vpat.pdf; confidence high)
  - Gemini's own conformance report admits the single most AI-specific accessibility failure: streaming/asynchronous status messages are not announced to screen readers, across roughly twenty feature surfaces. This is the defining defect of LLM interfaces and the highest-value target for an embeddable layer.
    (Google Education - Gemini Accessibility Conformance Report (VPAT 2.5), criterion 4.1.3, 2025 — https://www.google.com/accessibility/static/pdf/google-gemini-web-vpat.pdf; confidence high)
  - Gemini fails WCAG 1.4.12 Text Spacing: when users apply the text-spacing adjustments that low-vision, dyslexic and cognitively disabled readers rely on, content is cut off. User-side text spacing is therefore not a safe substitute for a product-side reading layer.
    (Google Education - Gemini Accessibility Conformance Report (VPAT 2.5), criterion 1.4.12, 2025 — https://www.google.com/accessibility/static/pdf/google-gemini-web-vpat.pdf; confidence high)
  - Essentially every vision-relevant WCAG criterion in Gemini is 'Partially Supports' rather than 'Supports' — contrast, reflow, non-text contrast, text spacing, focus visible, headings/labels, info and relationships, and name/role/value. Partial conformance, not absence of effort, is the actual industry state.
    (Google Education - Gemini Accessibility Conformance Report (VPAT 2.5), 2025 — https://www.google.com/accessibility/static/pdf/google-gemini-web-vpat.pdf; confidence high)
  - OpenAI does have a VPAT, contrary to widespread belief — but it sits behind the gated SafeBase trust portal rather than being published, so buyers and disabled users cannot read it without a request.
    (OpenAI Trust Portal, 2026 — https://trust.openai.com/; confidence medium)
  - Microsoft's Immersive Reader is the closest existing prior art for a portable embeddable reading-accessibility layer, and it is MIT-licensed, iframe-injected, and feature-rich — validating the architecture Clara proposes.
    (What is Azure AI Immersive Reader? — Microsoft Learn, 2026 — https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-services/immersive-reader/overview; confidence high)
  - Immersive Reader's adoption is negligible and its SDK is feature-frozen — the strongest available evidence that building an embeddable accessibility layer is easy and getting anyone to adopt it is the actual hard problem.
    (npm registry and GitHub API data for @microsoft/immersive-reader-sdk, 2026 — https://www.npmjs.com/package/@microsoft/immersive-reader-sdk; confidence high)
  - Immersive Reader's low adoption is explained by its integration cost: the MIT SDK is free but the layer only works against a paid Azure resource with Entra ID app registration, a client secret, and a server-side token-minting backend. An accessibility layer that requires a cloud account will not be embedded.
    (Create an Immersive Reader resource — Microsoft Learn, 2026 — https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-services/immersive-reader/how-to-create-immersive-reader; confidence high)
  - Anthropic's GUI surfaces are markedly worse than its CLI: the Claude Code VS Code extension renders the entire chat transcript as bare divs with no ARIA roles, no accessible names, no headings and no live regions, making it a single unnavigable block for VoiceOver users.
    (anthropics/claude-code issue #86874 — VS Code extension chat transcript unnavigable for screen reader users, 2026 — https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/issues/86874; confidence high)
  - Open-source LLM UIs (Open WebUI, LibreChat) do substantial accessibility work, but it is reactive defect-fixing — labels, contrast, ARIA roles — with no reading-level, simplification, text-spacing or dyslexia-formatting layer in either.
    (GitHub issue search across open-webui/open-webui and danny-avila/LibreChat, 2026 — https://github.com/open-webui/open-webui/issues/2790; confidence high)
  - Screen reader users report LLM-specific failure modes that generic web accessibility work does not catch — most notably that collapsing a model's 'thinking' block hides it visually but leaves it in the accessibility tree, so blind users are forced to read reasoning they chose to hide.
    (LibreChat issue #8097 — Folding of thoughts has no impact on screenreaders, 2025 — https://github.com/danny-avila/LibreChat/issues/8097; confidence high)
  - ChatGPT's web UI has documented LLM-specific screen reader failures, including a streaming indicator that spams the screen reader and failure to move focus to the new answer — but the evidence is from 2023 and OpenAI blocks retrieval of its current accessibility documentation, so present-day status is unverified.
    (Artificial Intelligence & Accessibility: A Look into ChatGPT — CapTech, 2023 — https://www.captechconsulting.com/technical/artificial-intelligence-accessibility-a-look-into-chatgpt; confidence medium)
  - No reading-level, simplification, output-length or dyslexia-formatting control was found in any mainstream AI chat product — the gap Clara targets is real even though the screen-reader gap is partially closed.
    (Google Education - Gemini Accessibility Conformance Report, 'Tested Pages' section, 2025 — https://www.google.com/accessibility/static/pdf/google-gemini-web-vpat.pdf; confidence medium)
DESIGN IMPLICATIONS:
  - Correct the pitch. 'No AI product ships a vision-accessibility layer' is now falsifiable and a reviewer will falsify it: Anthropic's Claude Code ships screen reader mode, reduced motion, daltonized colorblind themes and magnifier cursor support, and Google publishes NVDA-audited Gemini VPATs. The defensible claim is narrower and stronger: what nobody ships is a READING-COMPREHENSION layer (reading level, simplification, text size, verified plain language), and what exists is opt-in, per-vendor, non-portable and mostly confined to developer tools rather than consumer chat.
  - Target WCAG 4.1.3 Status Messages first. It is the one criterion where Google's own audit admits failure across ~20 surfaces, where the Claude Code VS Code extension has zero live regions, and where ChatGPT's streaming indicator spammed screen readers. A drop-in component that correctly announces streaming LLM output — announce on turn completion or semantic chunk, never per token — is the highest-value, most-cited, most-defensible piece of the layer.
  - Ship with zero backend. Immersive Reader is MIT-licensed and still gets ~38k npm downloads/month against axe-core's 272M because embedding it requires an Azure subscription, a paid S0/S1 SKU, an Entra app registration, a client secret and a server-side token endpoint. Clara's layer must run entirely client-side (or fully self-hosted) with no account, no key and no network dependency for the accessibility features themselves, or it will replicate that adoption failure.
  - Solve auto-detection, not just capability. Anthropic's docs explicitly list 'Screen reader mode doesn't turn on automatically when a screen reader is running' as a limitation, and a JAWS user filed to fix it. A layer that detects `prefers-reduced-motion`, `prefers-contrast`, forced-colors, OS text scaling and (where exposed) an active screen reader, and applies the profile without the user finding a settings page, is a genuine differentiator over every shipped product.
  - Make the portable accessibility profile the wedge, not the renderer. The gap is not that one vendor lacks a feature; it is that a disabled user must reconfigure every AI product separately and most products expose nothing to configure. A JSON profile that travels across ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and self-hosted UIs is something no vendor has an incentive to build alone and every vendor could adopt cheaply.
  - Fix the collapsed-reasoning bug as a named feature. 'Thinking' blocks that collapse visually but stay in the accessibility tree are an LLM-specific defect that generic a11y tooling (axe, WAVE, Lighthouse) will never flag, because the DOM is technically valid. A component that correctly ties visual disclosure to `aria-expanded` plus actual removal from the a11y tree addresses a documented, reported, unsolved complaint.
  - Target the CLI/agent-harness surface, not only web chat. The richest accessibility work found (Claude Code) is in a terminal, the worst is in its own GUI, and a blind architect built a TTS layer on agent hooks. An accessibility layer that binds to agent lifecycle events (tool start, tool failure, permission request, turn complete) is unoccupied territory with a demonstrated expert user waiting for it.
  - Pair simplification with the existing verifier and lead with the verifier. The reason no vendor ships a 'simplify' control is presumably that a simplification that silently drops a number, a date, a negation or an obligation is a liability in bureaucratic, legal and medical text. Clara's deterministic verification is the thing that makes the missing feature shippable for a risk-averse model operator — that is the adoption argument, not the rewriting itself.
  - Publish a real VPAT for Clara itself, openly. OpenAI's VPAT is gated behind SafeBase and Anthropic's reportedly requires an NDA; Google's is a free PDF. An MIT accessibility component whose own conformance report is a public download is both credible and a pointed contrast, and public ACRs are what institutional buyers (education, government) actually procure against.
  - Instrument adoption expectations realistically. The comparator is 38k downloads/month for Microsoft's equivalent, not millions. Design the distribution around a small number of high-leverage integrations (self-hosted UIs like Open WebUI and LibreChat, which already have active a11y contributors and 90 combined accessibility issues) rather than assuming model operators will adopt first.
PITFALLS:
  - Announcing streaming tokens through a naive `aria-live="polite"` region. This is the documented ChatGPT failure where screen readers repeated 'middle dot, middle dot, comma' continuously until the response finished. Per-token announcement floods the speech queue and makes output less accessible than silence. Announce arrival and completion, expose the text as navigable structure, and let the user read at their own pace with review commands.
  - Hiding content visually without hiding it from the accessibility tree (or the reverse). LibreChat's collapsed 'thinking' blocks stayed in the DOM, so blind users were forced through reasoning that sighted users had folded away. `display:none`-equivalent semantics and `aria-expanded` must move together, and automated scanners will not catch the mismatch.
  - Shipping the layer as opt-in settings. Anthropic ships genuinely good screen reader support that does not turn on when a screen reader is running, and a JAWS user had to file an issue to ask for detection. A feature a disabled user cannot find is, in practice, a feature that does not exist for them.
  - Requiring an account, API key or cloud service for accessibility features. Microsoft's Immersive Reader is MIT-licensed and still effectively unadopted because embedding it means an Azure subscription, a paid SKU, an Entra app and a token backend. Any dependency of that shape converts a free accessibility layer into a procurement project.
  - Building a generic 'accessibility overlay' widget. Toolbar-style overlays that bolt onto a page and offer font-size and contrast buttons are widely opposed by disabled users and accessibility practitioners, because they layer a second interface over an unfixed one and often break the assistive technology the user already has configured. Clara's value is in the AI-specific semantics — streaming announcement, message structure, verified simplification — not in re-implementing settings the OS and browser already own.
  - Simplifying text without deterministic verification. A plain-language rewrite that drops a negation, a dosage, a deadline or an obligation is more dangerous than the original bureaucratic text, precisely because it reads as trustworthy. This is the likeliest reason no vendor ships a simplification control, and shipping one without the verifier reproduces the risk rather than solving it.
  - Treating WCAG conformance as the finish line. Google's Gemini scores 'Partially Supports' on nearly every vision-relevant criterion while offering no reading-level or text-size control at all — a product can be largely WCAG-conformant and still be unreadable for dyslexic, low-literacy, aphasic or cognitively disabled users. Conformance tooling (axe, WAVE) measures the part of the problem Clara is not solving.
  - Assuming ChatGPT's 2023 defects still stand. The best public hands-on evaluation is three years old and OpenAI blocks automated retrieval of its current help pages. Building competitive claims on that evidence risks being wrong and discredits the rest of the argument.
OPEN QUESTIONS:
  - ChatGPT's current (2026) accessibility state could not be verified. openai.com/accessibility and help.openai.com/en/articles/6825453-chatgpt-accessibility both return HTTP 403 to automated retrieval, and the only hands-on evaluation found is from November 2023. Someone should manually test ChatGPT web, desktop and mobile with NVDA/JAWS/VoiceOver and read the help article in a browser before any competitive claim is made about it.
  - OpenAI's VPAT is listed on trust.openai.com but its contents are unknown — whether it covers ChatGPT consumer web or only the API/enterprise platform, which WCAG version, and what it marks as failing. Requesting it through the trust portal would materially sharpen the gap analysis.
  - Anthropic's Claude.ai (consumer web) accessibility was not directly assessed. All Anthropic evidence gathered concerns Claude Code (CLI, desktop, VS Code extension). A LinkedIn post asserts Anthropic's Claude.ai VPAT requires an NDA, but that was not independently verified. Whether claude.ai web has any accessibility settings panel is unknown.
  - Microsoft Copilot was not verified at all. The Microsoft conformance-reports page requires an interactive product-name search that could not be driven, so whether a Copilot ACR exists, and whether Copilot integrates Immersive Reader anywhere, remains unestablished. Given Microsoft has both the strongest accessibility organisation and the Immersive Reader asset, this is the single largest evidence gap and could most change the analysis.

  - A reported change in which Edge's Read Aloud button was repurposed to launch Copilot Vision — an AI feature displacing an existing accessibility feature — could not be verified; the article body was not retrievable. If true it is a strong cautionary example, but it should not be cited until confirmed.
  - Whether Azure AI Immersive Reader is formally scheduled for retirement could not be confirmed or refuted. The overview, release-notes and setup docs carry no deprecation notice and the GitHub repo was pushed to on 2026-09-03, but the npm package has had no release since June 2023. The service may be maintained-but-frozen rather than retired; a definitive answer affects how safely Clara can position itself as a successor.
  - No empirical survey data exists on how screen reader users actually experience AI chat interfaces. WebAIM's Screen Reader User Survey #10 — the field's standard instrument — contains no questions about AI chatbots or generative AI at all. Design decisions about which accessibility features AT users most want from LLM UIs are currently being made without population-level evidence, which is a significant weakness in this whole research area and an opportunity for Clara to generate primary data.
  - The claim that no AI product ships a reading-level or simplification control is an absence-of-evidence finding, inferred from Gemini's 31 tested surfaces, Anthropic's settings table and 90 open-source accessibility issues. It was not established by exhaustively enumerating every product's settings UI, and a counterexample (for instance in Copilot for Education or Gemini's Guided Learning) would weaken the central gap argument.
MISSED ANGLE (per verifier): Remediation velocity — the pass never measured how fast these gaps close, and treats a snapshot of issue trackers as a durable market gap. Fact-checking exposed this directly: of the nine claims, three rest on defects that have already been fixed (Anthropic shipped VS Code screen reader support in v2.1.236 against an issue filed at 2.1.233 weeks earlier; LibreChat #8097 closed COMPLETED; Open WebUI #17150 closed COMPLETED), and a fourth (the blind architect's wishlist) is now partly shipped in the CLI. An accessibility layer justified by "none of it ships" is being underwritten by a moving target, and the incumbents are moving faster than the research assumed. Two related blind spots compound this: (1) no regulatory or procurement analysis — the European Accessibility Act became applicable 28 June 2025 harmonized to EN 301 549 v3.2.1 / WCAG 2.1 AA, alongside Section 508 procurement, which is precisely why VPATs exist and precisely what will push vendors to build this natively rather than buy a layer; (2) no distribution or feasibility analysis — whether a third-party embeddable layer may legally and technically attach to ChatGPT, Gemini, or Claude at all, given terms of service, content security policy, and browser extension store rules. Separately, all nine claims draw on self-selected GitHub issue trackers and vendor self-reported VPATs; there is no evidence from disabled users directly (e.g. the WebAIM screen reader survey or first-party user research) and no prevalence or severity data to say which of these gaps actually matter most.

### Legal & regulatory drivers for operators
FINDINGS:
  - The European Accessibility Act (Directive (EU) 2019/882) has applied since 28 June 2025 — the deadline is already past, not upcoming. Transposition was due 28 June 2022; Art 31(2) sets application from 28 June 2025. Art 32 gives a transitional period to 28 June 2030 for services still using products lawfully in use before that date, contracts agreed before 28 June 2025 may run up to five more years, and self-service terminals may run up to 20 years from entry into use.
    (Directive (EU) 2019/882 (European Accessibility Act), OJ L 151, 7.6.2019, 2019 — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2019/882/oj/eng; confidence high)
  - A standalone AI chat product is NOT named in the EAA's closed scope list. Art 2(2) services are: electronic communications; services providing access to audiovisual media services; specified air/bus/rail/waterborne transport elements; consumer banking; e-books and dedicated software; and e-commerce services. Art 2(1) products include 'consumer general purpose computer hardware systems and operating systems for those hardware systems'. An AI chat product is therefore caught only indirectly: (a) via its online sign-up/purchase flow as an 'e-commerce service' (Art 3(30): services at a distance, by electronic means, at the individual request of a consumer 'with a view to concluding a consumer contract'); (b) by shipping as part of a consumer OS (Art 2(1)(a)); or (c) by being embedded in a covered service such as a bank's or telco's chatbot.
    (Directive (EU) 2019/882, Articles 2 and 3; European Commission, European Accessibility Act page, 2019 — https://commission.europa.eu/strategy-and-policy/policies/justice-and-fundamental-rights/disability/union-equality-strategy-rights-persons-disabilities-2021-2030/european-accessibility-act_en; confidence high)
  - The EAA text closest to what Clara does is Annex I Section VII (functional performance criteria), which requires 'at least one mode of operation' for specific impairments — including (b) 'Usage with limited vision' and (j) 'Usage with limited cognition': 'The product or service shall provide at least one mode of operation incorporating features that make it simpler and easier to use.' Annex I Section I(2) additionally requires products to provide '(c) flexible magnification, brightness and contrast', '(f) flexible ways of improving vision clarity' and '(m) ...enough, and flexible amounts of, time for interaction'.
    (Directive (EU) 2019/882, Annex I Sections I and VII, 2019 — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?uri=CELEX:32019L0882; confidence high)
  - The EAA imposes NO general plain-language duty. The only numeric readability ceiling in the directive applies to consumer banking services alone: Annex I Section IV(e)(ii) requires 'ensuring that the information is understandable, without exceeding a level of complexity superior to level B2 (upper intermediate) of the Council of Europe's Common European Framework of Reference for Languages.' Elsewhere the directive only says information must be 'presented in an understandable way' — a functional, unquantified standard.
    (Directive (EU) 2019/882, Annex I Sections III, IV, VII, 2019 — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?uri=CELEX:32019L0882; confidence high)
  - As of September 2026 no harmonised standard appears to have been cited in the Official Journal under the EAA, so there is no presumption-of-conformity route (Art 15(1)) for EAA compliance. EN 301 549 V3.2.1 (2021-03) is harmonised only under the Web Accessibility Directive (EU) 2016/2102, added to Implementing Decision (EU) 2018/2048 by Implementing Decision (EU) 2021/1339 of 11 August 2021. Operators must therefore self-assess against the Annex I functional requirements and document it.
    (Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2018/2048 (consolidated 12.10.2021); EC harmonised standards hub, 2021 — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dec_impl/2018/2048/2021-10-12; confidence medium)
  - EU AI Act Article 50(5) is the strongest and most immediate lever, and it already binds AI chat providers: transparency obligations apply from 2 August 2026, and Art 50(5) states the AI-interaction disclosure 'shall be provided to the natural persons concerned in a clear and distinguishable manner at the latest at the time of the first interaction or exposure. The information shall conform to the applicable accessibility requirements.' This attaches to Art 50(1) systems 'intended to interact directly with natural persons' — i.e. every consumer AI chat UI — regardless of risk classification.
    (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (AI Act), Article 50; European Commission regulatory framework for AI, 2024 — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?uri=OJ:L_202401689; confidence high)
  - Breach of AI Act Article 50 (including the Art 50(5) accessibility conformity requirement) carries administrative fines of up to EUR 15,000,000 or 3% of total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher, under Article 99(4)(g).
    (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (AI Act), Article 99(4), 2024 — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?uri=OJ:L_202401689; confidence high)
  - AI Act Article 16(l) requires providers of HIGH-RISK AI systems to 'ensure that the high-risk AI system complies with accessibility requirements in accordance with Directives (EU) 2016/2102 and (EU) 2019/882', and Recital 80 tells providers to 'ensure compliance with these requirements by design' with measures 'integrated as much as possible into the design'. But these dates were pushed back: the AI Omnibus, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 of 8 July 2026 (OJ 24 July 2026, in force 27 July 2026), moved Annex III high-risk obligations to 2 December 2027 and Annex I (product-embedded) high-risk to 2 August 2028.
    (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 Art 16(l) & Recital 80; Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 (Digital Omnibus on AI), 2026 — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj/eng; confidence high)
  - The US DOJ ADA Title II web/mobile rule deadlines are NOT April 2026/April 2027 — they were extended by one year on 20 April 2026. The 2024 final rule (89 FR 31320, published 24 April 2024) adopts WCAG 2.1 Level AA for state and local government web content and mobile apps; an Interim Final Rule (91 FR 20902, effective 20 April 2026, comments closed 22 June 2026) revised 28 CFR 35.200(b)(1) from 24 April 2026 to 26 April 2027 (entities serving 50,000+ population) and 35.200(b)(2) from 26 April 2027 to 26 April 2028 (under 50,000 and special district governments). The substantive WCAG 2.1 AA standard was not changed.
    (Extension of Compliance Dates for Nondiscrimination on the Basis of Disability; Accessibility of Web Information and Services of State and Local Government Entities, 91 FR 20902, 2026 — https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/04/20/2026-07663/extension-of-compliance-dates-for-nondiscrimination-on-the-basis-of-disability-accessibility-of-web; confidence high)
  - DOJ's own stated reason for the extension includes that AI cannot currently do this job: 'Advanced technology, such as generative AI, does not yet reliably automate the remediation of inaccessible content at scale.' DOJ also said it 'overestimated the capabilities (whether staffing or technology) of covered entities to comply'.
    (91 FR 20902, full text (Federal Register raw text), 2026 — https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/text/2026/04/20/2026-07663.txt; confidence high)
  - The ADA Title II rule reaches vendors indirectly: it binds public entities but obligations extend to content provided under arrangement with third parties. DOJ's guidance states the requirement applies 'when a state or local government has an arrangement with someone else' providing web content or apps, and the third-party-content exception does not cover contractor-provided tools and platforms. An AI chat product sold to a US public entity therefore inherits a WCAG 2.1 AA expectation by 26 April 2027/2028.
    (DOJ, Fact Sheet: New Rule on the Accessibility of Web Content and Mobile Apps Provided by State and Local Governments, 2024 — https://www.ada.gov/resources/2024-03-08-web-rule/; confidence high)
  - US private-sector web accessibility litigation is large and rising again: 8,667 federal ADA Title III lawsuits were filed in 2025, of which 3,117 (36%) were website accessibility cases — a 27% increase over 2024's 2,452, reversing two years of decline. Filings concentrate in New York (1,021), Florida (961) and Illinois (585); California had only 4 federal filings because courts there have held online-only businesses are not ADA-covered. State-court suits and demand letters are not counted in these figures.
    (Seyfarth Shaw ADA Title III blog, 'Federal Court Website Accessibility Lawsuit Filings Bounce Back in 2025', 2026 — https://www.adatitleiii.com/2026/03/federal-court-website-accessibility-lawsuit-filings-bounce-back-in-2025/; confidence medium)
  - For US federal procurement, Section 508 (29 U.S.C. 794d) plus the Revised 508 Standards (36 CFR Part 1194, issued 18 January 2017, effective 18 January 2018) incorporate WCAG 2.0 Level AA by reference for both electronic content (E205.4) and software user interfaces (E207.2), and FAR Part 39.2 carries this into acquisitions. The VPAT/ACR is however framed as a recommendation, not a statutory vendor obligation: 'We recommend that vendors generate an ACR for any ICT that's intended to be marketed to the Federal government.' The binding duty sits on the agency, which must specify accessibility requirements in solicitations — making an ACR a de facto, not de jure, market-access document.
    (Section508.gov, Accessibility Conformance Reports; US Access Board, Revised 508 Standards, 2026 — https://www.section508.gov/sell/acr/; confidence high)
  - The EU has its own hard procurement lever: EAA Article 24(1) provides that the Annex I accessibility requirements 'shall constitute mandatory accessibility requirements within the meaning of Article 42(1) of Directive 2014/24/EU and of Article 60(1) of Directive 2014/25/EU'. EU public buyers must therefore draw technical specifications by reference to them. Separately, EAA Article 13(2) obliges service providers to prepare Annex V information 'and shall explain how the services meet the applicable accessibility requirements', made public 'in written and oral format, including in a manner which is accessible to persons with disabilities'.
    (Directive (EU) 2019/882, Articles 13, 24, 30, 2019 — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?uri=CELEX:32019L0882; confidence high)
  - EAA penalties are set nationally and can be material. Article 30 requires penalties that are 'effective, proportionate and dissuasive', accompanied by 'effective remedial action', and taking account of 'the number of units of non-complying products or services concerned, as well as the number of persons affected'. Germany's implementing law (BFSG) § 37 sets maximum fines of EUR 100,000 for placing non-compliant products on the market, supplying them, or offering/providing non-compliant services, and EUR 10,000 for information and documentation failures.
    (Directive (EU) 2019/882 Art 30; Barrierefreiheitsstärkungsgesetz (BFSG) § 37, 2025 — https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/bfsg/__37.html; confidence high)
  - The EAA's microenterprise exemption is services-only and narrow: Art 4(5) exempts 'Microenterprises providing services' from the accessibility requirements and related compliance obligations; Art 3(23) defines a microenterprise as fewer than 10 persons AND turnover or balance sheet not exceeding EUR 2 million. Microenterprises dealing with products are not exempt — recital 71 says only that their obligations 'should be lighter'.
    (Directive (EU) 2019/882, Articles 3(23), 4(5), recitals 70-72, 2019 — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2019/882/oj/eng; confidence high)
  - Outside the EU and US the levers are weaker and mostly public-sector or general-duty. UK: no EAA equivalent; the Public Sector Bodies (Websites and Mobile Applications) (No. 2) Accessibility Regulations 2018 (SI 2018/952) bind public sector bodies only, while private services are reached only by the Equality Act 2010 s.20 reasonable-adjustments duty, whose s.20(6) expressly includes 'steps for ensuring that in the circumstances concerned the information is provided in an accessible format'. Canada: the Accessible Canada Act (Royal Assent 21 June 2019) s.5 covers 'information and communication technologies' and 'the procurement of goods, services and facilities' for federally regulated entities, but standards are binding only once the Minister makes them regulations. Australia: the Disability Discrimination Act 1992 covers public and private sectors without naming a standard, while WCAG 2.2 applies via the 2024 Digital Experience Policy to government only.
    (Equality Act 2010 s.20; SI 2018/952; Accessible Canada Act; W3C WAI Web Accessibility Laws & Policies, 2010 — https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/15/section/20; confidence high)
  - Current US federal AI policy does not add an accessibility obligation on top of existing law. OMB Memorandum M-25-21 on federal use of AI mentions accessibility only in generic, hortatory terms ('increasing the accessibility of government services'; agencies should resource 'accessibility, privacy, confidentiality, and security') and contains no Section 508 mandate, no disability-specific control, and no accessibility requirement for high-impact AI use cases.
    (OMB M-25-21, Accelerating Federal Use of AI through Innovation, Governance, and Public Trust, 2025 — https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/M-25-21-Accelerating-Federal-Use-of-AI-through-Innovation-Governance-and-Public-Trust.pdf; confidence medium)
  - Disability-led organisations regard the EAA itself as under-scoped, which limits how much of the adoption argument can rest on it. The European Disability Forum notes that 'Healthcare services, education, transport, housing, and household appliances were left out of the Act', criticises the microenterprise and urban-transport exemptions, the deferral of built-environment rules to Member States, the effective survival of inaccessible self-service terminals to ~2045, and the fact that the enforcement mechanism does not reach infringements by public authorities.
    (European Disability Forum, European Accessibility Act, 2025 — https://www.edf-feph.org/european-accessibility-act/; confidence medium)
DESIGN IMPLICATIONS:
  - Build the layer around two legally distinct features, not one. (1) An 'AI disclosure renderer' that makes the Article 50(1) 'you are talking to an AI' notice itself accessible — small, cheap, universally applicable to any EU-facing chat UI, and already in force since 2 August 2026 with a 3%-of-turnover fine attached. (2) A settings layer implementing EAA Annex I Section VII(b) 'usage with limited vision' and (j) 'usage with limited cognition' — the 'at least one mode of operation' framing is exactly the give-away's shape. Ship them as separable modules so an operator can adopt (1) in an afternoon.
  - Ship compliance evidence as a first-class artefact, not documentation. Because no harmonised standard has been cited under the EAA, there is no checkbox to certify against — operators must self-assess. The single highest-leverage deliverable is a machine-readable mapping from each Clara feature to specific Annex I clause numbers (I(2)(c), I(2)(f), I(2)(m), III(b), IV(g), VII(b), VII(j)), plus a pre-filled draft EAA Article 13(2)/Annex V service statement and a draft ACR/VPAT section against WCAG 2.1 AA / EN 301 549 V3.2.1 / Revised 508 Standards E205.4 and E207.2. That is what actually moves a procurement decision.
  - Target the embedding paths, not standalone chat. EAA scope reaches AI chat through three doors: the e-commerce sign-up flow (Art 3(30)), OS-bundled assistants (Art 2(1)(a) covers operating systems), and chatbots inside covered services (consumer banking, electronic communications, AVMS access). Prioritise integration adapters for banking and telco chat deployments and for OS/desktop assistant harnesses — those are the deployments with unambiguous legal exposure.
  - Make CEFR B2 a configurable, measurable target rather than a general claim. Annex I Section IV(e)(ii) is the only numeric readability ceiling anywhere in the EAA and it applies to consumer banking services. A 'banking mode' that measurably targets and reports CEFR B2 is a defensible, quantified compliance feature; a generic 'plain language' claim is not.
  - Support non-web software properly. EN 301 549 covers non-web software and mobile apps, and the Revised 508 Standards apply WCAG 2.0 AA to software UIs via E207.2, not only to web content. A layer that only works as a web widget misses desktop apps, mobile apps and agent harnesses — which is where the 508 procurement lever actually bites.
  - Position the deterministic verifier as the answer to DOJ's own objection. DOJ stated on the record in April 2026 that 'generative AI does not yet reliably automate the remediation of inaccessible content at scale'. Clara's differentiator — a deterministic check that numbers, dates, negations and obligations survived the rewrite — is precisely the rebuttal to that objection, and should be the headline claim to public-sector buyers rather than the rewriting itself.
  - Use the procurement argument in both jurisdictions, but state it accurately. In the US the ACR/VPAT is recommended, not legally required of vendors; the binding duty sits on the agency to specify accessibility in solicitations, which makes the ACR a de facto gate. In the EU the position is stronger: EAA Art 24(1) makes Annex I requirements 'mandatory accessibility requirements' under Art 42(1) of Directive 2014/24/EU, so EU public buyers must reference them in technical specifications.
  - Do not build urgency messaging on the US Title II dates without checking them. They moved to 26 April 2027 (50,000+ population) and 26 April 2028 (smaller entities and special districts) on 20 April 2026, and DOJ signalled possible further rulemaking on the substance. EU dates are the more reliable urgency anchor: EAA already applies, AI Act Art 50 already applies.
  - Design for the exempt tail differently. Microenterprises providing services are wholly exempt under Art 4(5) (<10 staff and <=EUR 2m). For that segment — and for UK, Australian and Canadian private-sector adopters, where only general non-discrimination duties bite — the adoption argument must be product quality and user reach, not compliance. Two different landing pages, two different pitches.
PITFALLS:
  - Claiming 'the EAA requires AI chat products to be accessible.' It does not, on its face. The scope in Art 2 is a closed list that names neither AI nor general software. Overstating this is the fastest way to lose credibility with the in-house counsel who has to sign off on adoption — and a wrong scope claim in marketing material is itself a legal exposure.
  - Claiming 'the EAA requires plain language.' The CEFR B2 ceiling in Annex I Section IV(e)(ii) is confined to consumer banking services. Everywhere else the directive says only 'presented in an understandable way', with no metric. This misquote circulates widely in vendor marketing; repeating it would put Clara in the same bucket.
  - Marketing the layer as automated accessibility remediation. DOJ has stated on the record that generative AI does not reliably automate remediation at scale, and the disability community's long-standing objection to automated 'accessibility overlay' widgets is that they promise conformance they cannot deliver and can actively break assistive-technology behaviour. A browser extension that injects a simplified overlay onto arbitrary pages is architecturally the same shape as the products that community rejects — this needs explicit design separation and honest scoping, and it is the single largest reputational risk in the current Clara feature set. (Flag: the overlay-backlash literature was not retrieved in this research pass and should be sourced directly before any public claim is made either way.)
  - Publishing an overclaiming VPAT/ACR, or encouraging adopters to. In US federal procurement an inaccurate conformance report is a representation to the government and carries False Claims Act exposure. A pre-filled ACR template is high value only if it is conservative, states 'partially supports' honestly, and makes clear the adopter must verify against their own build.
  - Building the pitch on US Title II deadlines that have already moved. Anyone citing April 2026 / April 2027 is working from pre-20-April-2026 material; the dates are now April 2027 / April 2028, and DOJ has invited comment on the underlying rule.
  - Assuming a free MIT/CC0 component removes the operator's compliance burden. Under EAA Art 13(2) and Art 14 the service provider carries the assessment and documentation duty regardless of what library it embeds; under AI Act Art 16(l)/50(5) the provider carries it. A layer that implies 'install this and you're compliant' will be rejected by exactly the legal reviewers whose sign-off is needed.
  - Treating the AI Act's Article 16(l) high-risk accessibility duty as an imminent driver. The AI Omnibus (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744) pushed Annex III high-risk obligations to 2 December 2027 and Annex I to 2 August 2028. Article 50(5) — already live — is the near-term hook; 16(l) is a 2027-28 story.
  - Designing only for the web. Section 508's E207.2 and EN 301 549's software clauses cover non-web software, and the highest-value procurement deployments (desktop assistants, mobile apps, OS-bundled assistants) are not browser pages. A web-only component forfeits the strongest procurement lever.

MISSED ANGLE (per verifier): Procurement mandates and private litigation — the two channels that actually bind AI chat vendors today and that an accessibility layer would actually be sold into — are entirely absent. (1) US Section 508 (29 U.S.C. 794d) plus the FAR: any ICT sold to a US federal agency must conform to the Revised 508 Standards, enforced through contract terms and VPAT/ACR demands at procurement rather than through a deadline, and it bites now with no scope debate about whether a chat product is a listed 'product' or 'service'. Its EU mirror is Directive 2014/24/EU Art 42(1), which requires accessibility criteria in technical specifications for any procurement intended for use by natural persons. (2) US ADA Title III private litigation: there is no DOJ Title III web rule, so exposure is driven by several thousand federal web-accessibility suits and far more demand letters per year, and AI chat widgets embedded on retail and banking sites are already being named — this is a live commercial driver that the pass's focus on the Title II government rule misses entirely. The pass also omits the EAA's own carve-outs cutting the other way — Art 4(5) exempts microenterprises from the services requirements outright, and Art 14 plus Annex VI let any operator claim fundamental alteration or disproportionate burden — which materially weaken the operator-urgency argument the rest of the research is building.

### How to build a portable, embeddable layer (technical)
FINDINGS:
  - Shadow DOM breaks every IDREF-based ARIA relationship (aria-labelledby, aria-describedby, aria-controls, aria-activedescendant, aria-owns, aria-flowto, aria-details, aria-errormessage) and native `for`/`form`/`list` association across the boundary. There is no shippable fix today, so an embeddable accessibility layer must render its interactive parts in the LIGHT DOM, not a shadow root.
    (Shadow DOM and accessibility: the trouble with ARIA, 2022 — https://nolanlawson.com/2022/11/28/shadow-dom-and-accessibility-the-trouble-with-aria/; confidence high)
  - The standards fix — Reference Target — is real and progressing but NOT deployable: as of July 2026 it is in a Chrome origin trial targeting M152, Firefox has signalled positive, and WebKit has given NO signal. Building the layer's semantics on it would strand Safari/VoiceOver users, i.e. a large share of screen-reader users.
    (Chrome Platform Status: Reference Target for Cross-root ARIA + WICG reference-target-explainer.md, 2026 — https://chromestatus.com/api/v0/features/5188237101891584; confidence high)
  - Web Components are now a viable framework-agnostic distribution format — the historical blocker (React passing everything as string attributes and not listening to custom events) is gone. React 19 scores 100% on Custom Elements Everywhere, as do Vue, Angular, Svelte, Preact, Lit and Solid.
    (Custom Elements Everywhere, 2026 — https://custom-elements-everywhere.com/; confidence high)
  - A custom element can carry correct default accessibility semantics WITHOUT a shadow root, using ElementInternals (Baseline since March 2023). Internals-set role/aria* act as defaults that host authors' own attributes override — exactly the right precedence for an embeddable layer.
    (ElementInternals — MDN, 2026 — https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/ElementInternals; confidence high)
  - You can get CSS politeness without shadow DOM. Cascade layers (Baseline March 2022) make library styles lose to host styles by default; `:where()` (Baseline Jan 2021) gives zero-specificity defaults; `all: revert-layer` resets within a layer; container queries let the component adapt to its slot rather than the viewport. This combination removes the main reason people reach for shadow DOM.
    (MDN: @layer, :where(), all, Container queries, 2026 — https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/@layer; confidence high)
  - The four user-preference media queries are NOT equally safe to depend on. prefers-reduced-motion (Baseline Jan 2020), prefers-contrast (Baseline May 2022) and forced-colors (Baseline Sept 2022) are widely available; prefers-reduced-transparency is explicitly NOT Baseline and is marked experimental. Any transparency/blur in the layer needs a non-media-query fallback (e.g. an explicit user setting), not a query.
    (MDN: prefers-reduced-motion / prefers-contrast / forced-colors / prefers-reduced-transparency, 2026 — https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/@media/prefers-reduced-transparency; confidence high)
  - In forced-colors mode the browser picks system colors from NATIVE element semantics, not ARIA roles — so a `<div role="button">` in the layer will not get ButtonText. Using real `<button>`/`<input>` elements is a functional requirement, not a style preference. Authors should make small targeted tweaks and must not use forced-color-adjust:none to reassert their palette.
    (forced-colors — MDN, 2026 — https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/@media/forced-colors; confidence high)
  - The disability community's consensus position rejects exactly the shape most people imagine for an 'accessibility layer': a floating widget with font-size/contrast/motion toggles, plus automatic AT detection. A portable layer must therefore be a semantic/content layer that respects OS and browser settings, never a visual-override widget, and must never sniff for assistive technology.
    (Overlay Fact Sheet, 2026 — https://overlayfactsheet.com/en/; confidence high)
  - Live regions only work if the empty container exists in the DOM first and content is injected later (with a small delay); creating a pre-populated live region, or toggling one from display:none, reliably fails. This constrains the layer's architecture: the announcement region must be mounted at init, not created when the stream starts.
    (Why are my live regions not working? — TetraLogical, 2024 — https://tetralogical.com/blog/2024/05/01/why-are-my-live-regions-not-working/; confidence high)
  - The spec-sanctioned technique for incremental/streamed content is aria-busy batching plus non-atomic polite regions — set aria-busy="true" on the region while tokens arrive, clear it when the response completes. aria-atomic must stay false, or every token re-reads the whole answer.
    (WAI-ARIA 1.2 aria-busy + MDN ARIA live regions + open-webui PR #29240, 2026 — https://www.w3.org/TR/wai-aria-1.2/#aria-busy; confidence medium)
  - A concrete, easily-made trap: putting role="log" directly on the transcript `<ul>` destroys the list semantics and orphans every message. The live region must be a WRAPPER around the list, not the list itself. Messages also need heading or list structure or there is no way to navigate between turns.
    (open-webui issue #29211: transcript is not navigable by screen reader, 2026 — https://github.com/open-webui/open-webui/issues/29211; confidence high)
  - The gap the project identified is empirically real and unclosed in shipping AI products. Major open-source AI chat UIs still have umbrella accessibility issues open for years, and NVDA's own tracker carries unresolved bugs against ChatGPT's Electron desktop app.
    (NVDA issue #18669 and open-webui issue #2790, 2026 — https://github.com/nvaccess/nvda/issues/18669; confidence high)
  - On desktop, Electron/Chromium gives you the platform accessibility tree for free (auto-enabled when AT is detected), but auxiliary COM interfaces that screen readers depend on can be missing in an Electron shell — so 'it works in the browser' does not imply 'it works in the desktop app'. Desktop shipping requires separate testing with the real screen reader.
    (Electron Accessibility docs (+ NVDA #18669 as counter-evidence), 2026 — https://www.electronjs.org/docs/latest/tutorial/accessibility; confidence high)
  - A single cross-platform accessibility layer for NATIVE mobile is not realistic at the API level: React Native's `accessibilityLiveRegion` is Android-only, so there is no shared live-region primitive for the streaming-announcement core. A portable layer should be web-first, with a thin per-platform adapter for announcements — the pattern AccessKit demonstrates for desktop/native.
    (React Native Accessibility docs + AccessKit, 2026 — https://reactnative.dev/docs/accessibility; confidence high)
  - For maximum adoption by large model operators, Apache-2.0 (or a permissive dual MIT-OR-Apache-2.0) beats MIT, and CC0 is the worst choice. Apache-2.0's explicit patent grant plus retaliation clause is what corporate legal teams look for; CC0 expressly withholds patent rights and the FSF advises against it for software; CNCF mandates Apache-2.0 for inbound and outbound code.
    (Apache License 2.0 §3; CC0 legalcode §4(a); FSF license list; CNCF Charter; Google third-party license policy, 2026 — https://www.apache.org/licenses/LICENSE-2.0; confidence high)
  - Microsoft Immersive Reader is the closest existing analogue to what Clara wants to be, and its architecture is the cautionary tale: an MIT-licensed client SDK that is only a thin wrapper around a proprietary, paid Azure service delivered in an iframe. The free-to-embed component must have NO mandatory server dependency or it will not be adopted as infrastructure.
    (Azure AI Immersive Reader overview + microsoft/immersive-reader-sdk + mozilla/readability, 2026 — https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-services/immersive-reader/overview; confidence high)
  - Existing headless a11y primitives are React-locked and therefore cannot be the base of a portable layer, but they are the right source of tested behaviour to port. React Aria in particular claims real screen-reader testing and 30+ language localisation, and is Apache-2.0 (patent grant) rather than MIT.
    (React Aria docs + adobe/react-spectrum + Radix Primitives + a11y-dialog, 2026 — https://react-aria.adobe.com/; confidence high)
  - There is already a W3C track for exactly the 'portable accessibility profile' Clara drafted — WAI-Adapt — and Clara should align vocabulary with it rather than invent a parallel schema, while being honest that WAI-Adapt is largely unimplemented. Only the Symbols module has reached Candidate Recommendation; Content, Help-and-Support and Tools are still Working Drafts.
    (W3C WAI-Adapt overview + Making Content Usable for People with Cognitive and Learning Disabilities (COGA), 2023 — https://www.w3.org/WAI/adapt/; confidence high)
DESIGN IMPLICATIONS:
  - Ship the layer as a LIGHT-DOM custom element, not a shadow-DOM component. Define it with `customElements.define()` for framework portability, but override the render root to `this` (Lit's `createRenderRoot() { return this; }`) so every ARIA IDREF, `<label for>`, aria-activedescendant and aria-controls relationship keeps working today in Safari as well as Chrome. Revisit only if/when Reference Target reaches WebKit.
  - Use ElementInternals for default semantics: `const i = this.attachInternals(); i.role = 'region'; i.ariaLabel = 'Plain-language version';` so host authors can override with plain attributes and nothing is lost if they delete them. Use `internals.states` + `:state()` for styling hooks rather than class names the host might collide with.
  - Solve the CSS problem with the cascade, not encapsulation. Wrap all library CSS in `@layer clara;` (host's unlayered styles then win automatically), give every default rule zero specificity via `:where(.clara-root) …`, expose the whole theme as CSS custom properties (`--clara-fg`, `--clara-bg`, `--clara-font-size`, `--clara-line-height`), and use `container-type: inline-size` + `@container` so the widget adapts to the pane it is dropped into rather than the viewport. Optionally offer an opt-in `all: revert-layer` reset on the root for hostile host stylesheets.
  - Distribute as (a) a zero-dependency ESM module with no build step and no CSS-in-JS, (b) a single IIFE bundle for `<script>` tags, and (c) a headless core with no DOM at all (the verifier + simplifier + profile logic) so React Native, Electron main-process, Swift/Kotlin and server integrations can call the same logic. Do NOT depend on React; port the *behaviour* from React Aria/Radix rather than the packages.
  - Build the streaming announcement as: one persistent, empty, `aria-live="polite" aria-atomic="false"` region mounted at init (never created on demand, never `display:none`), a wrapper element carrying role="log" AROUND the transcript `<ul>` (never on it), `aria-busy="true"` set on the response container for the duration of the stream and cleared on completion, and per-message headings (visually hidden if needed) so users can jump between turns.
  - Make the announcement policy a user setting persisted in the accessibility profile, with at least three modes: (1) silent while streaming, announce the complete response on finish — the default; (2) announce sentence-by-sentence as complete sentences land, never token-by-token; (3) off, user reads with virtual cursor. Never use aria-live="assertive" for model output, and never move focus into a streaming region.
  - Respect, never override, platform preferences. Read `prefers-reduced-motion`, `prefers-contrast`, `forced-colors` and `prefers-color-scheme` as the DEFAULTS for the layer's own settings, let the user override them explicitly inside the layer, and persist that choice. Because `prefers-reduced-transparency` is not Baseline, back it with an explicit setting rather than a media query. Never write CSS that undoes what the OS asked for.
  - Build every control from real `<button>`, `<input>`, `<select>` and `<dialog>` elements. In forced-colors mode the browser picks system colours from native semantics, so `div role="button"` gets no ButtonText. Add a small `@media (forced-colors: active)` block that restores borders where box-shadow was doing the work, and never use `forced-color-adjust: none`.
  - Use `inert` (Baseline April 2023) on the host page content when the layer opens a modal reading view, and `<dialog>.showModal()` which escapes ancestor inertness — rather than hand-rolled focus traps. Cross-check behaviour against a11y-dialog, which is 1.7KB, MIT, vanilla and already APG-conformant.
  - License as Apache-2.0, or dual `MIT OR Apache-2.0` (the AccessKit/Rust pattern) to cover the GPLv2-compatibility gap. Do not use CC0 for the code: it expressly grants no patent rights, the FSF advises against it for software, and Google's policy flags public-domain dedications as needing case-by-case legal analysis. Add a DCO (not a CLA) to lower the contribution barrier. CC0 is fine for the *profile schema document*, which is a specification rather than code.
  - Have no mandatory server. Immersive Reader is MIT-licensed but useless without a paid Azure resource, which caps its adoption as infrastructure. Clara's layer must run fully client-side by default (deterministic verification, live-region etiquette, pictogram board, preference plumbing) with the LLM simplification step pluggable — the host's own model, a local model, or Clara's proxy.
  - Align the accessibility profile JSON with WAI-Adapt vocabulary (adapt-symbol / BCI concept ids for the pictogram board, the Content module's purpose/destination terms where they exist) and say plainly in the spec that WAI-Adapt is not yet implemented by user agents — so the profile is a superset that degrades to the standard rather than a competitor.
  - On desktop, do not assume Electron gives you accessibility for free. Ship a test matrix run against NVDA+Chrome, JAWS+Chrome/Firefox and VoiceOver+Safari, and separately against the Electron build — the ChatGPT desktop MathML/ISimpleDOM failure shows web-verified markup can still break in an Electron shell. Contribute test plans to W3C ARIA-AT rather than inventing a private harness.
  - For native mobile, scope honestly: publish the headless core plus thin adapters (Android `accessibilityLiveRegion`/`announceForAccessibility`, iOS UIAccessibility announcement posting) rather than promising one cross-platform component. AccessKit is the right dependency if a native desktop/mobile widget is ever built, but note its own caveat that rich text and hypertext are unsupported.
  - Reuse mozilla/readability (Apache-2.0, dependency-free) for the browser-extension content-extraction path instead of writing a new extractor — but pair it with DOMPurify, since Readability explicitly does not sanitize and Clara's extension runs on arbitrary pages.
PITFALLS:
  - A floating 'accessibility' button that opens a panel of font-size, contrast, dyslexia-font and stop-animation toggles. This is the overlay pattern that 1,031 accessibility practitioners — including JAWS and NVDA contributors and accessibility staff at Microsoft, Google and Apple — publicly reject as redundant with what users already have in their OS and browser.
  - Detecting whether a screen reader is running in order to auto-enable features. There is no reliable web API for it, the sniffing techniques used in practice expose disability status without consent (a GDPR/CCPA problem per the Overlay Fact Sheet), and it produces a worse experience than simply shipping correct semantics for everyone.
  - Wrapping the layer in a shadow root 'for encapsulation'. It buys style isolation you can now get from cascade layers and `:where()`, and it costs you every cross-boundary ARIA relationship, with no fix available in Safari.
  - `aria-atomic="true"` on a streaming response region. Every token then re-announces the entire answer from the beginning. Equally bad: `aria-live="assertive"` on model output, which interrupts the user mid-sentence on every chunk.
  - `role="log"` applied directly to the transcript `<ul>`. It overrides the implicit list role and orphans every `listitem`, removing the only structural way to move between turns — a real, currently-open bug in a widely deployed AI chat UI.
  - Auto-reading responses aloud by default. Screen-reader users already have speech; layering a second TTS voice on top creates two voices talking at once. Read-aloud must be opt-in and must pause/duck when a screen reader is active — which you cannot detect, so make it an explicit user setting.
  - Injecting ARIA automatically into the host application's own DOM. This is precisely the automated-repair behaviour that overlays are condemned for; it produces wrong labels, floods the page with headings, and can break the host's existing correct semantics.
  - `forced-color-adjust: none` to preserve brand colours in Windows Contrast Themes. It defeats the user's chosen palette and disables the text backplates that keep text legible over images.
  - Overriding `prefers-reduced-motion` or forcing the layer's own colour scheme regardless of `prefers-color-scheme` / `prefers-contrast`. Respecting the platform is not a nice-to-have: a component that ignores it is strictly worse than no component.
  - Shipping only a CDN `<script>` tag as the integration path. Large operators run strict Content-Security-Policy and will not add a third-party origin; provide an npm ESM package and a vendorable single-file bundle they can self-host.
  - Requiring a hosted API for the core value. An MIT client SDK in front of a paid proprietary service (the Immersive Reader model) will not be adopted as free infrastructure by model operators who are themselves the service providers.
  - CC0 or 'public domain' for the code, chosen because it feels maximally free. It grants no patent licence by its own terms, the FSF advises against it for software, and it triggers case-by-case legal review at exactly the large companies you want adoption from — the opposite of the intended effect.
  - Focusing or scrolling the response container when a stream starts. It yanks the virtual cursor away from wherever the user was reading; the live region is what should speak, not a focus move.
  - Testing only with automated scanners (axe/pa11y). None of the failures above — flooded announcements, orphaned listitems, missing ISimpleDOM in Electron, atomic re-reads — are detectable by static rule engines.
OPEN QUESTIONS:
  - I found no peer-reviewed or empirical study on whether screen-reader users prefer incremental (sentence-by-sentence) versus complete-response announcement of streamed LLM output. The 'announce complete response' pattern is a reasonable default derived from live-region mechanics, not from measured user preference. This should be user-tested before being hard-coded, and the finding published — it would be a genuine contribution.
  - I could not find a screen-reader support matrix showing whether `aria-busy="true"` actually suppresses live-region announcements during the busy period in JAWS, NVDA and VoiceOver. The technique is spec-sanctioned (WAI-ARIA 1.2) but its real-world effect is unverified; treat the batching design as an assumption to test, not a fact.
  - Whether ARIA live regions inside a shadow root are announced reliably across screen readers is not well documented. I found only one anecdotal report of it working (dodona-edu/papyros #1141, Sept 2026) and no systematic testing. If the layer ever does use shadow DOM, this needs empirical verification.
  - WebKit has registered no standards position on Reference Target as of July 2026. Whether Safari will implement it — and therefore whether shadow-DOM-based accessibility components ever become viable — is unknown and materially affects the 5-year architecture.
  - W3C ARIA-AT has no published test plan for chat transcript, streaming response, or live-log patterns that I could locate. This is a concrete gap Clara could fill by contributing a test plan, which would also give the layer an independent conformance story.
  - I found no evidence of any existing cross-platform (web + iOS + Android + desktop) reading-simplification accessibility layer, open source or commercial. Either the demand is genuinely web-only, or nobody has attempted it — I cannot distinguish these from the sources available.
  - I have no direct data on what large model operators' legal teams actually require. The Apache-2.0 recommendation is inferred from CNCF's mandate, Google's third-party policy, and the licence choices of comparable Adobe/Mozilla/AccessKit projects — not from any operator's published intake policy.
  - Apple's developer documentation is JavaScript-rendered and could not be retrieved reliably, so I could not verify iOS-specific details (UIAccessibility announcement priority attributes introduced in iOS 17, `prefersCrossFadeTransitions`) from a primary source. The React Native docs corroborate the general shape but not the current API surface.
  - Android's `View.announceForAccessibility` deprecation status could not be confirmed from the primary reference page. If it is deprecated in recent API levels, the Android adapter's announcement strategy needs revisiting.
  - No evidence was found either way on the performance cost of Chromium's accessibility tree being enabled in Electron, which matters if the layer is expected to run inside a desktop AI client that already renders large token streams.
MISSED ANGLE (per verifier): How the layer survives contact with a host AI chat UI it does not control — and specifically how it FEEDS the live region during streaming, not just where the region is mounted. Three concrete gaps. (1) Streaming throttling: token-by-token generation is close to the worst possible input for aria-live. Announcing each mutation floods the AT speech queue and makes output unusable; the layer needs a coalescing strategy (debounce to sentence or paragraph boundaries, aria-busy during the burst then release, or announce a summary plus an on-demand 'read the full response' affordance). The pass established that the region must exist at init but said nothing about the rate problem, which is the actual hard part for this product. (2) Anchoring into a virtualized, framework-owned DOM: most AI chat interfaces are React apps with windowed message lists (react-window/virtuoso), so message nodes are recycled and destroyed on scroll — MutationObserver anchors and any attributes the layer injects get clobbered on the next render, and the layer needs a re-application loop or a stable keying strategy. (3) Injection constraints: Content Security Policy and Trusted Types on the host page, and — for the browser-extension distribution path already in this repo — content-script isolated worlds, MV3 service-worker lifetime, and whether custom elements and ElementInternals defined from an isolated world behave the same as page-context ones. None of the eight claims touches the host-integration surface, yet it determines whether any of the light-DOM/ElementInternals architecture actually survives in production.

### Why accessibility components do/don't get adopted
FINDINGS:
  - The canonical success story for a free accessibility component — axe-core — was not adopted because it was free. It was open-sourced in 2015 by Deque, a ~250-person, ~$50M-revenue commercial accessibility firm founded in 1999, and its adoption by Google (Chrome DevTools / Lighthouse) and Microsoft (Accessibility Insights) came with that institutional backing. 'Free MIT code from a small project' is not the same offer.
    (Axe-core Digital Accessibility Hit 100M Downloads (Deque) + Lighthouse Accessibility Scoring (Chrome for Developers), 2020 (post); Lighthouse docs current — https://www.deque.com/blog/axe-core-hit-100-million-downloads/; confidence high)
  - Free tooling saturation has demonstrably NOT improved outcomes. Despite axe-core being downloaded billions of times and Lighthouse shipping inside every Chrome install, 95.9% of the top million home pages had detectable WCAG 2 failures in 2026 — worse than 2025's 94.8%, the first regression after six years of improvement, at 56.1 errors per page.
    (The WebAIM Million — annual accessibility analysis of the top 1,000,000 home pages, 2026 — https://webaim.org/projects/million/; confidence high)
  - The single closest historical precedent to Clara's 'portable JSON accessibility profile' — ISO/IEC 24751 AccessForAll — has been a published ISO standard since 2008 and still has essentially no implementations. A portable preferences profile is a solved-on-paper, unsolved-in-practice problem, and the failure mode is complexity, not lack of standardisation.
    (Feasibility of Extending ISO 24751 — Fluid Project Wiki; ISO/IEC 24751-2:2008, 2008–2023 — https://wiki.fluidproject.org/display/fluid/Feasibility+of+Extending+ISO+24751; confidence high)
  - The W3C route for exactly this problem is also stalled. WAI-Adapt (formerly Personalization Semantics) — which would let content declare simplification/symbol/help semantics — has been in development since March 2019 and only ONE of its four modules (Symbols) has reached Candidate Recommendation; Content, Help-and-Support and Tools remain Working Drafts, with no browser implementations. The spec itself notes examples are 'illustrative only' pending implementations.
    (WAI-Adapt Overview + WAI-Adapt Explainer (W3C), 2019–2023 — https://www.w3.org/WAI/adapt/; confidence high)
  - The one accessibility-preference mechanism that DID achieve universal vendor implementation was a machine-readable signal with near-zero implementation cost: prefers-reduced-motion. It reached all modern browsers by 2020 and ~49–50% of sites use it. But uptake collapses for each additional preference axis: prefers-color-scheme is 12% and prefers-contrast is under 1%. Small, single-purpose, OS-backed signals spread; rich preference vocabularies do not.
    (Web Almanac 2024 — Accessibility chapter (HTTP Archive), 2024 — https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2024/accessibility; confidence high)
  - Procurement/VPAT is a much weaker forcing function than it is usually credited with. After ~25 years of Section 508 and mandatory Accessibility Conformance Reports in US federal procurement, only 23% of top-visited federal public websites and 20% of intranet pages fully conform, with no improvement in FY2024. Getting a component 'into VPATs' will not create adoption.
    (Governmentwide Section 508 Assessment FY2024 — Findings Summary (GSA / Section508.gov), 2024 — https://www.section508.gov/manage/section-508-assessment/2024/findings/summary/; confidence high)
  - Regulation — not altruism, not free code — is the strongest live lever, and it now reaches AI systems directly. The EU AI Act Article 16(l) obliges providers of high-risk AI systems to meet the accessibility requirements of Directives (EU) 2016/2102 and (EU) 2019/882, and the European Accessibility Act has been enforceable since 28 June 2025 with penalties described as 'effective, proportionate and dissuasive'.
    (EU AI Act Article 16 (Obligations of providers of high-risk AI systems), 2024–2025 — https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/16/; confidence high)
  - Shipping a component that makes an accessibility CLAIM is a liability transfer, and the market has already punished it. The FTC's final order (approved April 2025) requires accessiBe to pay $1M and bars it for 20 years from claiming its automated product can make any site WCAG-compliant absent evidence; separately, a paying customer filed a class action against the vendor in June 2024 after being sued itself.
    (FTC Approves Final Order Requiring accessiBe to pay $1 Million (Federal Trade Commission), 2025 — https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/04/ftc-approves-final-order-requiring-accessibe-pay-1-million; confidence high)
  - Disability-led organisations and in-house accessibility specialists are the real gatekeepers inside big vendors, and they have already organised against the category Clara superficially resembles. The Overlay Fact Sheet carries 1,031 signatories including internal accessibility staff at Google, Microsoft, Apple, Shopify and eBay — precisely the people who would review a proposed third-party accessibility layer.
    (Overlay Fact Sheet, 2021–present (living document) — https://overlayfactsheet.com/en/; confidence high)
  - That gatekeeping is enforceable and has been enforced: the National Federation of the Blind revoked accessiBe's convention sponsorship on 22 June 2021 and passed a condemnation resolution on 10 July 2021. A single credible objection from a disability-led org can end a vendor relationship regardless of the code's quality or price.
    (National Convention Sponsorship Statement Regarding accessiBe (National Federation of the Blind), 2021 — https://nfb.org/about-us/press-room/national-convention-sponsorship-statement-regarding-accessibe; confidence high)
  - Anything shaped like an overlay is dead on arrival with the very reviewers who must approve it. Clara's browser extension (a script that post-processes someone else's page and surfaces simplified text) is structurally the overlay pattern, and overlays are both marginal (≈2% of sites) and rising, which is why the community treats each new one as a threat rather than a contribution.
    (Web Almanac 2024 — Accessibility chapter (overlay usage) + Overlay Fact Sheet, 2024 — https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2024/accessibility; confidence high)
  - Even the most successful free, government-funded, mandate-adjacent accessibility component library gets forked rather than adopted. After the GOV.UK Design System, the Home Office, DfE, DWP, MoJ and NHS all built their own systems. GDS's own 2018 discovery named five reasons, none of which are fixable by making the code better or cheaper.
    (How the GOV.UK Design System can work alongside other government design resources (GDS Design Notes), 2019 — https://designnotes.blog.gov.uk/2019/02/14/how-the-gov-uk-design-system-can-work-alongside-other-government-design-resources/; confidence high)
  - The reliably winning distribution pattern is to become the invisible substrate under something developers already reach for, not to be a product organisations install. axe-core spread by being inside Lighthouse and Accessibility Insights; Radix UI's accessible primitives spread by being the layer beneath shadcn/ui, which developers adopt for styling and ergonomics, inheriting WAI-ARIA behaviour they never chose.
    (Lighthouse Accessibility Scoring (Chrome) + What is the difference between Radix and shadcn/ui (WorkOS), 2024–2026 — https://developer.chrome.com/docs/lighthouse/accessibility/scoring; confidence medium)
  - A protocol CAN go from one small origin to universal vendor adoption in about a year — MCP did — but only under conditions Clara does not currently meet: it removed an expensive N×M integration cost the adopters were already paying, it was launched by a model lab with distribution, and it was handed to a neutral foundation once it mattered (Linux Foundation / Agentic AI Foundation, 9 Dec 2025, co-founded with OpenAI and Block). Specs spread when they delete work for the adopter, not when they add an obligation.
    (Donating the Model Context Protocol and establishing the Agentic AI Foundation (Anthropic), 2025 — https://www.anthropic.com/news/donating-the-model-context-protocol-and-establishing-of-the-agentic-ai-foundation; confidence high)
  - Maintenance capacity is a hard adoption gate independent of code quality. A single-maintainer repo predictably fails large-vendor third-party review: enterprises name lack of technical support (36%) and security (36%) among their top OSS adoption barriers, 60% of maintainers are unpaid, 61% of unpaid maintainers work alone, and 44% of those who quit cite burnout. 97% of codebases contain OSS and 86% contain vulnerable components — which is exactly why the review exists.
    (2024 Tidelift State of the Open Source Maintainer Report + Black Duck OSSRA 2025, 2024–2025 — https://assets-eu-01.kc-usercontent.com/ef593040-b591-0198-9506-ed88b30bc023/d325a56f-05be-4379-bfd1-ee4776fcad41/2024-tidelift-state-of-the-open-source-maintainer-report-.pdf; confidence high)
  - License choice is a real gate but a low bar: it must be boring, not merely permissive. Google requires all non-Google code to live in third_party and pass license review; unrecognised or customised license text triggers legal escalation and some licenses are simply unusable. MIT clears this trivially; note that axe-core itself is MPL-2.0, so permissiveness was clearly not the deciding factor in its adoption.
    (Third-Party Licenses / License Review (Google Open Source) + dequelabs/axe-core, current — https://opensource.google/documentation/reference/thirdparty/licenses; confidence high)
  - Automated remediation and checking has a hard ceiling that must be stated in the component's own marketing, or it recreates the overlay failure. axe-core — the most mature engine in existence — reports catching on average 57% of WCAG issues automatically, and its central design rule is 'zero false positives'.
    (dequelabs/axe-core README, current — https://github.com/dequelabs/axe-core; confidence high)
DESIGN IMPLICATIONS:
  - Ship a SPEC + a conformance TEST SUITE as the primary artefact, and the MIT library as a reference implementation — because the GOV.UK evidence says large organisations reimplement rather than depend, and the ISO 24751 evidence says a spec alone dies. The pairing that actually travels is: small normative spec + executable test suite + reference impl + published test results. Optimise for someone at OpenAI reimplementing Clara's verifier in their own stack and passing your tests, not for them adding your npm package.
  - Scope the machine-readable surface to ONE signal, not a preference vocabulary. prefers-reduced-motion (~50% site usage) versus prefers-contrast (<1%) is the clearest natural experiment available: each extra dimension costs an order of magnitude of adoption. If the accessibility profile spec has more than about three fields in v1, it is following ISO 24751 and WAI-Adapt into non-adoption. Consider whether the profile can be expressed as, or map onto, existing OS/CSS user preferences rather than inventing a new registry.
  - Never let Clara emit or enable a conformance CLAIM. The FTC order and the Tribeca class action mean any adopter's legal team will read 'accessibility layer' as 'liability'. Invert it: Clara's deterministic verifier should output falsifiable EVIDENCE about a specific rewrite (numbers/dates/negations/obligations preserved: yes/no, with diffs), never a WCAG or ADA status. Publish the ceiling loudly, the way axe-core publishes '57% of WCAG issues' and 'zero false positives'. A verifier that says 'this rewrite dropped a date' is defensible; a layer that says 'now accessible' is accessiBe.
  - Structurally separate the embeddable component from the browser extension in positioning and in the repo. The extension is an overlay by construction and will be read as one by the exact in-house accessibility staff (Google/Microsoft/Apple/Shopify signatories) who gate adoption. Frame the extension as a user-agent-side tool the *user* installs for themselves (which the fact sheet does not object to), and the embeddable layer as first-party functionality the product ships — with settings that defer to OS/platform preferences rather than adding a floating widget.
  - Lead with the AI Act / EAA compliance obligation, not with goodwill. Art. 16(l) plus EAA enforcement from 28 June 2025 gives a named person inside a model operator (legal/compliance, not the a11y team) a problem Clara reduces. But do not overclaim: Section 508's 23% conformance shows mandates alone don't produce adoption — the offer has to be 'this removes work you already owe', the MCP condition.
  - Design for third-party review, not for GitHub stars: MIT (not MPL, not custom), no runtime network calls at all in the embeddable path, deterministic and offline-capable, an SBOM, a published OpenSSF Scorecard, pinned minimal dependencies, and a documented security-contact and release-signing process. Also publish an Accessibility Conformance Report for the component's OWN UI — a component that fails review on its own accessibility is unusable regardless of what it does.
  - Solve the bus-factor problem before pitching, or expect to fail review on it. 60% of maintainers unpaid, 61% of unpaid maintainers solo. Realistic options in ascending order of credibility: a documented governance file and second maintainer; adoption by a disability-led org or an existing foundation; or contributing the verifier upstream into something already inside enterprise review (an axe-core rule, a Radix/React-Aria pattern, an eslint-plugin-jsx-a11y rule, an MCP server spec). Upstream contribution is cheaper and higher-probability than a standalone adoption pitch.
  - Route validation through disability-led organisations BEFORE approaching vendors, and be willing to be told the design is wrong. The NFB/accessiBe episode shows a single credible objection ends a vendor relationship; the inverse — a documented co-design and endorsement record — is one of the few assets a tiny project can hold that a large vendor cannot manufacture internally. Note the cognitive/plain-language space is where community disagreement with industry practice is sharpest and where Clara's claims are least covered by testable standards (COGA's 'Making Content Usable' is non-normative; WCAG 3 is not expected as a Recommendation before ~2028–2029).
  - Measure success in shipped user-facing behaviour, not downloads. WebAIM's 2026 regression despite billions of axe-core downloads is the disproof of download-count as a proxy. A defensible v1 goal is one real product shipping one Clara-verified surface with a public write-up — not 'adopted by model operators'.
PITFALLS:
  - Announcing 'a free accessibility layer for AI' and expecting inbound interest. The category currently reads as 'overlay vendor' to every reviewer who matters; the default prior on a new third-party accessibility script is hostile, and it is a well-earned prior.
  - Any language of the form 'makes your AI accessible', 'WCAG-compliant', 'ADA-safe', or 'one line of code'. This is the precise phrasing the FTC ordered accessiBe to stop using and the phrasing the NFB condemned. It converts a good tool into a legal exhibit for the adopter.
  - Detecting whether the user has assistive technology enabled, or storing an accessibility profile server-side. The Overlay Fact Sheet's fourth objection is that this exposes disability status without informed consent — a special-category-data problem under GDPR. A profile that travels must be user-held, user-transmitted, and non-inferring, or it is worse than nothing.
  - Building the accessibility profile spec with an ambitious taxonomy of needs and preferences. That is exactly what ISO 24751 / AccessForAll did, and the Fluid Project's own assessment names the complexity and the absence of implementations as the reason. Ambition in the vocabulary is the failure mechanism, not a virtue.
  - Treating a VPAT/ACR mention or a Section 508 hook as the adoption path. Federal procurement has enforced accessibility for 25 years and produces 23% conformance; procurement forces documentation, not implementation, and third-party components are rarely named in it.
  - Assuming pictogram/AAC boards, speech, and simplification are uncontested goods. They are contested: overlays are rejected partly BECAUSE they duplicate capabilities users already have in their own AT and OS. An embeddable layer that re-implements text scaling, contrast or TTS that the user's screen reader and OS already provide will be read as redundant at best and interfering at worst. The defensible unique contribution is the deterministic verification of meaning preservation, which nothing else does — not the presentation controls.
  - Pitching model operators directly as the first move. Vendor product teams cannot say yes to third-party code without legal, security and accessibility review; a cold pitch enters at the point of maximum scrutiny and minimum context. Standards-body participation (W3C APA/COGA), a disability-led-org partnership, or upstream contribution to a library already inside their review are all lower-friction entry points — but all are slower than the project's likely expectations.
  - Believing the MCP precedent transfers. MCP was launched by a frontier lab with distribution, solved a cost problem the adopters were already paying for, and was governance-neutralised only after it had won. A small project cannot replicate the first two conditions, and donating an unadopted spec to a foundation confers nothing.
OPEN QUESTIONS:
  - I could not find a published post-mortem or independent evaluation of why GPII/Morphic failed to reach platform adoption despite roughly a decade of EU and US public funding. The Trace R&D Center and Raising the Floor pages describe the vision and the continuing Morphic product but not the adoption outcome. This matters: GPII is the closest large, well-funded attempt at exactly Clara's portable-profile idea, and its failure mode is currently inferred (from the absence of any platform implementing it) rather than documented. Worth asking the Raising the Floor / Trace principals directly.
  - No primary source confirming that OpenAI, Anthropic or Google publish (or fail to publish) an accessibility conformance report / VPAT for their consumer chat products. Practitioner discussion on the ATHEN accessibility mailing list (Oct 2024, https://mailman22.u.washington.edu/pipermail/athen-list/2024-October/020020.html) reports difficulty locating any ACR for ChatGPT and that the composer text area is not usable with standard screen readers, and Google publishes VPATs for many Cloud/Workspace products but I found none for Gemini's chat UI. The project's founding observation ('no AI chat product ships a vision-accessibility layer') is plausible and consistent with what I found, but I could not verify it to a primary-source standard and it should be re-checked product-by-product before being used in outreach.
  - No evidence found on bundle-size thresholds as an actual adoption gate for accessibility components specifically. This is widely asserted in practitioner writing but I found no study or vendor policy quantifying it. Treat 'keep it small' as a reasonable engineering default, not an evidenced requirement.
  - I found no research measuring whether Easy Read / plain-language rewriting improves comprehension for LLM outputs specifically (as opposed to static documents). The Immersive Reader evidence base is adjacent — a 2017 RTI study reported a 10-point comprehension gain, but a more recent study found the difference not statistically significant with a small sample and 30-day intervention. This is a separate dimension's problem, but it bears on adoption: a vendor's research team will ask for efficacy evidence before shipping a comprehension feature, and the honest current answer is 'mixed and thin'.
  - Unknown whether any accessibility component has ever been adopted by a major vendor on the strength of standards-body participation alone (as opposed to commercial backing or acquisition). ARIA-AT is the nearest live test — it convened Apple, Google, Microsoft, NVDA and Vispero with Meta funding — but I could not find published data on how much AT-vendor behaviour actually changed as a result, or over what timescale. If someone can obtain that, it would materially change the estimated value of the W3C route.
MISSED ANGLE (per verifier): The pass has no evidence from disabled users themselves — it explains why developers fail to adopt accessibility components, never why disabled people fail to adopt accessibility products, which is the adoption that actually decides the outcome. Missing entirely: the WebAIM Screen Reader User Survey and equivalent longitudinal user data on what AT users actually want; the switching-cost and conflict problem, where screen reader, magnifier and switch-access users already run deeply configured stacks (JAWS/NVDA/VoiceOver) and a new layer that duplicates or fights that stack gets uninstalled no matter how good it is; and the participatory-design/'nothing about us without us' norm, which is the specific mechanism that produced the NFB verdict in claim 9 and is the strongest single predictor of whether a disability-facing product is endorsed or condemned. Closely coupled to this and also absent: the distribution question specific to AI chat. Frontier chat UIs are closed products with no third-party injection point except a browser extension — which is structurally the same delivery pattern as the overlays the FTC and NFB punished, and this repo already ships one (commit 5444d4a, 'Clara as an overlay on any page'). The pass never asks how that pattern gets distinguished from an overlay in users' eyes, nor whether the realistic route is instead upstream: the AI vendors' own accessibility teams, platform accessibility APIs, and OS-level AT integration.
