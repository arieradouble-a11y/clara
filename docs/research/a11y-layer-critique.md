# Completeness and Honesty Critique

## 1. Underserved groups and barriers

**Speech disability is named only as a reason not to ship voice, never as a design target.** All three proposals correctly cite the >51% WER finding to argue that voice must not be the motor-accessibility feature. None of them then does anything for the people who are stuck with voice anyway, or for whom voice is the only viable channel. Clara ships browser speech input *today*, which means it currently fails dysarthric and stuttering users silently. The evidence supports a concrete module nobody proposed: show the ASR hypothesis rather than requiring re-dictation, let the model propose disambiguations, accept or correct in one or two switch hits, and carry a personal vocabulary plus correction history in the profile (personalisation is the only intervention with strong evidence: 31% → 4.6% median WER). That module is cheap, evidence-backed, and absent from all three.

**Deafblind users do not exist in any proposal.** Braille is treated throughout as a variant of screen-reader output — "also brailleable," "the braille invariant." A deafblind user of a 40-cell display with no speech at all has a different failure model: there is no ephemeral channel, everything must be persistent and re-readable, announcement is meaningless, and cell-count budgets matter more than word counts. Proposal 1's rule that everything announced must also exist as on-page text half-covers this by accident. Nobody designs for it.

**Photosensitive epilepsy is absent from all three.** WCAG 2.3.1 is Level A. AI products increasingly render model-generated SVG, HTML, canvas and video inline. A model can emit a flashing artifact, and none of these designs has a single sentence about flash thresholds on generated renderable output. That is the only Level A criterion in the whole space with a documented seizure risk and it appears nowhere.

**Fatigue and energy-limiting conditions** (ME/CFS, long COVID, MS) are named in the research as real-but-unquantified and then dropped. Proposal 2's composer module preserves drafts within a session; nobody designs for resumption across days, for a persistent thread state summary, or for the "I have twenty minutes of capacity today" case. COGA Objective 6 (processes must not rely on memory) is cited in the research and implemented by nobody.

**Aphasia gets the symbol channel pointed the wrong way.** The design-probe evidence says the higher-value use of symbols for people with aphasia is *verifying the answer*, not composing the question. Clara already has a pictogram board and it faces the input side. No proposal turns it around.

**Voice-control users under mutation.** Proposal 1 has Label in Name; that is not the problem. The problem is that Dragon and Voice Access number visible targets, and a transcript that grows and recycles DOM makes "click Copy" ambiguous across twelve Copy buttons whose numbering recomputes on every mutation. That is a distinct, unaddressed failure that the delivery-mode control would incidentally help — nobody says so.

**Multiply disabled users and preference conflict.** The research flags explicitly that Easy Read and blind-user verbosity needs pull in opposite directions, and that nobody has studied blind-plus-cognitive users. All three proposals ship a flat preference object with no conflict-resolution semantics. What does `reading: easy_read` plus `announce: complete` plus a braille display actually produce? Undefined in all three.

**Older adults with multi-domain age-related impairment** — the single largest population touched by any of this — are not mentioned once across three documents.

**Non-English users.** Clara ships five locales and a Russian validation package. Proposal 2 proposes a COGA checker whose central rule is "words outside a common-1500 list," and the research states plainly that COGA ships no authoritative list and has no cross-lingual equivalent. Proposal 2 flags this in a parenthesis and builds the module anyway. Reading-level targets, Easy Read validation norms, disordered-speech ASR, and hedging framing are all English/US-derived across the entire evidence base, and no proposal treats localisation as anything but a translation problem.

## 2. Contradictions with the evidence, and assertions the research did not establish

**The `role="log"` wrapper is a self-contradiction that would ship the bug it exists to prevent.** Proposal 1 states as a non-negotiable that "the visible transcript must carry zero live semantics," and then, four modules later, specifies "wrap the transcript with `role="log"` on a container that is a parent of the `<ul>`." `role="log"` carries implicit `aria-live="polite"`. A live-region ancestor of mutating content is a live region over mutating content; putting it on the wrapper instead of the list preserves list semantics (which is what open-webui #29211 was about) and does nothing whatsoever about the announcement flood. Proposal 3's CAT-STREAM asserts only "no `role="log"` on the streaming message," which permits the same bug. Only proposal 2 is internally consistent here. This needs resolving before anything is written down as normative, because it is exactly the kind of error that passes code review and destroys the experience.

**"Exactly one live region for the whole page" is unenforceable and, as a global rule, wrong.** Every real host application already has a live region — a toast system, a form-error announcer, a router announcement. Proposal 1 makes single-region a non-negotiable *and* proposes a runtime assertion that fails if a second is registered. That component cannot be adopted by any real product. w3c/aria#1689 establishes that simultaneous multi-region behaviour is *undefined*, not that a page may contain only one. The defensible rule is narrower: the layer owns exactly one, mounted once, and must not create a second of its own — plus guidance on sequencing against the host's. As written, it is a design that fails on first contact.

**SC 2.2.2 is the most legally aggressive claim in all three documents and the least supported.** Proposal 1 flags it as contested and argues the mechanism instead — correct. Proposals 2 and 3 assert it flatly, and proposal 3 builds an entire conformance category on it and pipes the result into an ACR row generator. Two problems. First, the auto-updating clause concerns content that starts automatically, lasts more than five seconds, and is *presented in parallel with other content*; a single streaming response is arguably neither parallel nor non-essential. Second, and worse: a "Stop generating" button is, on a plain reading, an author-provided mechanism to stop the update. The proposals' answer — that aborting the model is not the same as freezing the render — is a usability argument, and a good one, dressed as a conformance finding. Asserting a 2.2.2 failure against a named vendor in a generated ACR row is precisely the "claim inflation" these documents accuse the overlay industry of. Argue the AT mechanism; do not put 2.2.2 in the ACR generator.

**The Gemini VPAT wedge is misread and strategically self-harming.** All three, and proposal 3 most heavily, treat "4.1.3 marked as failing across ~20 surfaces" as a scandalous admission. It is a normal artifact of an honest conformance report; VPATs are full of Partially Supports and Does Not Support rows, and the research itself notes that essentially every vision-relevant criterion in that document is Partially Supports. Opening a conversation with "your own document says you fail" (a) reads as naive to the accessibility professional who wrote it, and (b) punishes the only vendor that publishes freely while OpenAI's sits behind a portal and Anthropic's reportedly needs an NDA. The incentive this creates is for Google to stop publishing. Whoever fought internally for that PDF will read the outreach exactly that way.

**Proposal 2's population ranking rests on a category error.** CDC's 12.2% mobility figure is self-reported serious difficulty walking or climbing stairs. It is not the population that uses switch scanning, eye gaze or BCI, which is smaller by orders of magnitude. Using it to rank motor above vision is unsound and a reviewer will catch it in one line. The composer fixes are correct and cheap on their own merits — argue them on severity-of-failure (a lost 20-minute draft is total task failure) rather than on a prevalence number that does not mean what it is being used to mean.

**Enter-to-send off by default, for everyone, is the bundled-mode error inverted.** Proposal 2 makes it a non-negotiable. It is a global change to the most-used interaction in the product, justified by a small population, with zero evidence that it is the right default and considerable evidence that defaults changed for everyone are the ones that get reverted. Ship it as a setting with an evidence-backed heuristic (confirm on Enter when the draft exceeds N characters or M minutes of composition), not as a default.

**The redundancy test is being applied with a blind-user prior to features whose users are not blind.** Two instances. (a) Text-to-speech is condemned across all three as duplicating what screen-reader users already have configured — true, and irrelevant to dyslexic readers, low-literacy readers, people with aphasia, and low-vision users who do not run a screen reader, for whom Read Aloud is a genuine first-party feature that Word, Edge and Pocket all ship. (b) In-product text size is condemned as duplicating browser zoom — and the proposals' own best precedent, Wikipedia's 2024 Accessibility for Reading work, is an in-product text-size and line-height control on a *web* product where browser zoom exists, praised in the research as the accepted first-party pattern. Browser zoom scales the whole layout; a reader who wants larger message text at the same UI density has no route. The redundancy test is a good gate and it is being over-applied because the evidence base behind it (Overlay Fact Sheet, NFB/ACB/AFB, Kubesch, Kohler) is almost entirely blindness-organisation-derived. The research's own verifier note says this. All three proposals absorbed the conclusion without the caveat.

**The `a11y` field on the OpenAI chat-completions body has an engineering error.** Proposal 3 presents it as riding "an existing wire format every operator already parses." Strict-schema servers reject unknown top-level body params; Azure OpenAI does; validating proxies do. There are `extra_body` and `metadata` conventions but they are not uniform and `metadata` has its own constraints. "They already parse it" is exactly wrong: they parse it and 400 on it.

**The 4.9x figure is being transferred across mechanisms.** It measures surfacing *disagreement across multiple model outputs*. Clara does a *deterministic diff against a source*. Proposal 2 flags the transfer honestly; proposal 1 leans on it. It is one unreplicated n=15 study and it is being used as the empirical anchor for the project's central differentiator.

**Per-claim negative hedging is derived from one study, in a different modality, against contradicting evidence in the same corpus.** MacLeod 2017 is single-shot image captions. The same research base records that participants found hedges ambiguous and confusing, that four of fifteen preferred no indicator at all, and that one participant judged a description reliable *because* it lacked hedges — on the image with the most errors. Applied per-claim across a 400-word answer this becomes a hedging flood with a documented risk that absence of hedging reads as accuracy. Proposals 1 and 2 adopt it as a design rule.

## 3. The verifier's scope limit, which no proposal states

This is the most important honesty problem in the set. Clara's deterministic verifier checks that numbers, dates, negations and obligations survived a transformation. That requires a source text. In an ordinary chat turn — user asks a question, model answers from parameters — **there is no source, and the verifier has nothing to check against.** It verifies a *rewrite* against its *input*. It cannot verify a *generation*.

All three proposals present the verifier as the reason a simplification layer is safe to embed in an AI chat product, and proposal 3 calls it "the only genuinely novel asset" while pitching it into conversational interfaces generally. The accessibility-proxy framing ("simplify and fact-check any LLM's answer") elides the distinction: fact-checking the rewrite against the model's own output tells you the rewrite is faithful to a possibly-hallucinated original. That is genuinely valuable — it is exactly ATAG B.1.2.1 — and it is not what a reader will understand from "deterministically verifies." A blind user who cannot independently check a claim, reading "3 facts checked," will reasonably conclude the facts were checked against the world.

Two further problems compound it. First, "verified" is an affirmative signal, and the research explicitly warns that confidence indicators may be read as endorsement by cognitively disabled users — a known security-indicator failure pattern. None of the three designs against it; the correct design is to surface only *losses* and never an affirmative pass. Second, preserving every number and date does not preserve meaning: a rewrite can retain all four categories and invert the sense of a clause. A checker that passes will be read as "safe." That is false reassurance built on a deterministic mechanism, which is worse than false reassurance built on a probabilistic one, because it sounds like proof.

The honest headline claim is narrow and still strong: *this rewrite dropped a date / weakened an obligation / introduced a figure not present in its input*. Every proposal needs that sentence in place of "verifies."

## 4. Is "write code, give it to operators free" the right lever?

No, and the evidence in the research is decisive rather than suggestive. Immersive Reader is MIT, feature-rich, backed by the largest accessibility organisation in the industry, and sits at ~38k downloads/month. The GOV.UK Design System is forked by departments under the same mandate. ISO/IEC 24751 has been a standard since 2008 with no implementations. WAI-Adapt is one attribute, frozen since January 2023. GPII's servers are offline. DNT died and GPC lived because of law, not spec quality. Free code has never been the binding constraint. The constraints are the operator's incentive, the operator's structural inability to accept third-party runtime code in a frontier product, the bus factor, and the absence of an agreed measurement.

Proposal 3 says most of this and then still puts the buyer at Phase 4. The ordering should be inverted:

**The lever is the buyer, and the buyer is also the first adopter.** Public universities, city service portals, school districts, health systems and federal agencies deploying AI assistants carry hard, dated obligations — Section 508 plus FAR today with no scope debate, ADA Title II at 26 April 2027/2028 reaching contractor-provided tools, EAA Art 24(1) making Annex I mandatory in EU public procurement. Critically, these organisations do not merely *pressure* vendors: many of them **build their own chat UIs on top of APIs**. They can compile in a component, they have a legal obligation, and they have budget. None of the three proposals says this plainly. They all treat the buyer as a lever to move OpenAI and miss that the buyer is the customer. The highest-leverage single artifact in the entire plan is a model accessibility clause and vendor questionnaire for AI assistants, written to be pasted into an RFP, with the test suite behind it.

**Second lever: be the evidence, not the code.** The largest gap in the entire research base is that nobody has measured any of this with disabled users of AI chat — no announcement-strategy comparison, no magnifier study of an LLM interface, no braille-under-streaming data, no test of the verifier with the populations it targets, no first-hand dated audit of the four major products. A small project can produce a pre-registered, published study for the cost of participant payments. It cannot produce adoption. A citable study gets into every vendor's internal design doc without anyone merging anything. All three propose the announcement study and all three rank it last.

**Third: upstream in the scaffold.** Proposal 3's Vercel AI SDK insight is the best distribution idea in the three documents and is undersold at Phase 2. Extend it: `assistant-ui`, shadcn-style copyable blocks, Gradio and Streamlit and Chainlit (where an enormous number of model demos and internal tools live, and where accessibility is worst).

**Fourth, and unnoticed by all three: Claude Code is an open injection point with a documented user waiting.** The claim that frontier products have no third-party integration surface is false for exactly one of them. Claude Code has hooks, a plugin system, an accessibility docs page, a responsive issue tracker, and a blind Lead Accessibility Architect who already built a TTS layer on those hooks himself (#70425). Shipping a Claude Code hook package is the fastest available route to a real disabled user using Clara's work, and no proposal mentions it.

## 5. Steelman: what a disability-led organisation would say

*"You are a non-disabled developer with no disabled co-maintainer. You have already shipped an overlay extension, a text-to-speech feature and a pictogram board — three things our members either already own or did not ask for. You have now written a two-hundred-page research base about us and consulted none of us. The word co-design appears in your plan at step seven.*

*Every artifact you propose positions you as arbiter: your spec, your test suite, your scoreboard, your conformance-report generator. You have appointed yourself auditor of an industry on the strength of a literature review, with no lived experience and no organisational accountability. When you are wrong — and you will be, because you have never watched one of us use this — we have no mechanism to hold you to account, because you are a GitHub repository with one maintainer.*

*Your core act is rewriting our text and telling us you checked it. We have heard 'we simplified it for you' before; Easy Read done badly is the most patronising artifact in our world. Your own evidence says the strongest RCT found no comprehension benefit and that these models are systematically paternalistic about people with intellectual disabilities. You propose to automate that at the scale of every AI product on earth, validated against a corpus you assembled, tested by no-one with an intellectual disability.*

*And here is the part you have not engaged with at all. This project assumes the correct response to inaccessible AI is to make AI more usable. Some of us think the correct response is that these systems should not be deployed into benefits offices, clinics and legal advice until they work — and that every accessibility layer built on top of an unreliable system extends its licence to operate. You are doing free compliance work for companies with more money than every disability organisation on earth combined. The reason they have not built this is not that they lack a component. It is that it is not a priority. Your free labour changes that arithmetic in exactly the wrong direction."*

That last paragraph is the strongest objection available and none of the three proposals engages it. The available answers are real — Clara's verifier is a check on the system rather than a coat of paint on it; the layer is honest about what it cannot do; the alternative to a bad layer is not no AI but AI with nothing — but they have to be argued, in the README, not assumed.

## 6. The single most likely way this fails

**All three plans defer contact with a disabled user until after two to six months of unvalidated construction, and the project dies in that window — of burnout, or of a premise that has been overtaken by vendor fixes, or of a beautifully engineered default that turns out to be the wrong one.**

Look at the orderings. Proposal 1 puts the user study at step 9 of 10. Proposal 2 at step 10 of 10. Proposal 3 at step 9 of 9. Every one of them front-loads a spec, a test suite, a scoreboard, a TypeScript port, five pattern implementations and a conformance kit, and treats disabled users as a validation gate at the end. That ordering is the failure mechanism, and it is doubly indefensible here because the research says three things simultaneously: the largest gap in the field is evidence, satisfaction is an actively misleading metric, and participatory design is the strongest single predictor of whether a disability-facing product is endorsed or condemned.

The concrete end state: 15,000 lines of Apache-2.0 code implementing announcement and delivery defaults derived from first principles; a founding premise ("nobody ships this") that decayed three times during the research window alone; no adopters, because 61% of unpaid maintainers work alone and enterprise review fails on bus factor before anyone reads the code; and no data, because the study was step nine. The most likely artifact of this project in eighteen months is a very good, very dead repository.

The second-order variant, and it is nearly as likely: the project succeeds at precisely one thing — a test suite plus a handful of merged upstream PRs — which is a genuinely good outcome, and is experienced as failure because the stated goal was "model operators embed it."

The mitigation is an ordering change, not a scope change: recruit through a disability-led organisation in week one, run a formative study on paper prototypes and on the four existing products before writing the spec, and let the first module be whatever those sessions say it is.

## 7. The high-impact module nobody proposed

**A document view of the conversation.**

The strongest comparative finding in the entire research base is that a Q&A interface produces measurably worse comprehension for blind users than a document interface — 40% fewer main topics, double the error rate — while being subjectively preferred. All three proposals take the chat form as fixed and optimise inside it. The intervention that directly addresses the measured harm is to offer the same content in the form that measured better.

Concretely: a generated, static, navigable document rendering of a long answer or a whole thread. Real headings, a table of contents, sections, no live regions, no mutation, re-readable, printable, exportable, brailleable. Plus a persistent plain-language state summary of what has been asked and decided.

Why it dominates everything else on the list:

- It is grounded in a **direct comparative measurement of interface type**, not inferred from magnifier studies of static Office documents or from mechanism arguments about virtual buffers.
- It passes the redundancy test absolutely. No operating system can restructure a conversation into a document.
- It serves nearly every constituency at once: blind and braille users (navigable, re-readable, no mutation); low-vision and magnifier users (a static document reflows and magnifies without moving under the lens, which is the entire streaming problem solved by removing the stream); cognitive and intellectual disability (COGA Objective 6 requires that processes not rely on memory — a running state summary is literally a COGA requirement the research names and nobody builds); motor users (one artifact instead of scrolling and panning a hundred-turn thread); fatigue (come back to it tomorrow).
- It is the one content transformation with no paternalism problem. It does not change a single word. It adds structure. It is unassailable by the Deaf verbatim-text objection, by the Easy Read RCT, and by the intellectual-disability self-advocacy critique — all of which are aimed at *rewriting*.
- It answers the "am I getting the full answer / I did not know what to ask" problem the research repeatedly surfaces, and it makes the completion-summary and table-of-contents-preamble open questions moot by giving the user the structure directly.
- It is cheap. Markdown to headed HTML with an anchor list, plus a thread digest.
- It is Clara's identity — a content layer — rather than the AT plumbing that dominates all three proposals and that vendors are demonstrably fixing themselves.

Runners-up that also appear nowhere across three documents: a **curated human-escape-hatch registry** (COGA's voice module requires reachable human backup; CDT found every model tested invented disability organisations to refer users to; Clara's domain is benefits, legal and medical — a small, locale-aware, machine-readable list of real help routes, always present, never generated, is a tiny module against a named documented harm); **prompt-side composition support** as a first-class module (telegraphic expansion with visible diff and one-action reject, multi-turn consolidation with restate-and-confirm against the 39% degradation finding, saved templates) supported independently by the AAC, gulf-of-envisioning, DHH prompt-difficulty and intellectual-disability query-formulation literatures, with Clara's existing pictogram board as the front end; and the **original-versus-simplified diff as the primary trust surface** rather than a toggle, which is the one presentation of verification that surfaces losses without ever asserting a pass.
